# Added Sugar

## The Sweet Problem Hiding in Plain Sight

Sugar is everywhere. From fizzy drinks and biscuits to breakfast cereals, sauces and flavoured coffees, added sugar has become a familiar part of many modern diets.

Most conversations about sugar focus on calories, weight and dental health. But there is another interesting part of the story: how the body processes different types of sugar, particularly fructose, and what happens when we consume too much.

Understanding this takes us beyond the sweet taste and into the science of metabolism.

## What's Proven? The Impact of Too Much Added Sugar

There is strong evidence linking high intakes of added and free sugars, particularly from sugar-sweetened drinks, with several health concerns.

These include:

- **Weight gain:** Sugary drinks provide energy but tend to be less filling than solid foods, making it easier to consume more calories overall.
- **Dental health:** Frequent exposure to sugar increases the risk of tooth decay.
- **Metabolic health:** High intakes, particularly from sugary drinks, are associated with a greater risk of type 2 diabetes and metabolic disease.
- **Liver health:** Excessive sugar intake can contribute to fat accumulation in the liver, particularly when it adds to an overall excess of dietary energy.

The evidence is particularly consistent for sugar-sweetened beverages. This doesn't mean that an occasional sweet food is harmful, but that regularly consuming large amounts of added sugar can have consequences beyond simply increasing calorie intake.

## What's Emerging? The Liver and Fructose

One of the most interesting areas of nutritional research concerns fructose.

Table sugar, or sucrose, contains approximately equal parts glucose and fructose. Both are sugars, but the body processes them differently.

Glucose can be used by cells throughout the body, while much of the fructose we consume is processed by the liver.

Researchers are particularly interested in what happens when large amounts of fructose arrive regularly.

The liver can convert some fructose into fat through a process called *de novo lipogenesis*. This is a normal metabolic pathway, but under conditions of excess energy intake, it can contribute to increased fat production and accumulation in the liver.

### When Sugar Becomes Liver Fat

Excess liver fat is associated with metabolic dysfunction and can contribute to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

Studies have found that high intakes of fructose, particularly from sugar-sweetened beverages, can increase liver fat under certain conditions.

However, the picture is more nuanced than fructose simply turning into fat. The overall amount of energy consumed, body weight, physical activity and wider dietary pattern all influence liver fat accumulation.

Researchers are continuing to investigate whether fructose has additional metabolic effects beyond its contribution to excess energy intake.

## What's Marketing? The Fructose and Alcohol Comparison

You may have heard fructose described as "alcohol without the buzz".

This comparison comes from research highlighting some similarities in how the liver processes excessive amounts of fructose and alcohol.

Both can contribute to fat production in the liver and influence pathways associated with oxidative stress and inflammation.

However, the comparison can be misleading.

Fructose and alcohol are not interchangeable substances. They have different metabolic effects, and the evidence does not support treating ordinary dietary fructose as equivalent to drinking alcohol.

The comparison is useful for understanding why researchers are interested in liver metabolism, but it shouldn't be taken literally.

## Not All Sugar Sources Are the Same

This is an important distinction.

The sugar in a whole apple is not nutritionally equivalent to the sugar in a fizzy drink, even though both contain fructose.

Whole fruit provides fibre, water and a range of beneficial plant compounds. Its structure also means it takes longer to eat and digest.

Fruit juice, sweetened drinks and foods containing added sugars deliver sugars in a different form, often making it easier to consume larger amounts quickly.

This is why public health guidance distinguishes between sugars naturally present in whole fruit and milk, and *free sugars* – those added to foods and drinks, as well as sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices and concentrates.

Whole fruit should not be confused with foods and drinks high in added or free sugars.

## The Takeaway

Sugar isn't simply a question of calories. The type, source and amount all influence how it interacts with the body's metabolism.

Human research strongly supports reducing excessive added and free sugar consumption, particularly from sugary drinks. Meanwhile, research into fructose metabolism is helping scientists understand more about how the liver responds to different dietary patterns.

The important distinction isn't that all sugar is harmful. It's that regularly consuming large amounts of added sugar can place unnecessary demands on metabolic health.

Understanding what's happening inside the body helps put the marketing, headlines and nutritional claims into perspective.

## The Science Deep Dive: How the Body Processes Sugar

### Glucose and Fructose

Glucose is a major energy source for the body. It enters the bloodstream after digestion and can be used by tissues throughout the body.

Fructose is handled differently. Much of it is taken up by the liver, where it can be used for energy, converted into glucose or stored as glycogen. Some can also be converted into fat through de novo lipogenesis.

### The Liver's Role

The liver is central to maintaining energy balance. It processes nutrients, stores energy and releases substances when the body needs them.

When fructose intake is high, particularly alongside an excess of calories, the liver may increase fat production. This can contribute to the accumulation of fat within liver cells.

### Why the Amount and Source Matter

The metabolic response to fructose depends on several factors, including how much is consumed, whether it is part of a whole food or a sugary drink, and the person's overall energy balance.

This is why findings from studies using large amounts of pure fructose cannot automatically be applied to ordinary fruit consumption.

The science isn't simply that fructose is bad. It is that the body's response depends on the context in which it is consumed.

This article is for general educational purposes only. It is not medical advice and should not be used to diagnose, treat or prevent any health condition. If you have concerns about blood sugar, liver health or your diet, speak with a qualified healthcare professional.

# Ayurveda

*Exploring the wisdom of Ayurveda, from traditional herbs and breathing practices to everyday rituals that could support modern wellbeing.*

Ayurveda is a traditional system of health and wellbeing that originated in India thousands of years ago. Its name is often translated as “the science of life”, and its approach considers the relationship between the body, mind, environment and daily habits.

At its heart is the idea of balance: eating in a way that suits you, moving regularly, making time to rest and paying attention to how you feel. Many of these principles have parallels in modern health research, even though the traditional explanations behind them are not always supported by clinical science.

I believe there is value in exploring traditional approaches alongside modern evidence. The aim isn't to accept every traditional health claim at face value, but to remain curious about practices that may help us feel healthier and more connected to our everyday routines.

## Understanding the Doshas

One of Ayurveda's central concepts is the idea of three doshas: Vata, Pitta and Kapha. These are described as different combinations of natural elements and qualities that influence how the body and mind function.

In Ayurvedic philosophy, everyone has a unique constitution, or *Prakriti*, made up of all three doshas in varying proportions. The doshas are not fixed medical categories, but a traditional framework for thinking about individual differences and balance.

**Vata – Air and Space**

Vata is associated with movement, communication and creativity. It is traditionally linked to the nervous system, breathing and circulation.

When Vata is considered out of balance, Ayurveda associates it with restlessness, worry, dryness and irregular routines.

**Pitta – Fire and Water**

Pitta is associated with digestion, metabolism and transformation. It represents heat, intensity and the ability to process experiences.

When Pitta is considered out of balance, traditional descriptions include irritability, frustration, overheating and digestive discomfort.

**Kapha – Earth and Water**

Kapha represents structure, stability and nourishment. It is associated with strength, endurance and steadiness.

When Kapha is considered out of balance, Ayurveda describes sluggishness, heaviness, congestion and low motivation.

**How could this be useful today?**

Although there is no established scientific test for identifying someone's dosha, the framework can be an interesting prompt for self-reflection. For example, noticing whether you feel better with more regular routines, gentler movement or a little more time to unwind can help you understand your own preferences.

The practical value is in becoming more aware of what supports your wellbeing, rather than treating a dosha assessment as a medical diagnosis.

## Herbs: Traditional Uses and Modern Research

Plants have been used in Ayurvedic medicine for centuries. Many contain biologically active compounds, and modern research is investigating how some of these may influence inflammation, digestion, stress and other aspects of health.

However, evidence varies considerably between herbs. The benefits of using a spice in food are also not necessarily the same as taking a concentrated supplement.

Here are some familiar examples.

### Ashwagandha

Ashwagandha is traditionally used as a restorative herb and is often described today as an adaptogen — a term used for substances thought to help the body respond to stress.

Small clinical trials suggest certain ashwagandha extracts may help reduce perceived stress and modestly improve sleep. However, studies have used different preparations and doses, and longer-term safety is not well understood.

**What could be worth exploring:** Ashwagandha is an interesting example of a traditional herb now being studied for its potential effects on stress and sleep. It is not suitable for everyone: it can affect thyroid function, interact with medication and has been linked to rare cases of liver injury. It should be avoided during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

### Turmeric

Turmeric is a familiar ingredient in South Asian cooking and has a long history of use in Ayurveda. Its best-known active compounds are curcuminoids, including curcumin, which have been studied for their potential anti-inflammatory effects.

Human research is particularly interested in turmeric and curcumin for joint health. Some studies suggest they may help reduce knee osteoarthritis pain and stiffness, although the quality of evidence and results vary.

**What could be worth exploring:** Including turmeric in meals is an easy way to enjoy its flavour and nutritional compounds. Concentrated curcumin supplements are a different proposition: formulations vary, and some enhanced-absorption products have been linked to liver injury.

### Ginger

Ginger is traditionally used to support digestion and is one of the better-studied culinary herbs. Research suggests it may help with some forms of nausea, including pregnancy-related nausea, although the evidence depends on the situation and preparation.

**What could be worth exploring:** Fresh ginger in food, tea or warm water is a simple way to include it in everyday life. It is also a useful reminder that traditional ingredients can have practical uses without needing to be treated as medicines.

### Cinnamon

Cinnamon has traditionally been used for digestive complaints and general wellbeing. It contains plant compounds that have attracted interest for their possible effects on blood sugar regulation.

Some studies suggest cinnamon supplements may influence blood glucose in people with type 2 diabetes, but findings are inconsistent and it is not a replacement for established treatment. Claims that cinnamon treats headaches, colitis or fungal infections are not well established.

**What could be worth exploring:** Use cinnamon as a flavourful addition to porridge, fruit or warm drinks. Large amounts of cassia cinnamon can contain significant coumarin, a compound that may affect the liver, so concentrated daily use is best approached cautiously.

### Garlic

Garlic has a long history in traditional food and herbal practices. It contains sulphur-containing compounds that are being studied for their potential cardiovascular effects.

Some research suggests garlic supplements may modestly reduce cholesterol or blood pressure in certain people, although this does not mean garlic prevents heart disease. Garlic also contains fermentable fibres, including fructans, which can be used by gut bacteria.

**What could be worth exploring:** Garlic is a useful everyday ingredient that adds flavour and contributes to dietary variety. Concentrated supplements can increase bleeding risk and may interact with medication, so they are not automatically a better option than food.

### Mint

Mint has traditionally been used for digestive discomfort and is widely enjoyed as a herbal tea. Peppermint oil has been studied more extensively than mint tea, particularly for symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), where some enteric-coated preparations may help relieve abdominal pain and bloating.

**What could be worth exploring:** Fresh mint in meals or peppermint tea can be a pleasant addition to a daily routine. Peppermint can worsen reflux in some people, so it may not suit everyone.

## Breathing Practices: Pranayama

Breathwork is an important part of traditional yoga, where breathing practices are known as *pranayama*. The word is often interpreted as the regulation or expansion of life force through the breath.

Different techniques use different breathing patterns, from slow and steady breathing to alternate nostril breathing. Modern research suggests that some of these practices can influence the autonomic nervous system, heart rate variability and feelings of stress.

### Alternate Nostril Breathing

Known as *Nadi Shodhana*, this practice involves gently breathing in and out through one nostril at a time, alternating sides.

In traditional yoga, it is said to balance the body's energy channels. Modern research has explored its potential effects on relaxation, attention and cardiovascular responses, although the evidence is still developing.

**How to explore it:** Sit comfortably, gently close one nostril and breathe in through the other. Switch sides and breathe out, then alternate again. Keep the breath natural and unforced.

### Deep Diaphragmatic Breathing

This technique encourages breathing with the diaphragm, allowing the abdomen to expand gently as you inhale. It can help slow the breathing rate and reduce unnecessary tension in the chest and shoulders.

Slow, comfortable breathing is associated with changes in heart rate and nervous system activity, and research suggests it may help reduce stress and support relaxation.

**How to explore it:** Place one hand on your chest and the other on your abdomen. Breathe gently through your nose, allowing the abdomen to rise more than the chest. Avoid taking exaggerated breaths.

### The Physiological Sigh

The physiological sigh is not a traditional pranayama technique, but it is an interesting modern breathing practice that has attracted scientific attention.

It involves two consecutive inhales — the second a shorter top-up breath — followed by a long, slow exhale. Research suggests that regular practice may help reduce physiological arousal and improve mood.

**How to explore it:** Take a gentle inhale through the nose, add a shorter second inhale, then slowly breathe out through the mouth. Repeat for a few comfortable cycles.

These techniques are generally gentle, but breathing should never feel forced. Intensive hyperventilation or prolonged breath-holding practices are different and carry additional risks.

## Everyday Rituals: Dry Brushing and Tongue Scraping

Ayurveda includes a range of daily self-care practices, sometimes described as *Dinacharya*. The idea is that small, consistent rituals can help create structure and encourage awareness of the body.

Some practices have straightforward modern explanations, while others are more traditional and have limited clinical evidence.

### Dry Brushing

Dry brushing involves gently brushing the skin with a natural-bristle brush, usually before showering. It is traditionally used as part of Ayurvedic self-care.

**What could it offer?** Brushing can exfoliate the outer layer of dead skin cells, leaving skin feeling smoother. The sensation may also be enjoyable as part of a personal care routine. Claims that it removes toxins or meaningfully improves circulation have not been established.

**How to explore it:** If you enjoy it, use gentle strokes and avoid broken, irritated or sensitive skin. It is an optional skincare ritual, not a detox treatment.

### Tongue Scraping

Tongue scraping is a traditional oral hygiene practice intended to remove the coating that can build up on the surface of the tongue.

**What could it offer?** It can physically remove some surface debris and leave the mouth feeling cleaner. However, evidence that it prevents persistent bad breath or improves digestion is limited. It does not replace brushing with fluoride toothpaste or cleaning between the teeth.

**How to explore it:** Use a clean tongue scraper gently, moving from the back of the tongue towards the front. Avoid pressing hard or scraping to the point of irritation.

## Stretching and Yoga

Movement is another important part of Ayurvedic daily life. Yoga brings together physical postures, breathing and meditation, with a long tradition of supporting both physical and mental wellbeing.

Modern research has investigated yoga for flexibility, balance, strength, stress and emotional health. There is evidence that regular yoga practice can improve balance and flexibility, help with some types of persistent pain and support stress management. The results depend on the style of yoga, how often it is practised and the individual.

**What good looks like:** Movement that feels sustainable, enjoyable and appropriate for your body. Yoga does not need to be intense or particularly advanced to be beneficial.

**How to explore it:**

- Try a short sequence of gentle stretches to ease into or wind down from the day.
- Explore different styles, from restorative and Hatha yoga to more dynamic practices.
- Combine movement with comfortable breathing and moments of stillness.
- Adapt postures to your mobility and experience, rather than forcing a particular shape.

The benefits of yoga do not depend on balancing a dosha. Its physical and psychological effects can be explored in their own right.

## Bringing Traditional Wisdom into Modern Life

Ayurveda offers a broad way of thinking about wellbeing, encouraging us to pay attention to daily rhythms, food, movement, rest and our individual needs. Some of its practices — including yoga, mindful breathing and the use of familiar culinary herbs — have modern research behind aspects of their potential benefits. Others remain rooted mainly in traditional understanding.

The most useful approach may be to stay curious while keeping a clear distinction between tradition and evidence. Enjoy the rituals that help you feel good, explore the science behind herbs and practices that interest you, and be cautious of claims that promise to detoxify, cure or rebalance the body.

Traditional approaches and modern science do not always use the same language, but both can encourage a more thoughtful relationship with everyday health.

# Barefoot Walking

*From the wellbeing benefits of green spaces to walking barefoot on the earth, discover how spending time in nature may support our physical and mental health.*

## Why Our Connection With Nature Matters

Most of us spend much of our day indoors, surrounded by screens, artificial light and built environments. Yet humans evolved in close connection with the natural world. Spending time outdoors offers a change of pace, engaging our senses and creating opportunities for movement, relaxation and recovery.

Research increasingly suggests that access to green spaces can support mental wellbeing. One interesting 2018 study in *JAMA Network Open* looked at vacant lots in Philadelphia. Some were transformed by clearing rubbish, preparing the soil and planting grass and a small number of trees. People living nearby reported reductions in feelings of depression and worthlessness compared with those near untreated lots. Simply removing rubbish did not produce the same results.

This suggests that improving our natural surroundings may offer benefits beyond making neighbourhoods more attractive.

## Spending Time in Green Spaces

Whether walking through woodland, visiting a local park or sitting beneath a tree, being around nature can help us step away from everyday demands.

Studies have linked time in natural environments with improvements in mood, perceived stress and attention. Some have also explored changes in physical markers such as heart rate and blood pressure. The strength of the evidence varies, but the overall picture is encouraging.

Trees may contribute in less obvious ways. They release natural airborne compounds called *phytoncides*, which help plants communicate and defend themselves. Small human studies have investigated whether exposure to these compounds influences immune markers, including natural killer (NK) cell activity. This is an interesting area, although meaningful long-term health benefits have not yet been established.

Natural environments also offer a richer sensory experience: birdsong, moving leaves, changing light, fresh air and the textures and scents of plants. These experiences may help us slow down, shift our attention and feel more present.

## Barefoot Walking: Feeling the Earth Beneath You

Our feet contain thousands of sensory receptors that detect pressure, texture, temperature and movement. These signals help the nervous system understand where the body is in space, contributing to balance and coordination.

Walking barefoot on a safe natural surface, such as grass or sand, provides different sensory feedback from walking in shoes. It encourages us to notice how our feet meet the ground and how our weight moves through each step.

There is also growing interest in whether barefoot and minimalist-footwear training can improve foot strength and function. Some research suggests it may help strengthen the muscles of the feet when introduced gradually.

Our feet work as part of a wider movement system, alongside the calves, hamstrings, glutes and muscles supporting the spine. Developing foot awareness and strength may therefore contribute to how we move as a whole. It isn't a guaranteed way to improve posture or prevent injury, but it can be an enjoyable way to introduce variety into everyday movement.

## The Hidden Chemistry of Trees

The natural world is full of subtle interactions. Alongside the compounds released by trees, researchers have explored tiny airborne particles called negative air ions.

These can form when water droplets break apart, a process associated with waterfalls, breaking waves and fast-moving water. Known as the Lenard effect, it helps explain why these environments can produce high concentrations of negative ions. Wind can then help disperse them through the surrounding air.

Research has explored possible effects on mood and fatigue. A 2013 review found associations between high concentrations of negative ions and lower depression ratings in some studies, particularly those involving seasonal or chronic depression. Findings were less consistent for anxiety, general mood, relaxation and sleep.

It's an intriguing area, but there isn't enough evidence to say that the air around waterfalls or the coast reliably improves energy or health.

## Trees, Sunlight and the Invisible Spectrum

Sunlight contains more than the visible colours we see. It also includes ultraviolet and infrared radiation, which interact with the natural environment in different ways.

Leaves absorb much of the visible red and blue light used in photosynthesis. Near-infrared light interacts differently: the internal structure of healthy leaves scatters and reflects a substantial proportion, while some passes through the leaf and canopy. This is why vegetation appears particularly bright in near-infrared satellite images.

Near-infrared light is also being studied in *photobiomodulation* — research exploring how specific wavelengths of red and near-infrared light may influence cellular activity. It raises an interesting question about the light environment around trees, but there is currently no good evidence that light reflected by leaves produces specific therapeutic effects in people. Findings from targeted light devices cannot simply be applied to natural exposure outdoors.

Trees also provide shade, reduce direct solar exposure and influence local temperature and humidity. They transform the light and conditions around us in ways that are both complex and valuable.

## Touching Nature: Grounding and Our Electrical Connection With the Earth

Touch is one of the most direct ways we experience the natural world. Whether walking barefoot on grass, resting a hand against a tree or sitting on the ground, physical contact can make us feel more connected to our surroundings. But there is also an interesting scientific question: what happens electrically when our bodies come into contact with the earth?

The human body conducts electricity, while the earth acts as a vast electrical reservoir. When bare skin touches a conductive surface, such as moist soil or grass, an electrical pathway can form. Electrons can move between the body and the ground, allowing electrical charge to equalise. Rubber-soled shoes and some synthetic materials can reduce this connection because they act as insulators.

This is the principle behind *grounding*, sometimes called *earthing*. Researchers have proposed that electrons from the earth could influence processes associated with oxidative stress and inflammation, potentially affecting recovery, sleep and pain.

It's an intriguing idea, but it's important to distinguish the electrical principle from the proposed health effects. Electrical contact with the earth is physically plausible; whether it produces meaningful changes in human health remains uncertain. Research so far is limited, with larger, well-designed studies needed.

There is also value in the experience itself. Feeling grass beneath our feet, noticing the texture of bark or simply sitting outdoors can encourage us to slow down and engage with our surroundings. We don't need to prove every proposed mechanism to appreciate the simple pleasure of physical contact with nature.

## Bringing More Nature Into Everyday Life

Reconnecting with nature doesn't need to involve a remote woodland or a long walk. Small experiences can help us feel more connected to our surroundings:

- **Find green spaces:** Spend time in a local park, garden or tree-lined street.
- **Engage your senses:** Notice natural sounds, textures, scents and changes in light.
- **Explore barefoot walking:** If safe and comfortable, try walking briefly on grass or sand.
- **Pause outdoors:** Take a few minutes to sit quietly, breathe and observe what's happening around you.

If walking barefoot, choose a clean, safe surface and be mindful of sharp objects, hot ground and uneven terrain. People with reduced sensation or circulation in their feet should take particular care.

Enjoy daylight without deliberately seeking intense sun exposure. Shade, suitable clothing and sunscreen help protect against excessive ultraviolet radiation. Never look directly at the sun.

## The Bigger Picture

The science of reconnecting with nature is still developing, but research into green spaces, movement and mental wellbeing gives us plenty of reasons to spend more time outdoors.

Some ideas, such as the benefits of access to green spaces, have encouraging support. Others, including the proposed health effects of grounding, negative air ions and tree-reflected infrared light, remain more exploratory.

We don't need to understand every mechanism to appreciate the value of nature. A walk beneath trees, the feel of grass beneath our feet or a few quiet moments outdoors can offer a welcome opportunity to move, pause and reconnect with the world around us.

# Creatine

*3 min read*

Creatine is best known for its role in strength training and athletic performance, but its importance in the body goes beyond muscle. It is a naturally occurring compound involved in cellular energy production, helping tissues meet their energy demands.

While research into creatine and physical performance is extensive, scientists are increasingly exploring its potential role in brain function, healthy ageing and neurological health. Some findings are well established, while others are still emerging.

## What Does the Science Clearly Show?

The strongest evidence for creatine relates to physical performance, particularly during short, intense bursts of activity.

Creatine is stored predominantly in skeletal muscle, where it helps support rapid energy production. Research has consistently demonstrated several effects:

- **Strength and power:** Creatine supports performance during high-intensity activities such as weightlifting, sprinting and repeated explosive movements.
- **Muscle development:** Research shows that creatine, alongside resistance training, can increase gains in strength and lean body mass compared with training alone.
- **Exercise capacity:** It can help maintain performance across repeated bouts of intense exercise, allowing muscles to sustain effort.
- **Age-related muscle function:** Studies in older adults suggest creatine combined with resistance exercise can improve strength and lean mass, potentially supporting mobility and physical function.

These are among the most thoroughly researched effects of creatine, supported by a substantial body of clinical evidence.

## What Is Exciting About the Emerging Research?

The brain also contains creatine and has significant energy demands. This has prompted researchers to investigate whether creatine's role in cellular energy metabolism could have wider implications for cognitive and neurological health.

### Cognitive Function and Memory

Some studies suggest creatine may influence aspects of memory and cognitive performance. Findings have been particularly interesting in people with lower dietary creatine intake, such as vegetarians, and in situations involving sleep deprivation or mental fatigue.

However, results are not consistent across all studies. It is not yet established that creatine improves everyday concentration, focus or memory in healthy adults generally.

### Brain Health and Ageing

Researchers are investigating whether creatine's involvement in cellular energy production could help maintain normal brain function as we age.

Because brain cells rely heavily on energy to communicate and carry out their functions, maintaining effective energy metabolism is an important area of study. There is interest in whether creatine could have implications for age-related cognitive decline and neurological conditions.

However, this research is still developing. There is currently insufficient evidence to conclude that creatine prevents or treats dementia, Parkinson's disease or other neurological disorders.

### Mood and Mental Wellbeing

Creatine has also attracted interest in research exploring depression and mental health. Some small clinical studies have investigated its potential role alongside conventional treatment, with early findings suggesting possible benefits.

However, this is a developing area, and the evidence is limited. Creatine is not an established treatment for depression or other mental health conditions.

## The Science Deep Dive: Creatine, ATP and Cellular Energy

To understand why creatine is attracting interest across different areas of health, it helps to look at its role in energy production.

Almost every process in the body relies on a molecule called adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Often described as the cell's energy currency, ATP provides the energy needed for muscle contraction, nerve signalling, cellular repair and many other essential functions.

When a cell uses energy, ATP loses one of its phosphate groups and becomes adenosine diphosphate (ADP). The cell must then regenerate ATP to continue functioning.

This is where creatine becomes important.

Inside cells, creatine can be converted into phosphocreatine, which acts as a readily available energy reserve. Phosphocreatine donates its phosphate group to ADP, helping rapidly regenerate ATP when energy demand increases.

This process is particularly important in tissues with high or fluctuating energy requirements, such as skeletal muscle and the brain.

It helps explain why creatine has such a well-established role in high-intensity physical performance, while also providing a biological reason for researchers to investigate its potential effects on cognition and brain health.

However, understanding how a biological mechanism works is not the same as demonstrating a clinical benefit. The potential wider applications of creatine remain an active area of research.

## Important Disclaimer

**This article is for general educational purposes only. It is not medical advice and should not be used to diagnose, treat or prevent any health condition.**

Although creatine has been extensively studied, research findings do not mean that supplementation is necessary or suitable for everyone. Evidence for potential cognitive, neurological and mental health benefits remains limited.

Anyone considering a supplement, particularly those with an existing medical condition or taking medication, should discuss this with a qualified healthcare professional. Never stop, reduce or change prescribed medication based on information in this article.

## The Bigger Picture

Creatine offers an interesting example of how a compound involved in a fundamental biological process can have effects across different tissues.

Its role in muscle energy and physical performance is well established. Its potential contribution to brain function and healthy ageing is more exploratory, but provides some fascinating questions for future research.

As scientists learn more about cellular energy metabolism, creatine may continue to offer valuable insights into the relationship between energy, physical performance and wider human health.

# Cultured Foods

Cultured foods are foods and drinks that have been transformed through fermentation by beneficial bacteria and yeasts. This age-old process not only helps preserve food but also creates new flavours, textures and nutritional properties.

Fermented foods are a wonderful way to introduce a diverse range of microorganisms into our diet and support a healthy, balanced gut microbiome.

## Potential health benefits

Cultured foods can offer a range of potential health benefits, including:

- **Supporting gut health:** Introducing beneficial microorganisms that may help maintain a healthy balance within our gut microbiome.
- **Supporting digestion:** Fermentation helps break down certain sugars and compounds, which can make some foods easier to digest.
- **Supporting immunity:** Our gut microbiome plays an important role in the normal functioning of our immune system.
- **Producing beneficial compounds:** Fermentation creates organic acids, enzymes, vitamins and other bioactive compounds that contribute to the nutritional properties of food.
- **Supporting overall wellbeing:** Emerging research suggests that the gut microbiome may influence inflammation, metabolic health and even communication between the gut and brain.

Different fermented foods contain different microorganisms, so including a variety in your diet is a great way to support microbial diversity.

## Examples of cultured foods

There are lots of delicious ways to incorporate cultured foods into everyday life. Some of my favourites include:

**Milk Kefir**

A tangy, yoghurt-like fermented milk drink made using kefir grains. Rich in diverse live cultures and a great way to introduce fermented dairy into your diet.

**Kombucha**

A lightly sparkling fermented tea made using a culture of bacteria and yeast. It contains fermentation-derived compounds and tea polyphenols.

**Water Kefir**

A naturally fermented, dairy-free and caffeine-free alternative to kombucha. Made using water kefir grains, it produces a refreshing, lightly sparkling drink.

**Probiotic Yogurts**

Traditional yoghurt provides beneficial live cultures, protein and calcium. You can also make specialised probiotic yoghurts using selected bacterial strains to introduce different microbes into your diet.

**Cultured Vegetables**

Vegetables such as carrots, cucumbers and cabbage can be fermented through a process called lacto-fermentation. This creates naturally tangy, crunchy foods containing beneficial lactic acid bacteria.

**Kimchi**

A traditional Korean fermented vegetable dish, usually made with cabbage, radish, garlic, ginger and chilli. It's packed with flavour, fibre and a variety of nutrients, as well as beneficial live cultures.

## What is a SCOBY?

You may come across the term SCOBY when exploring home fermentation. It stands for *Symbiotic Culture of Bacteria and Yeast* and describes a community of microorganisms that work together to ferment food and drinks.

Kefir grains and kombucha cultures are both examples of these living microbial communities, although they look quite different.

## Where can you get started?

The wonderful thing about cultured foods is that many are incredibly easy to make at home. Once you have your starter cultures or grains, you can often reuse them again and again to create fresh batches.

There are lots of online communities dedicated to home fermentation, where people regularly share or sell starter cultures.

I regularly use the **Fermenting Friends UK** Facebook group, where members often offer kefir grains and kombucha cultures.

Facebook Marketplace is another good place to look, or you can find starter cultures through online shops such as Etsy and Amazon.

A good-quality, plain, unpasteurised kombucha can also be used as a starter for brewing your own kombucha.

## What does the science tell us?

Interest in fermented foods has grown considerably in recent years, and we're beginning to see some exciting research into how they may influence our gut microbiome and overall health.

### The importance of microbiome diversity

Our gut is home to trillions of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi and other microbes. Together, they form what we call our gut microbiome, and they play an important role in our overall health.

One of the key indicators researchers look at when studying gut health is microbial diversity – essentially, the variety of different microorganisms living in our digestive system.

Generally, greater microbial diversity is associated with a healthier and more resilient gut ecosystem. Different microorganisms perform different jobs, helping to break down food, produce beneficial compounds, support our immune system and maintain the delicate balance within our digestive tract.

Research organisations such as ZOE have helped bring this subject into the spotlight, highlighting the relationship between the microbes living in our gut, the foods we eat and our wider health.

Importantly, diversity isn't the only measure of a healthy microbiome. The types of microorganisms present and the functions they perform matter too. Nevertheless, encouraging a varied and well-functioning gut microbiome is an important part of supporting our overall wellbeing.

### The Stanford fermented-food study

One of the most interesting studies was conducted by researchers at Stanford University and published in the journal *Cell* in 2021.

The researchers compared two dietary approaches in 36 healthy adults over 17 weeks. One group followed a high-fibre diet, while the other increased their intake of fermented foods such as yoghurt, kefir and fermented vegetables.

The results were fascinating. Those eating more fermented foods experienced an increase in gut microbial diversity, alongside reductions in several inflammatory markers.

Interestingly, the high-fibre group did not experience the same increase in microbial diversity, although fibre did produce other beneficial changes in the gut, including an increase in the microbiome's capacity to break down carbohydrates.

This suggests that fermented foods and fibre may support our gut health in different, complementary ways. Ideally, we want plenty of both!

### What other research tells us

Other research is equally encouraging:

- **Digestive health:** A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 studies, involving more than 4,300 participants, found potential benefits of fermented foods for bowel regularity, stool consistency and digestive comfort.
- **Fermented dairy:** A substantial body of research supports the benefits of yoghurt and fermented milk products. These foods provide beneficial live cultures, and traditional yoghurt cultures have been shown to help people digest lactose more easily.
- **Probiotic strains:** Researchers are also investigating specific bacterial strains, including *Lactobacillus reuteri*, for their potential effects on digestive health, immune function and other aspects of wellbeing. This is an area that has attracted considerable interest, particularly through the work of Dr William Davis and his book *Super Gut*, which explores making specialised probiotic yoghurts at home.

It's worth remembering that not all fermented foods are the same, and benefits can vary depending on the microorganisms involved, the food itself and how it is prepared. Not every fermented food is necessarily a probiotic, and research into many of the claimed benefits is still developing.

What is becoming increasingly clear is that fermented foods are a valuable addition to a varied diet. Alongside fibre-rich vegetables, fruits, legumes and wholegrains, they offer another way to nourish our gut microbiome and support our overall health.

For me, incorporating a variety of traditionally fermented foods into everyday eating is a simple, delicious and enjoyable way to support gut health.

# Deliberate Cold Exposure

*From ice baths to cold showers, we explore the science behind cold exposure, its potential effects on stress resilience and what the evidence really tells us.*

Cold showers, ice baths and wild swimming have become increasingly popular. Celebrities, athletes and wellness influencers often describe cold exposure as a way to boost energy, improve mood, strengthen the immune system and build mental resilience.

But how much of this is supported by science? Are the benefits as impressive as the headlines suggest, and do we need to plunge into icy water to experience them?

One of the most interesting questions is whether deliberately experiencing a manageable physical challenge can help us change the way we respond to stress.

## The practical guide: Exploring cold exposure

### Why deliberately expose ourselves to cold?

Cold is a physical stressor. When we encounter it, the body responds automatically: breathing changes, heart rate and blood pressure can rise, and stress hormones are released to help us respond.

With repeated exposure, we may become more accustomed to that initial discomfort. This has led researchers to explore whether cold exposure could help us practise managing stress, rather than immediately avoiding it.

There are also studies investigating possible effects on alertness, mood, brown fat and energy use. However, evidence for lasting health benefits is still developing.

### What might the benefits be?

Research is exploring several possible effects:

- **Stress response:** Repeated, manageable exposure may help us become more accustomed to the body's initial response to cold.
- **Alertness and mood:** Cold exposure triggers the release of noradrenaline and other signalling chemicals involved in alertness. Some people report feeling more awake and energised afterwards.
- **Heat production:** Cold activates brown fat, a type of tissue that helps the body produce heat by using energy.
- **Metabolism:** Researchers are investigating how cold exposure affects energy use and metabolism, but meaningful long-term benefits in humans have not been established.

It is important to distinguish between what happens immediately when we get cold and what might happen to our health over time.

### How can you explore cold exposure?

If you're curious about the potential benefits of cold exposure, a good place to start is simply by making your shower a little cooler.

You could try finishing your usual shower with 15–30 seconds of cooler water, focusing on keeping your breathing steady rather than holding your breath. If you find it comfortable, you can gradually increase the time.

There is no scientifically established ideal temperature or duration for improving stress resilience. The aim is to experience a manageable level of cold, not to push yourself to the limit.

Cold showers have also been studied much less than cold-water immersion, so we should not assume they produce the same effects.

### What about ice baths and cold-water swimming?

Ice baths and cold-water swimming are other ways people practise cold exposure, but there is limited evidence that these more intense approaches offer additional health benefits.

They also carry greater risks. Sudden immersion can trigger a cold-shock response, causing involuntary gasping, rapid breathing and a sharp increase in heart rate and blood pressure. This can be particularly dangerous in open water.

For most people who want to explore the potential benefits of cold exposure, a cooler shower is a simpler and more controllable option. There is no established need to progress to more extreme forms of cold exposure.

### Does cold water help after exercise?

You may have heard that an ice bath or cold-water immersion after a workout can help reduce soreness and speed up recovery.

There is some evidence that cold-water immersion can reduce perceived muscle soreness after strenuous exercise. However, feeling less sore doesn't necessarily mean your muscles have recovered or adapted better.

In fact, research suggests that regularly using cold-water immersion immediately after resistance training may blunt some of the adaptations that help muscles grow and become stronger.

If your goal is to build muscle through weight training, routinely taking an ice bath straight after your session may work against some of the progress you're trying to achieve.

The evidence is still developing, but it highlights an important distinction: **reducing soreness and supporting long-term training adaptations are not always the same thing.**

This research relates to cold-water immersion, not simply finishing a shower with cooler water.

[Read the 2024 systematic review on cold-water immersion and muscle growth](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235606/).

### A note on safety

Cold exposure is not suitable for everyone. Even cold showers may be uncomfortable or unsafe for some people, while immersion in cold water places greater demands on the body.

- If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, an abnormal heart rhythm or a condition affecting circulation or temperature regulation, seek medical advice before trying cold exposure.
- Never jump or dive into cold water.
- Never go cold-water swimming alone.
- Stop if you feel faint, dizzy, confused, numb or unwell.

The aim is not to see how much cold you can tolerate. More intense exposure does not necessarily mean greater benefit.

*This article is for general information and is not a substitute for medical advice.*

## Learn more: What does the science say?

### 1. What happens to the body when we get cold?

Cold exposure activates the sympathetic nervous system — our natural fight-or-flight response. Heart rate, blood pressure and breathing can increase, while the body releases stress hormones called catecholamines, including adrenaline and noradrenaline.

Noradrenaline helps regulate alertness and body temperature. Cold exposure has also been linked to changes in dopamine, a chemical involved in motivation and mood. However, claims about lasting increases in brain dopamine need more research.

These are well-recognised short-term responses. What they mean for long-term health is less clear.

### 2. Can cold exposure help us become more resilient to stress?

Repeated exposure to manageable cold may help the body become more accustomed to the initial stress response. Over time, some people experience less discomfort and a calmer response to the same cold stimulus.

This is sometimes described as *stress inoculation*: practising a controlled challenge may help us feel more capable of handling discomfort.

But there is an important distinction. Becoming accustomed to cold does not necessarily mean becoming more resilient to everyday psychological stress. That wider benefit remains uncertain.

A 2025 systematic review of cold-water immersion studies found a reduction in perceived stress 12 hours after exposure, but not immediately or at most other time points. A 2026 review also concluded that the evidence for improvements in mood, stress and wellbeing remains uncertain. The studies varied considerably, and many had significant limitations.

So, while the idea is interesting, cold exposure should not be presented as a proven way to improve mental health or manage chronic stress.

### 3. Brown fat, mitochondria and heat production

The body uses several ways to stay warm. One involves brown adipose tissue, or *brown fat*, which produces heat by burning energy. Its cells contain mitochondria, which help convert stored energy into heat through a protein called UCP1.

Research by Susanna Søberg and colleagues has explored how repeated cold exposure affects brown fat and heat production. A small 2021 study found differences in experienced winter swimmers compared with controls. However, participants also used saunas, so the effects cannot be attributed to cold exposure alone.

This research is interesting, but it does not establish that cold exposure leads to meaningful weight loss or improved mitochondrial health.

Søberg has also discussed the idea of allowing the body to rewarm naturally after cold exposure. This is an interesting part of her approach, but it should not be mistaken for a proven method of gaining additional health benefits.

### 4. Do women and men respond differently?

Body composition, hormones and circulation can all influence how someone responds to cold.

Exercise physiologist Dr Stacy Sims has raised questions about whether women respond differently to intense cold exposure, and whether protocols developed largely around male participants are appropriate for everyone. In a conversation on the Huberman Lab podcast, she discusses how factors such as circulation and vasoconstriction may affect women's responses.

These are important questions, but there is not yet enough comparative research to establish universal differences or specific protocols for women and men. Individual responses matter, and extreme cold is not necessary to explore the effects of cold exposure.

### 5. What can we conclude?

Cold exposure clearly triggers a range of short-term physiological responses. There is also promising research into stress adaptation, brown fat and heat production.

But evidence for lasting improvements in mood, stress resilience, metabolism or overall health is still limited. Many studies are small, use different protocols and include relatively few women. Much of the research focuses on cold-water immersion rather than everyday cold showers.

Andrew Huberman's *Protocols: An Operating Manual for the Human Body* brings together research and practical suggestions for deliberate cold exposure, including its potential effects on alertness and stress responses. It is a useful example of how these findings are being translated into wellness advice, but an expert's suggested protocol is not the same as an established medical recommendation.

The most balanced conclusion is that cold exposure can be an optional way to practise tolerating physical discomfort. It should not be treated as a proven health intervention, a treatment for stress or a test of willpower.

**Perhaps the real value is not in how cold we can get, or how long we can endure it, but in learning to meet a manageable challenge with a little more calm and control.**

# Feeding Your Body

## Everyday Nutrition

#### Whole Foods First

Before worrying about carbohydrates, fats or protein, it makes sense to start with the quality of the food itself.

Whole foods provide combinations of fibre, vitamins, minerals and naturally occurring plant compounds, and researchers are still learning how these interact within the body. An apple is far more than the vitamin C it contains.

This is one reason concerns around ultra-processed foods have grown so rapidly.

A large umbrella review published in The BMJ found associations between higher intakes of ultra-processed foods and poorer health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and increased mortality risk. These are mostly observational findings, so they cannot show that processing itself is the cause, and foods within the category vary widely.

That doesn't mean you need perfection. It simply means that if most of your diet is built around foods that still resemble their natural form, you're likely heading in the right direction.

#### Ultra-Processed Foods UPFs

Many UPFs are also built around commercial objectives rather than biological ones. Long shelf life, consistency, convenience and repeat purchases are all priorities for manufacturers.

The result is often products that are highly palatable, heavily marketed and remarkably easy to overconsume. Additives such as emulsifiers are also attracting growing scientific interest for their potential effects on the gut microbiome and intestinal barrier, although the full implications in humans are still being investigated.

Rather than obsessing over whether a food is technically a UPF, focus on what processing has changed. Foods that have lost their fibre, can be consumed rapidly, rely on health claims for credibility and contain long lists of unfamiliar ingredients deserve closer scrutiny.

After all, an apple doesn't need a marketing campaign to convince you it's food.

#### Feed Your Microbiome

For years, nutrition has focused almost exclusively on feeding the human body. We’ve counted calories, tracked protein, measured vitamins and debated fats and carbohydrates. But we're not the only organisms being fed when we eat. Trillions of bacteria, fungi and other microbes live within our digestive tract, forming a complex ecosystem known as the gut microbiome. Increasingly, scientists believe that many of the health benefits associated with fruits, vegetables, coffee, herbs, spices and other plant foods may come not only from the nutrients they provide us, but from the compounds they provide to the microbes living alongside us.

This is where polyphenols become so fascinating. Found naturally in foods such as berries, cocoa, olive oil, tea, coffee and colourful vegetables, these plant compounds appear to help shape the microbial environment within the gut. Rather than being valued solely for their antioxidant properties, polyphenols are now being studied for their ability to influence microbial diversity, support beneficial bacteria and generate compounds that may impact inflammation, metabolism and overall health. In many ways, feeding your microbiome may be just as important as feeding yourself. The good news is that the foods your microbes seem to love are the same real, minimally processed foods that have formed the foundation of healthy diets for generations.

#### Reduce Added Sugar

Added sugar has become so normalised that we barely notice it anymore. It turns up in soft drinks, cereals, sauces, protein bars, flavoured yoghurts and even foods marketed as healthy. Yet the real concern isn't simply that added sugar provides calories. It's that half of the sugar we consume is fructose, a molecule that is processed predominantly by the liver and follows a very different metabolic path from glucose.

When the liver is repeatedly flooded with fructose, particularly from sweetened drinks and ultra-processed foods, it can convert that surplus into fat through a process known as de novo lipogenesis. Over time, this may contribute to liver fat accumulation, insulin resistance and broader metabolic dysfunction. It's this unique relationship between fructose and the liver that has led researchers such as Robert Lustig to draw comparisons with alcohol metabolism and describe fructose as "alcohol without the buzz". While an apple is not a can of cola and sugar is not alcohol, understanding what happens to fructose once it reaches the liver sheds light on why added sugar deserves far more attention than its calorie count alone.

#### Fibre Does More Than Keep You Regular

Fibre has probably suffered from a marketing problem. It rarely receives the attention given to protein, yet it may be one of the most important components of a healthy diet.

Part of the confusion is that fibre isn't a single substance. Different types of fibre perform different jobs within the body.

Soluble fibres found in foods such as oats, beans and lentils help regulate blood sugar and cholesterol.

Insoluble fibres found in vegetables, nuts and whole grains support digestive function and bowel regularity.

Prebiotic fibres found in foods such as onions, garlic, leeks and asparagus act as fuel for beneficial gut bacteria.

#### Make Your Starches Work Harder

Resistant starch is considered a type of dietary fiber because it resists digestion in the small intestine and is fermented by gut bacteria in the colon.

Starchy foods often get caught up in nutritional debates, but the real question isn't whether you should eat starches. It's what type you're eating and what happens to them once they've been prepared.

Foods such as potatoes, rice, oats, beans and lentils can all play a role in a healthy diet. Even better, some can be transformed into a richer source of resistant starch simply by cooking and **cooling them before eating**.

Resistant starch escapes digestion in the small intestine and reaches the colon, where it becomes fuel for gut bacteria. This fermentation process helps produce beneficial compounds such as butyrate, which is associated with gut barrier integrity and metabolic health.

Something as simple as cooled potatoes in a salad or leftover rice used the following day can provide benefits beyond basic nutrition.

#### Fermented Foods Are Making a Comeback

Long before probiotics became a billion-pound industry, humans were consuming fermented foods as a normal part of life.

Foods such as kefir, live yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut and miso introduce beneficial compounds into the diet and may help support microbial diversity.

Modern research has renewed interest in these traditional foods, with studies suggesting that regular consumption can increase microbiome diversity while also influencing markers of inflammation.

The lesson here isn't that everyone needs another supplement. Sometimes the oldest approaches remain some of the most effective.

#### Prioritise Protein Quality

Protein remains important for maintaining muscle mass, supporting recovery and promoting satiety, particularly as we age.

However, quality matters just as much as quantity. Eggs, fish, seafood, meat, Greek yoghurt, tofu, tempeh, beans and lentils all provide valuable sources of protein alongside other nutrients that support health.

Rather than chasing ever-higher protein targets, a more balanced approach is to ensure each meal contains a meaningful source of protein while leaving plenty of room for vegetables, fibre-rich foods and plant diversity.

#### Intermittent Fasting

Another area that has attracted increasing attention is time-restricted eating.

Research suggests that leaving a longer gap between the evening meal and breakfast may support metabolic health, blood sugar regulation and circadian rhythm alignment.

Importantly, this isn't about starving yourself. A simple twelve-hour overnight fasting window is enough for many people to capture some of the potential benefits. Finishing dinner at 7pm and eating breakfast at 7am is hardly extreme, yet it gives the digestive system a valuable break from the constant cycle of eating that has become normal in modern life.

# Fibre

Fibre is often associated with digestive regularity, but its influence reaches much further. It helps regulate cholesterol and blood sugar, supports bowel health and provides nourishment for the trillions of microbes living in the gut.

Yet not all fibre behaves in the same way. Different types have different effects, and the foods that contain them bring a wider range of nutrients and plant compounds to the table.

So what does the science tell us about fibre, gut health and the microbiome?

## What's Proven? Why Fibre Matters

Dietary fibre is the part of plant foods that our digestive enzymes cannot fully break down. Much of it passes through the small intestine and reaches the colon, where some types are fermented by gut bacteria.

Research supports several important roles for fibre:

- **Digestive health:** Fibre helps maintain bowel regularity and can support healthy stool consistency.
- **Cholesterol:** Certain soluble fibres, including those found in oats and barley, can help lower LDL cholesterol.
- **Blood sugar:** Some fibres slow digestion and the absorption of nutrients, helping moderate the rise in blood glucose after meals.
- **Gut health:** Fermentable fibres provide nourishment for gut microbes, which produce compounds that help maintain the intestinal environment.
- **Long-term health:** Higher-fibre diets are associated with lower risks of conditions including cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. These associations are supported by a substantial body of research, although overall dietary patterns also matter.

Fibre is not a single nutrient with one function. Its effects depend on the type, amount and food source.

## Not All Fibre Is The Same

Different fibres have different physical properties and are processed differently by the digestive system.

**Soluble fibre**

Soluble fibre absorbs water and can form a gel-like substance in the digestive tract. Some types help regulate cholesterol and blood glucose, while others are readily fermented by gut bacteria.

Sources include oats, beans, lentils, apples, citrus fruits and carrots.

**Insoluble fibre**

Insoluble fibre does not dissolve in water. It contributes bulk to stool and helps food move through the digestive system.

Sources include whole grains, vegetable skins, nuts, seeds and many vegetables.

**Resistant starch**

Resistant starch escapes digestion in the small intestine and reaches the colon, where gut bacteria can ferment it. It occurs naturally in foods such as legumes, green bananas and oats. Cooking and then cooling foods such as potatoes and rice can also increase their resistant starch content.

**Prebiotic fibres**

Some fibres are considered prebiotics because they are used by particular microorganisms and can produce health benefits. Examples include inulin, fructooligosaccharides (FOS) and galactooligosaccharides (GOS).

They occur naturally in foods such as onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus, chicory root and legumes.

These categories can overlap: a fibre may be soluble, fermentable and prebiotic. The important point is that different fibres support different digestive and microbial processes.

## What's Emerging? Fibre And The Gut Microbiome

One of the most fascinating areas of nutrition research is the relationship between dietary fibre and the gut microbiome.

When gut bacteria ferment certain fibres, they produce compounds called short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). The main ones are:

- **Butyrate:** An important energy source for cells lining the colon. It also plays a role in maintaining the gut barrier and regulating local immune activity.
- **Propionate:** Absorbed and processed largely by the liver, it may influence glucose and energy metabolism.
- **Acetate:** The most abundant SCFA, it can be used by tissues throughout the body and acts as a signalling molecule.

These compounds help explain how food can influence processes beyond digestion itself. Researchers are investigating their potential roles in metabolic health, immune regulation and inflammation.

Another area of interest is microbial diversity. Different microbes use different food sources, so a varied range of plant foods can provide a wider range of substrates for the gut ecosystem.

However, more diversity is not automatically better in every circumstance, and researchers are still working to understand which microbial changes translate into meaningful health benefits.

### Beyond Fibre: Polyphenols And Coffee

Fibre is not the only plant-derived substance that interacts with gut microbes.

Polyphenols are naturally occurring compounds found in foods and drinks such as berries, tea, cocoa and coffee. Gut microbes can help break down some of these compounds, while polyphenols may also influence which microbes thrive.

A large multi-cohort study published in *Nature Microbiology* reported an association between regular coffee consumption and higher levels of a bacterium called *Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus*.

This is an interesting finding, but it does not prove that coffee causes a health benefit or that this particular bacterium is responsible. It does highlight how the relationship between diet and the microbiome extends beyond fibre alone.

### The Akkermansia Connection

Another bacterium attracting scientific attention is *Akkermansia muciniphila*.

It lives in the mucus layer lining the intestine and is being studied for its potential role in gut barrier function and metabolic health. Some studies have found associations between its abundance and markers of better metabolic health.

Its relationship with the gut is complex, however. Higher levels are not necessarily better in every context, and researchers have not established a simple dietary formula for increasing it.

Plant-rich diets, fibre and other bioactive food compounds are all being investigated as influences on the microbiome. For now, *Akkermansia* is an intriguing area of research rather than a target that needs to be pursued through a particular food or supplement.

## What's Marketing? The Truth About Added Fibre

Fibre is increasingly added to breakfast cereals, snack bars, yoghurts and other packaged foods.

Common ingredients include inulin, chicory root fibre, psyllium, polydextrose and resistant starch.

Some isolated fibres have demonstrated benefits. Psyllium, for example, has evidence supporting its role in bowel function and cholesterol management. Inulin can be fermented by gut bacteria and may increase certain microbial populations.

But a product labelled "high fibre" is not automatically equivalent to a whole plant food.

An apple or handful of raspberries provides more than fibre. It also contains water, vitamins, minerals and a range of plant compounds, all within the natural structure of the food.

Processing can remove some of this structure and its associated nutrients. Adding isolated fibre back may improve the fibre content, but it does not necessarily restore everything that was lost.

That does not make fortified foods worthless. It simply means the type of fibre, the wider nutritional composition and the food it comes in all matter.

## The Real Goal: Fibre Diversity

It is easy to focus on the number of grams of fibre in a day. But the variety of fibres in the diet is also worth considering.

Different plant foods contain different combinations of soluble fibre, insoluble fibre, resistant starch and other fermentable compounds. A varied diet can therefore provide a broader range of materials for gut microbes to use.

Foods such as beans, lentils, whole grains, vegetables, berries, nuts, seeds and fruit all contribute different types of fibre. Even herbs and spices bring additional plant compounds to the overall mix.

The science around plant diversity and microbial diversity is still developing, but it offers an important perspective: fibre is not just about quantity. Variety matters too.

## The Science Deep Dive: From Plant Structure To Short-Chain Fatty Acids

Human digestive enzymes cannot break down many of the complex carbohydrates found in plant cell walls and other plant structures. These compounds therefore pass into the colon, where microbes with the right enzymes can ferment them.

During fermentation, bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids, particularly acetate, propionate and butyrate. These molecules can be absorbed and used by the body, or act locally within the gut.

Butyrate is especially important because colon cells use it as a major energy source. It also influences the activity of cells involved in maintaining the intestinal barrier and regulating immune responses.

Different microbes specialise in using different substrates. This is one reason why a range of plant foods can support a broader range of microbial activity.

The process is not identical in everyone. Microbiome composition, gut transit time, diet and other individual factors all influence how fibre is fermented and which compounds are produced.

The bigger picture is that dietary fibre connects the food we eat with the activity of our gut microbes — and the compounds those microbes produce.

## Final Thoughts

Fibre is much more than roughage. It supports digestion, influences cholesterol and blood sugar, and provides the raw material for gut microbes to produce compounds involved in intestinal health.

The emerging science is particularly exciting because it shows that the relationship between food and health does not end when digestion begins. What our gut microbes can do with the food we eat may be just as interesting as the nutrients themselves.

Rather than focusing only on fibre claims on food packaging, the wider picture is worth considering: different fibres, a variety of plant foods and the many compounds that naturally occur alongside them.

**Important Disclaimer:** This article is for educational purposes only and is not medical advice. It is not intended to diagnose, treat or prevent any medical condition. Fibre intake and tolerance can vary, particularly for people with gastrointestinal conditions or specific medical needs. Anyone considering significant dietary changes should consult an appropriately qualified healthcare professional.

# Reducing Our Toxic Burden

#### Understanding everyday chemical exposures


We are exposed to thousands of chemicals through the food we eat, the water we drink, the products we use and the environment around us. While not every chemical exposure is harmful, the growing number of substances we encounter daily raises important questions about their potential effects on long-term health.

I believe one of the most worthwhile things we can do for our wellbeing is to become more conscious of our everyday chemical exposures.

This isn't about becoming fearful of our surroundings or trying to eliminate every chemical from our lives. That would be impossible. Instead, it is about understanding where unnecessary exposures occur and making sensible changes to reduce our overall toxic burden.

Three areas I find particularly interesting are phthalates, bisphenol A (BPA) and microplastics.

## 1. Phthalates: chemicals that interfere with hormone signalling

Phthalates are a family of chemicals used to make plastics more flexible and durable. They are also used in some cosmetics, fragrances, flooring, food packaging and household products.

One reason they attract scientific attention is that certain phthalates are recognised endocrine-disrupting chemicals.

Our endocrine system is responsible for producing and regulating hormones, which influence reproduction, metabolism, growth, thyroid function and many other biological processes.

Certain phthalates can interfere with hormone signalling, particularly pathways involved in reproductive development.

Research has associated exposure to some phthalates with:

- **Reproductive health:** Associations have been found between certain phthalate exposures and changes in reproductive hormone levels, semen quality and fertility-related outcomes.
- **Pregnancy and development:** Exposure during pregnancy is being investigated for potential effects on foetal growth and neurodevelopment.
- **Metabolic health:** Research has explored associations with insulin resistance, obesity and metabolic dysfunction.
- **Thyroid function:** Some phthalates may interfere with thyroid hormone signalling, although findings vary between chemicals and studies.

It is important to remember that phthalates are not one single substance. Their properties and potential health effects differ considerably.

## 2. BPA: a chemical with hormone-like activity

Bisphenol A, commonly known as BPA, has traditionally been used in the manufacture of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins.

It has been found in products such as some food and drink containers, the linings of food cans and certain thermal paper receipts.

BPA has attracted considerable attention because it can interact with hormone receptors and influence endocrine signalling.

It has attracted attention because it can interact with hormone-related pathways, and scientists and regulators continue to assess its potential health effects, particularly during sensitive stages of development.

Areas of research include:

**Hormonal health**

BPA can interact with several hormone-related pathways, potentially influencing reproductive and metabolic processes.

**Brain development**

Researchers have investigated whether exposure during pregnancy and early childhood may influence brain development, behaviour and cognitive function.

**Metabolic health**

Some studies have identified associations between BPA exposure and obesity, insulin resistance and metabolic disturbances.

**Cancer research**

Laboratory and animal studies have explored possible links between BPA exposure and hormone-sensitive cancers, including breast and prostate cancer. However, a direct causal relationship in humans has not been established.

Regulatory authorities have also taken action. The European Union adopted a major restriction on BPA in food-contact materials in 2024, reflecting concerns about exposure and health risks.

## 3. Microplastics: an emerging environmental concern

Perhaps one of the most concerning developments in environmental health research is the discovery of microplastics and nanoplastics throughout our environment.

Microplastics are tiny plastic particles, generally smaller than five millimetres. Nanoplastics are even smaller.

They originate from the breakdown of larger plastic products, synthetic textiles, packaging, tyres and numerous other sources.

They have been detected in drinking water, food, indoor dust and outdoor air.

Researchers have also detected plastic particles in various human tissues and biological samples.

This raises important questions about what happens when these particles enter our bodies.

Areas currently being investigated include:

- **Inflammation and oxidative stress:** Laboratory studies suggest that certain particles may trigger inflammatory responses and oxidative stress.
- **Gut health:** Researchers are investigating whether microplastics influence the gut barrier, microbial balance and digestive function.
- **Chemical exposure:** Plastics can contain additives, and particles may carry other environmental contaminants.
- **Cellular effects:** Laboratory research suggests that particle size, shape, polymer type and chemical composition may influence how cells respond.

The science is still developing. We do not yet know the extent to which everyday microplastic exposure causes disease in humans, or what levels of exposure may be harmful.

The UK Committee on the Medical Effects of Air Pollutants published an assessment of airborne microplastics in 2025, highlighting significant gaps in our understanding.

## 4. How I reduce everyday exposure

I prefer practical changes that can easily become part of everyday life.

**Move towards glass and stainless steel**

I prefer glass storage containers and stainless steel water bottles rather than relying heavily on plastic.

I am particularly mindful about heating food in plastic containers, as heat can increase the release of certain chemicals and particles.

**Be selective with food packaging**

Where practical, I choose fresh or frozen foods and avoid unnecessary packaging.

I also look at the materials used in food containers, particularly those intended for repeated use.

**Filter drinking water**

Water filtration can be useful, but different filters remove different contaminants. I would choose a system based on independently verified performance rather than assuming every filter removes microplastics, heavy metals and chemicals equally well.

**Reduce unnecessary contact with thermal receipts**

Some thermal papers have contained BPA or related chemicals. I avoid handling receipts unnecessarily and wash my hands before eating.

**Reduce household plastic dust**

Regular damp dusting and vacuuming with a good filtration system can help reduce indoor dust, which may contain particles from synthetic materials.

## 5. The bigger picture

I don't believe we need to live in fear of our environment. However, becoming more aware of what we bring into our homes, what we store our food in and what we use on a daily basis is a sensible step towards protecting long-term health.

The objective is to reduce unnecessary exposure wherever practical, particularly during vulnerable periods such as pregnancy and early childhood.

Small changes, repeated consistently, can help us create a healthier environment without making life unnecessarily complicated.

**Sometimes, protecting our health starts with simply questioning the things we have come to regard as normal.**


> Evidence note: the main concern is chemicals such as BPA and phthalates migrating from plastic into food, which increases with heat, fatty foods and scratched or old containers. Regulators set limits on these chemicals; the strongest evidence of harm comes from animal and population studies, and the long-term effects at everyday levels in people are still being researched. Simple steps such as not microwaving food in plastic reduce exposure at little cost.
# Food Additives and Gut Health

#### Looking beyond calories to understand what we are feeding our microbiome

We have become increasingly focused on calories, carbohydrates, fats and protein when thinking about nutrition. But there is another important question we should be asking: what else are we consuming alongside our food?

Many modern processed foods contain a range of additives designed to improve texture, colour, flavour, shelf life and appearance.

Although approved food additives undergo safety assessments, I believe it is worth understanding how some of these ingredients may interact with our digestive system and the trillions of microorganisms living within our gut.

Our gut microbiome plays an important role in digestion, immune regulation, nutrient metabolism and communication with the brain.

Maintaining a diverse and balanced microbial community is therefore an important part of overall wellbeing.

## 1. Emulsifiers: what happens when we interfere with the gut environment?

Emulsifiers are widely used in processed foods to prevent ingredients from separating and to create a smooth, consistent texture.

They are found in products such as:

- Ice cream.
- Commercial sauces and dressings.
- Baked goods.
- Processed desserts.
- Plant-based milk alternatives.
- Packaged convenience foods.

Some emulsifiers, including carboxymethylcellulose and polysorbate 80, have attracted scientific interest because of their potential effects on the gut microbiome.

Laboratory and animal studies suggest that certain emulsifiers may influence the mucus layer lining the intestine, alter microbial populations and affect intestinal barrier function.

Human research is much more limited, although some controlled studies have reported changes in gut microbial composition following exposure to particular emulsifiers.

We should be careful not to assume all emulsifiers have the same effects. Different compounds behave differently, and the long-term implications of ordinary dietary exposure are still being investigated.

## 2. Preservatives: extending shelf life, but what about our gut?

Preservatives are used to prevent spoilage and extend the shelf life of food.

They help protect against microbial growth and can reduce food waste, so they do serve a useful purpose.

However, researchers are increasingly interested in how different preservatives interact with the human gut microbiome.

Some preservatives have demonstrated antimicrobial effects in laboratory settings. This raises questions about whether particular compounds might also influence beneficial gut microorganisms.

Sodium benzoate, for example, is widely used in acidic foods and drinks.

Although it is approved for use within regulated limits, it is worth remembering that the overall nutritional quality of a food matters too.

Rather than becoming preoccupied with individual ingredients, I prefer to reduce dependence on heavily processed products and choose foods with shorter, simpler ingredient lists where possible.

## 3. Artificial colours and flavour enhancers

Artificial colours are used to make foods look more appealing, particularly confectionery, drinks, desserts and children's foods.

Some synthetic colours have been associated with increased hyperactivity in certain children, although responses vary and this does not mean that all artificial colours have the same effects.

Flavour enhancers, such as monosodium glutamate (MSG), are used to intensify savoury flavours.

MSG has been extensively studied and is considered safe for most people at normal dietary exposures by major food safety authorities. Claims that ordinary dietary MSG overstimulates the brain or damages neurotransmitter function are not well established.

My preference is not to demonise individual ingredients but to question why so many foods need extensive formulation to taste, look and feel appealing.

## 4. Ultra-processed foods and the microbiome

The wider issue is not necessarily one particular additive. It is the growing proportion of our diet made up of ultra-processed foods.

These products are often formulated to be convenient, highly palatable and long-lasting.

They may also contain relatively little fibre and fewer naturally occurring plant compounds than minimally processed foods.

Fibre is particularly important because beneficial gut bacteria ferment certain fibres to produce short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate.

These compounds help support the intestinal environment and influence immune and metabolic signalling.

A diet dominated by highly processed foods can therefore displace the fibre-rich foods that help maintain a healthy microbiome.

Research published in *Nature Reviews Gastroenterology & Hepatology* has highlighted the potential effects of ultra-processed foods and additives on the gut, while also noting that much of the evidence remains preclinical.

## 5. How I support a healthier gut environment

Rather than trying to eliminate every additive, I focus on building a diet that naturally reduces reliance on heavily processed foods.

**Eat more whole foods**

Vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts, seeds and whole grains provide fibre and beneficial plant compounds.

**Increase dietary diversity**

Different plant foods provide different fibres and polyphenols, supporting a wider range of gut microorganisms.

**Include fermented foods**

Kefir, live yoghurt, sauerkraut and kimchi can introduce beneficial microorganisms and add variety to the diet.

[Explore my article on cultured and fermented foods](https://wellnous.godaddysites.com/cultured-foods).

**Read ingredient labels**

I find it useful to become familiar with the ingredients in everyday foods. The aim isn't to reject everything unfamiliar, but to make more informed choices.

**Reduce heavily processed convenience foods**

Where practical, prepare meals using recognisable ingredients rather than relying on packaged alternatives.

**Prioritise fibre**

Beans, lentils, vegetables, oats, seeds and nuts are excellent ways to support microbial diversity and short-chain fatty acid production.

## 6. Looking after the gut means looking after the whole body

Our gut is much more than a digestive organ. It is an important communication centre linking our immune system, metabolism and nervous system.

The microbiome is influenced by diet, sleep, exercise, stress, medication and our wider environment.

I believe one of the simplest ways to support it is to return to a more varied, minimally processed diet.

We don't need to become obsessed with every ingredient on a food label. We simply need to make whole, nutrient-dense foods the foundation of our diet.

**Feed the body well, and create an environment in which beneficial gut bacteria can thrive.**

# The Air We Breathe

#### Creating a healthier indoor environment

We spend a significant proportion of our lives indoors, whether at home, at work or in other enclosed environments. Yet how often do we stop to consider the quality of the air we breathe?

We tend to think of air pollution as something outside, associated with traffic, factories and industrial emissions. However, indoor air can also contain a complex mixture of pollutants from furniture, building materials, cleaning products, cooking, fragrances and everyday household activities.

I believe creating a healthier home environment should be an important part of our overall approach to wellbeing.

We invest time thinking about nutrition, exercise and sleep, but the air we breathe continuously throughout the day deserves just as much attention.

## 1. Understanding indoor air pollution

Indoor air pollution can come from many different sources.

Some pollutants enter from outside, while others are generated within the home.

Common sources include:

**Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)**

VOCs are chemicals that evaporate into the air from certain products and materials.

They can be released by:

- Paints and varnishes.
- Furniture and composite wood products.
- Carpets and flooring.
- Cleaning products.
- Air fresheners and fragrances.
- Adhesives and building materials.

Some VOCs can irritate the eyes and respiratory tract, while prolonged exposure to certain compounds is associated with more serious health concerns.

Formaldehyde is one example. It can be released from certain pressed-wood products, furniture and building materials.

**Combustion pollutants**

Cooking, gas appliances, candles, fireplaces and tobacco smoke can all contribute to indoor air pollution.

Gas cooking can release nitrogen dioxide, while burning wood or other fuels can produce fine particulate matter.

Fine particles are particularly concerning because they can penetrate deep into the lungs.

**Household dust**

Dust is not simply dirt. It can contain fibres, pollen, pet dander, mould spores, particles from furniture and plastics, and residues from everyday household products.

Regular cleaning and good ventilation help manage this accumulation.

## 2. Why air quality matters

Our respiratory system is exposed to the surrounding environment every time we breathe.

Poor indoor air quality can contribute to:

- Irritation of the eyes, nose and throat.
- Respiratory symptoms and asthma exacerbation.
- Allergic reactions.
- Headaches and discomfort.
- Increased exposure to pollutants associated with cardiovascular and respiratory disease.

The effects depend on the particular pollutant, its concentration, duration of exposure and individual susceptibility.

Children, older adults and people with existing respiratory or cardiovascular conditions may be particularly vulnerable.

## 3. Practical ways to improve indoor air quality

I prefer to focus on reducing pollutants at their source rather than simply trying to filter everything out afterwards.

**Ventilate regularly**

Opening windows when outdoor air quality allows can help dilute indoor pollutants and introduce fresh air.

Use kitchen extractor fans that vent outside when cooking, particularly when frying or using a gas hob.

Bathroom extraction is also important for removing moisture and reducing the conditions that encourage mould growth.

**Consider a HEPA air purifier**

A properly sized HEPA air purifier can help remove airborne particles, including dust, pollen, pet dander and some other fine particles.

Look at the clean air delivery rate (CADR) and choose a unit suitable for the size of the room.

It is important to understand that HEPA filters remove particles, not all gases and chemical vapours. Activated carbon filtration can help reduce certain gaseous pollutants and odours, although effectiveness depends on the quantity and quality of the carbon.

**Choose cleaning products carefully**

Many conventional cleaning and fragranced products release VOCs.

I prefer simpler, fragrance-free products where practical, and avoid using multiple cleaning chemicals unnecessarily.

Never mix household cleaning products, particularly bleach with ammonia or acids.

**Reduce indoor fragrance**

Air fresheners, scented candles and diffusers may make a room smell pleasant, but they can also introduce additional airborne chemicals.

Rather than masking unwanted smells, I prefer to identify and remove their source.

**Control dust**

Regular vacuuming, preferably with a well-sealed HEPA-filtered vacuum, and damp dusting can help reduce airborne dust and allergens.

**Address damp and mould**

Excess moisture creates conditions in which mould can grow.

Fix leaks, improve ventilation and address persistent condensation rather than simply cleaning visible mould repeatedly.

## 4. The role of indoor plants

Plants can make a home feel calmer and more connected to nature.

They also provide visual interest and can contribute to our sense of wellbeing.

There is considerable interest in their ability to remove pollutants, but it is important to distinguish laboratory experiments from real homes.

Research has not demonstrated that ordinary numbers of houseplants significantly improve indoor air quality. The US Environmental Protection Agency highlights ventilation and source control as much more effective approaches.

I still enjoy having plants around the home, but I see them primarily as a way of bringing nature indoors rather than as a replacement for proper air filtration.

## 5. Creating a healthier sleeping environment

We spend a substantial amount of time in our bedrooms, making this an especially worthwhile area to consider.

I would pay particular attention to:

- Ventilating the bedroom regularly.
- Keeping dust accumulation under control.
- Washing bedding regularly.
- Avoiding strong fragrances.
- Keeping humidity at a comfortable level.
- Addressing any damp or mould.
- Avoiding unnecessary use of scented products.

A clean, comfortable and well-ventilated sleeping environment can help support better rest and respiratory comfort.

## 6. The bigger picture

We cannot control every environmental pollutant, particularly those outside our homes. But we have considerably more influence over our immediate surroundings than we sometimes realise.

Improving ventilation, reducing unnecessary chemical products, controlling dust and using appropriate filtration are relatively straightforward changes.

I believe a healthy home should be more than visually attractive. It should also be a place where we can breathe comfortably, sleep well and recover from the demands of everyday life.

**The quality of our environment matters because we are interacting with it every moment of every day – and the air we breathe is perhaps the most constant interaction of all.**

# Skincare, Cosmetics and Sunscreen

#### Becoming more conscious of what we put on our bodies

We spend a great deal of time thinking about what we eat and drink, but how much attention do we give to the products we apply to our skin every day?

Moisturisers, shampoos, deodorants, cosmetics, fragrances and sunscreens can contain a wide variety of chemical ingredients. Many are perfectly safe when used as intended, but some ingredients deserve closer attention because of their potential to irritate the skin, trigger allergies or influence hormone-related pathways.

Our skin is an important protective barrier, but it is not completely impermeable. Certain substances can pass through it, depending on their chemical properties, formulation and the condition of the skin.

I prefer a simpler approach to personal care: use products that serve a purpose, understand their ingredients and avoid unnecessary exposure where practical.

## 1. Parabens: understanding the concerns

Parabens are preservatives used in some cosmetics and personal care products to prevent microbial growth and extend shelf life.

They have been widely used because they are effective and relatively inexpensive.

Some parabens demonstrate weak oestrogen-like activity in laboratory studies, which has led to questions about their potential endocrine-disrupting effects.

Research has explored possible relationships with:

- Hormone signalling.
- Reproductive health.
- Breast tissue exposure.
- Metabolic and developmental processes.

However, detecting parabens in human tissue does not establish that they cause disease. Evidence of harm at normal cosmetic exposure levels is not conclusive, and different parabens have different safety profiles.

I prefer to check ingredients and choose simpler formulations where practical, rather than assuming that every paraben-containing product is dangerous.

## 2. Sulphates: are they too harsh for our skin?

Sulphates such as sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS) and sodium laureth sulphate (SLES) are cleansing agents commonly found in shampoos, body washes, facial cleansers and toothpaste.

They work by helping water remove oil, dirt and other substances.

The main concern with SLS is its potential to irritate the skin, particularly at higher concentrations or with prolonged contact.

It can strip away some of the skin's natural oils, potentially leaving the skin dry or irritated.

People with sensitive skin or conditions such as eczema may prefer gentler, fragrance-free alternatives.

I don't believe everyone needs to avoid sulphates completely, but it is worth considering whether the products we use are unnecessarily harsh for our skin.

## 3. Synthetic fragrances: more than just a pleasant smell

Fragrance is one of the most common causes of allergic contact dermatitis from cosmetic products.

The term fragrance or parfum can represent a mixture of many different substances.

Some fragrance ingredients are associated with skin sensitivity, irritation and allergic reactions.

Certain fragrance formulations may also contain phthalates, although this is not true of all fragranced products.

For people with sensitive skin, choosing fragrance-free rather than simply unscented products can be a useful distinction.

I prefer to avoid unnecessary fragrance in products that remain on my skin for long periods.

## 4. Sunscreen: protection versus ingredient concerns

Sunscreen is an interesting area because we need to balance two important considerations: protecting our skin from ultraviolet radiation and understanding the ingredients used to provide that protection.

Ultraviolet radiation from the sun is a major contributor to premature skin ageing and skin cancer.

UVA rays penetrate more deeply into the skin and contribute to photoageing, while UVB rays are particularly associated with sunburn and direct DNA damage.

Both are important, which is why broad-spectrum protection matters.

Some chemical sunscreen filters, including oxybenzone and octinoxate, have attracted attention because studies have demonstrated that certain ingredients can be absorbed through the skin.

Oxybenzone has also shown hormone-related activity in some laboratory and animal studies.

However, absorption into the bloodstream does not automatically mean an ingredient causes harm. The implications for human health at normal levels of use remain an area of ongoing research.

I think it is sensible to understand these concerns without losing sight of the established benefits of protecting ourselves from excessive UV exposure.

## 5. Mineral sunscreens: an alternative worth considering

Mineral sunscreens generally use zinc oxide, titanium dioxide or a combination of both to protect against ultraviolet radiation.

They are often a good option for people with sensitive skin and those who prefer to avoid certain organic UV filters.

Zinc oxide is particularly useful because it can provide broad-spectrum UVA and UVB protection when properly formulated.

Some people prefer non-nano mineral products, although it is worth remembering that particle size alone does not establish whether a sunscreen is safer or more effective.

For everyday use, I would look for:

- Broad-spectrum protection.
- SPF 30 or higher.
- Good UVA protection.
- A formulation suitable for my skin.
- Water resistance when swimming or exercising outdoors.

I would also favour lotions and creams over sprays containing mineral particles, to avoid unnecessary inhalation.

## 6. A word about natural oils and sun protection

There is growing interest in natural oils such as red raspberry seed oil as alternatives to conventional sunscreen.

Although some laboratory studies have explored their ability to absorb ultraviolet radiation, these oils do not have sufficiently reliable, standardised and independently established SPF protection to replace a tested sunscreen.

I see natural oils as potentially useful moisturising ingredients, but not as a substitute for proven sun protection.

Similarly, a product being described as natural or organic does not automatically mean it is safer or less irritating.

## 7. A simpler approach to personal care

Rather than having a complicated bathroom cabinet filled with numerous products, I prefer to focus on a few well-chosen essentials.

**Read ingredient labels**

Become familiar with the ingredients in your everyday products and identify those that cause irritation or sensitivity.

**Choose simpler formulations**

Where practical, choose products with fewer unnecessary fragrances and additives.

**Use fragrance-free products when appropriate**

This can be particularly helpful for people with sensitive skin.

**Avoid unnecessary product layering**

Using multiple serums, creams, fragrances and cosmetics can increase the number of ingredients coming into contact with your skin.

**Choose sun protection carefully**

Use a properly tested broad-spectrum sunscreen and combine it with shade, clothing and sensible sun exposure habits.

## 8. The bigger picture

Our skin is our largest organ and an important part of our body's protective system.

Looking after it doesn't require an elaborate collection of expensive products or an obsession with every ingredient.

I believe the best approach is to understand what we are using, choose products that suit our individual needs and avoid unnecessary exposure where sensible.

Most importantly, we should never sacrifice proven sun protection because of concerns about individual sunscreen ingredients.

**Being conscious of what we put on our bodies is simply another way of taking responsibility for our long-term health and wellbeing.**

# Infra Red Exposure

#### From natural sunlight to red-light therapy, discover how infrared interacts with our cells, why mitochondria are at the centre of the research, and what this could mean for our health.

Light is about much more than helping us see. Different wavelengths of light interact with the body in different ways, influencing everything from our body clock to processes taking place deep within our cells.

One part of the light spectrum attracting growing interest is infrared. Found naturally in sunlight and produced by sources such as fire, infrared is best known for the warmth we feel when it reaches our skin. But researchers are increasingly interested in how certain wavelengths of infrared and near-infrared light may influence cellular activity, particularly within our mitochondria.

So, what is infrared, why is it attracting attention, and could it have a role in supporting our health?

## Key points

- Infrared is the part of sunlight we feel as warmth but cannot see, and near-infrared penetrates further into tissue than visible red light.
- Red and near-infrared light are used in an approach known as photobiomodulation (PBM), which may influence cellular activity, particularly within our mitochondria.
- Clinical interest is strongest for specific applications such as some wound-healing, pain and hair-loss outcomes, while wider metabolic and brain benefits are still being researched.
- Sunlight, fire and incandescent bulbs are natural sources of infrared, whereas many modern LEDs produce very little.
- Getting natural daylight during the day supports our body clock — a practical benefit quite apart from the emerging research.
- Sun safety still applies: infrared is not a reason to seek more UV exposure.

## The basics: what is infrared?

Infrared is a form of electromagnetic radiation that sits just beyond the red end of the visible light spectrum. We cannot see it, but we often experience it as warmth.

Sunlight is a natural source of infrared, alongside visible light and ultraviolet radiation. Fire, heated objects and traditional incandescent light bulbs also produce infrared.

Infrared covers a range of wavelengths, generally grouped into near, mid and far infrared. These different wavelengths interact with the body in different ways. Near-infrared light is particularly interesting to researchers because it can penetrate further into biological tissue than visible red light.

This is also why red and near-infrared light are used in a treatment approach known as photobiomodulation (PBM), sometimes called red-light therapy. Rather than simply warming the body, these treatments use specific wavelengths of light to influence biological processes.

## Have we changed our relationship with light?

Our modern indoor environment is very different from the one in which humans evolved.

We spend much of our time inside, surrounded by screens, LEDs and artificial lighting. Many modern LEDs produce very little infrared compared with natural sunlight or traditional incandescent bulbs.

Incandescent bulbs produce a broad spectrum of light, including substantial infrared, because their filaments become very hot. Fire is another familiar source of infrared.

This doesn't mean modern lighting is automatically harmful, but it does highlight how different our everyday light environment has become.

Natural daylight provides a broad spectrum of light and plays an important role in regulating our circadian rhythm — our internal body clock. This influences sleep, hormone rhythms and many aspects of metabolism.

Getting outside during the day is therefore a simple way to reconnect with an important part of our natural light environment.

## Why is infrared attracting so much interest?

The excitement around infrared comes partly from its relationship with our cells and, in particular, our mitochondria.

Mitochondria are often described as the powerhouses of the cell because they help produce ATP, the energy currency that powers many of the body's processes. They also play important roles in cellular communication, repair and responses to stress.

Researchers are exploring whether certain wavelengths of red and near-infrared light can influence mitochondrial activity and support the processes involved in maintaining healthy cells.

This has led to interest in a range of potential applications, including:

- **Cellular energy:** influencing mitochondrial activity and the production of ATP.
- **Skin health:** supporting wound healing, skin repair and collagen-related processes.
- **Recovery and repair:** exploring effects on inflammation, pain and tissue recovery.
- **Brain health:** investigating whether light can influence cellular activity and function in the nervous system.
- **Metabolic health:** exploring possible effects on the cellular processes involved in energy regulation.

These areas are not all at the same stage of research, but they help explain why infrared and photobiomodulation have become such active areas of scientific interest.

## How can we experience infrared?

Sunlight is one of the most accessible natural sources of infrared. Spending time outdoors during the day exposes us to a broad spectrum of light, including visible light and infrared.

Morning and evening daylight can also be useful ways to build a consistent daily light routine. As the sun changes position, the balance of wavelengths reaching us changes too. Getting outside regularly supports our body's relationship with natural light and its daily rhythms.

**A note on sun safety:** Enjoy natural daylight without seeking intense sun exposure. Avoid prolonged exposure when UV levels are high, protect your skin with shade, clothing and sunscreen as appropriate, and take care not to get sunburnt. Never look directly at the sun. Infrared exposure is not a reason to increase UV exposure, and it does not replace established sun protection.

Infrared saunas and heat lamps provide another form of exposure, primarily through warmth. Red and near-infrared light devices are different: they are designed to deliver more specific wavelengths used in photobiomodulation research.

If using a red or near-infrared device, it is worth checking the wavelength, output and recommended exposure time. Devices vary considerably, and following the manufacturer's instructions, including any eye-protection guidance, is important.

## Learn more: what is happening inside our cells?

### 1. Mitochondria and cellular energy

Mitochondria are essential to how our bodies produce and use energy. Through a process called oxidative phosphorylation, they help convert energy from food into ATP.

But their role goes beyond energy production. Mitochondria also help regulate calcium levels, cellular signalling and the body's responses to oxidative stress.

Because these functions are so central to cell health, researchers are interested in whether light could influence mitochondrial activity and, in turn, support processes involved in repair and healthy ageing.

### 2. How does red and near-infrared light interact with mitochondria?

One of the leading explanations involves an enzyme in the mitochondrial energy-producing system called cytochrome c oxidase.

This enzyme plays an important role in the electron transport chain, which helps generate ATP. Laboratory research suggests that certain wavelengths of red and near-infrared light can interact with this enzyme.

Light exposure may influence how efficiently the system works, as well as the availability of nitric oxide and the production of reactive oxygen species. Although these molecules are often discussed in the context of stress, they also act as important signals within cells.

This is one proposed explanation for how photobiomodulation may influence cellular energy, signalling and repair.

The response depends on factors such as wavelength, intensity and exposure time. More light is not necessarily better, and different protocols can produce different effects.

### 3. What does the clinical research show?

Photobiomodulation is being investigated across a growing range of health applications. Research has explored its use in wound healing, pain management, skin health, hair growth and some neurological and musculoskeletal conditions.

There is encouraging clinical evidence for certain specific applications. For example, research and expert consensus support its potential use in some types of peripheral neuropathy, wound healing and androgenetic hair loss.

Other areas, including recovery, cognitive function and wider metabolic health, are still being explored. The results are interesting, but more research is needed to understand which treatments work best, for whom, and under what conditions.

### 4. Infrared in our natural environment

Infrared is a normal part of the sunlight spectrum, and our bodies are exposed to it whenever we spend time outdoors.

Researchers are also investigating whether near-infrared exposure can influence how skin responds to subsequent environmental stress. This includes an area of research sometimes described as photopreconditioning.

It is an interesting area, but it should not be confused with sun protection. Infrared exposure has not been shown to replace sunscreen, shade or other measures that protect against ultraviolet radiation.

### 5. Modern lighting and our daily rhythms

Our modern lighting environment is not simply a question of how much infrared we receive. The timing, intensity and spectrum of light all matter.

Daylight provides a strong signal to the circadian system, helping the body distinguish day from night. Indoor lighting and screens can provide a different pattern of exposure, particularly when we spend much of the day indoors and use bright screens in the evening.

Getting natural daylight during the day and keeping evenings less brightly lit can help support a healthy sleep-wake rhythm. This is a practical benefit of reconnecting with natural light, quite apart from the emerging research into infrared.

### 6. Could infrared influence metabolic health?

Mitochondria are closely connected to metabolism because they help cells use energy. This has led researchers to explore whether photobiomodulation might influence metabolic processes.

There are promising mechanisms and early findings, but we do not yet have strong evidence that infrared exposure improves whole-body metabolism, prevents metabolic disease or extends lifespan.

It is an exciting area for future research, particularly as we learn more about the relationship between light, mitochondria and cellular health.

## What is established, and what is still emerging?

Infrared and photobiomodulation research is developing quickly. It is useful to separate the areas where clinical evidence is encouraging from the broader possibilities that scientists are still investigating.

**Where there is encouraging clinical support:**
- Certain wound-healing applications.
- Some types of pain and peripheral neuropathy.
- Androgenetic hair loss.
- Selected outcomes involving fatigue and physical function.

**Where the research is promising but still developing:**
- Improving mitochondrial function and cellular energy in otherwise healthy people.
- Wider benefits for whole-body metabolism.
- Brain health, cognition and age-related decline.
- Disease prevention and longevity.
- Additional health benefits from specific patterns of natural infrared exposure, such as morning or evening sunlight.

The evidence varies considerably between applications, and the findings from targeted red-light treatments cannot automatically be applied to every source of infrared.

## The bigger picture

Light is more than something we see. It is part of the environment our biology evolved alongside, and researchers are only beginning to understand the many ways it may interact with our cells.

Infrared is particularly interesting because of its potential relationship with mitochondria, cellular energy and repair. While some applications of photobiomodulation already have encouraging clinical evidence, the wider possibilities for metabolic health and healthy ageing remain areas of active research.

In the meantime, getting outside, enjoying natural daylight safely and building a healthier relationship with light throughout the day are simple ways to reconnect with an important part of our natural environment.

# Intermittent Fasting



## Giving Your Body a Little More Structure

Most conversations about nutrition focus on what we eat. Protein, fibre, vitamins and food quality all matter, but there is another question worth considering: how long do we spread our eating across the day?

For many of us, food features from early morning until late evening. Breakfast, lunch, snacks, dinner and perhaps something in front of the television.

One simple idea attracting growing scientific interest is **shortening our daily eating window**. Rather than changing what we eat, it involves bringing meals into a more consistent part of the day and allowing a longer break overnight.

So, could when we eat be just as interesting as what we eat?

## What's Supported by Human Research?

Research into time-restricted eating has explored whether keeping meals within a consistent daily window can influence health.

Human studies suggest it may help some people:

- **Support weight management:** A shorter eating window can reduce opportunities for habitual snacking and may help some people naturally consume fewer calories.
- **Improve metabolic markers:** Some studies have found improvements in blood glucose, insulin sensitivity and blood pressure, particularly in people with metabolic risk factors.
- **Create more consistent eating habits:** Having a clearer start and finish to the day's eating can help reduce unplanned eating, particularly late in the evening.

The ZOE Big IF Study, involving more than 148,000 participants, explored a 10-hour eating window. Participants reported improvements in energy, mood, hunger and weight. It provides an interesting insight into everyday eating habits, although it cannot prove that the shorter eating window caused those changes.

It's worth noting that results vary, and time-restricted eating isn't consistently more effective than other approaches to improving diet or managing weight.

## What's Emerging? The Science Behind Meal Timing

Beyond weight and metabolic health, researchers are exploring how the timing of meals influences the body's internal processes. Three areas are particularly interesting.

### 1. Metabolism and Energy Use

After eating, the body uses nutrients from food for energy and stores some for later. As time passes between meals, insulin levels generally fall and the body gradually draws more on stored energy, including fat.

During longer periods without food, the liver can also produce **ketones** – an alternative fuel source that the brain and other tissues can use.

**The science deep dive**

Ketones aren't just an energy source. One called beta-hydroxybutyrate is also involved in cellular signalling and may influence pathways linked to inflammation and how cells respond to stress.

This is one reason researchers are interested in the effects of meal timing. However, many of these wider effects are still being investigated, and laboratory findings don't automatically translate into proven health benefits.

### 2. Autophagy: The Cell's Recycling System

One of the more fascinating areas of research is autophagy.

This is the body's natural cellular recycling process. Cells break down damaged or unnecessary components and reuse some of the materials, helping to maintain normal cell function.

**The science deep dive**

Autophagy is influenced by several cellular signalling pathways, including one called mTOR. When nutrients are plentiful, mTOR helps coordinate cell growth and protein production. Changes in nutrient availability can influence these signals and the processes involved in cellular maintenance.

This has attracted interest in healthy ageing research. However, we still don't know exactly how much simply shortening an eating window affects autophagy in humans, or whether this leads to meaningful long-term health benefits.

### 3. Your Body Clock: Why Eating Earlier May Matter

Our bodies operate on internal 24-hour rhythms, known as circadian rhythms. These influence sleep, hormones, digestion and metabolism.

Interestingly, our ability to manage blood glucose tends to be better earlier in the day. This has led researchers to investigate whether eating earlier, rather than late into the evening, may support metabolic health.

**The science deep dive**

This field is known as *chrononutrition* – the study of how meal timing interacts with our biological clocks.

Early research suggests that aligning eating patterns more closely with these natural rhythms may support glucose regulation and metabolic health. It is an exciting area, although the ideal eating window and its long-term effects are still being studied.

## A Simple Change Worth Exploring

This doesn't need to involve a strict eating schedule or major dietary changes.

For some people, it may simply mean:
- Keeping meal times relatively consistent.
- Avoiding habitual late-night snacking.
- Allowing a little more time between the evening meal and breakfast.
- Paying attention to whether eating late affects comfort, digestion or sleep.

The aim isn't to eat as little as possible or ignore hunger. It's to find a natural daily rhythm that allows enough time for nourishing meals while reducing constant grazing.

## The Takeaway

Nutrition isn't only about what we eat. The timing and pattern of our meals may also influence metabolic health and our internal body clock.

Human research suggests that a more defined eating window may benefit some measures of metabolic health, while emerging research into cellular maintenance and circadian rhythms offers some fascinating possibilities.

Perhaps a useful starting point isn't a complicated diet or strict routine, but simply becoming more aware of when we eat and whether our bodies benefit from a little more structure.

# Mental Wellbeing

*Our mental wellbeing is closely connected to our physical health. Discover how everyday habits — from quality sleep and nourishing food to movement, daylight and human connection — can support a healthier mind and body.*

## Mental Wellbeing Starts With the Whole Body

When we think about mental wellbeing, we often focus on our thoughts, emotions and mindset. But our brains don't operate independently from the rest of the body. They are constantly responding to signals from our nervous system, hormones, immune system, gut and surrounding environment.

Sleep, nutrition, movement, daylight and relationships all play a part in how we feel, think and respond to stress.

There is no single supplement, breathing technique or wellness routine that can replace these foundations. Sometimes, supporting mental wellbeing means returning to the basics and giving our bodies what they need to function well.

## The Foundations of Mental Wellbeing

### 1. Prioritise Quality Sleep

Sleep is one of the most important foundations of mental and physical health. During sleep, the brain processes information, consolidates memories and regulates emotional responses. Poor or insufficient sleep can affect concentration, mood, motivation and our ability to manage stress.

**What could help?**
- Aim for a consistent sleep and wake time.
- Get natural daylight during the morning.
- Reduce bright light and screen use towards bedtime.
- Keep your bedroom cool, dark and comfortable.
- Be mindful of caffeine and alcohol, which can disrupt sleep.

Good sleep isn't simply about the number of hours spent in bed. Quality, consistency and how refreshed you feel also matter.

### 2. Disconnect From Devices and Make Space for Stillness

We are constantly exposed to information, notifications and stimulation. Even when we're relaxing, we may be scrolling, watching or listening to something.

When was the last time you genuinely did nothing?

The Italian expression *il dolce far niente* translates as the sweetness of doing nothing. It captures the value of allowing ourselves to pause without feeling the need to be productive.

Quiet moments can give us space to reflect, daydream and process experiences. Taking a walk without your phone, sitting with a cup of tea or simply looking out of the window can offer a welcome break from constant stimulation.

It isn't about avoiding technology altogether, but creating a healthier relationship with it.

### 3. Nourish Your Brain With Good Food

The brain is an energy-demanding organ, relying on a steady supply of nutrients to support its structure and function. A varied diet containing vegetables, fruit, whole grains, quality protein, healthy fats, nuts and seeds provides many of the nutrients involved in brain health.

Omega-3 fatty acids are particularly interesting because they contribute to the structure of brain cell membranes and are involved in cell signalling. Oily fish, such as salmon, sardines and mackerel, are useful dietary sources.

You can explore this in more detail in our article on [Omega-3 and its role in health](https://wellnous.lovable.app/articles/omega-3).

Rather than focusing on individual superfoods or supplements, the overall quality and variety of your diet are a good place to start.

### 4. Look After Your Gut

The gut and brain are in constant communication through what is known as the gut-brain axis. This involves neural pathways, immune signals, hormones and chemicals produced or modified by gut microbes.

The gut microbiome — the community of microorganisms living in our digestive system — is an increasingly interesting area of mental health research.

Eating a varied, fibre-rich diet can help support a diverse gut microbiome. Fermented foods such as live-culture yoghurt, kefir, kimchi and sauerkraut can also add variety.

These foods are not treatments for anxiety or low mood, but they can form part of a dietary pattern that supports general health.

### 5. Get Outside and Enjoy Natural Daylight

Light does much more than help us see. Natural daylight helps regulate our circadian rhythm — the internal body clock that influences sleep, hormones, alertness and other biological processes.

Getting outside during the day, particularly in the morning, helps provide a strong timing signal to the body. It can also encourage movement and give us a break from indoor environments.

Sunlight also enables the skin to produce vitamin D, which plays important roles in bone health, muscle function and immune regulation. Vitamin D receptors are found in the brain, and researchers are investigating possible links between vitamin D status and mental health.

However, evidence that vitamin D supplements improve mood in people who already have adequate levels remains mixed. Correcting a deficiency is important for health, but taking more is not necessarily better.

Enjoy daylight sensibly, without seeking excessive sun exposure or allowing your skin to burn.

### 6. Move Your Body

Exercise is one of the most well-established ways to support both physical and mental wellbeing.

Movement can influence mood, sleep, stress regulation and brain function. Physical activity is also associated with better cognitive health over time.

It doesn't need to mean intense training. Walking, cycling, swimming, strength training, yoga or dancing all offer ways to move.

The most useful activity is often the one you enjoy and can maintain. A combination of cardiovascular movement, strength work and activities that challenge balance and coordination can support different aspects of health.

### 7. Make Time for Human Connection

Mental wellbeing isn't just an individual pursuit. Our relationships and sense of belonging are important parts of health.

Spending time with friends, family and community can provide emotional support, shared experiences and a sense of purpose. Even small interactions — a conversation with a neighbour, meeting a friend for a walk or sharing a meal — can help us feel more connected.

In a world where so much communication happens through screens, making time for face-to-face connection can be especially valuable.

## Deep Dive: The Science Behind Mental Wellbeing

The foundations above may seem simple, but they influence some of the body's most important biological systems.

### The Gut-Brain Connection

The gut communicates with the brain through several interconnected pathways. The vagus nerve provides a direct neural link, while immune signals and microbial metabolites also contribute to communication between the two.

Gut bacteria can produce substances such as short-chain fatty acids from dietary fibre. These compounds are involved in gut health, immune regulation and metabolic signalling.

Researchers are exploring how changes in the microbiome may relate to stress, mood and neurological function. The field is promising, but it is still developing: we don't yet know whether specific foods or probiotic supplements can reliably improve mental health.

### Omega-3 and Brain Cell Function

The omega-3 fatty acid DHA is an important structural component of brain cell membranes. EPA and DHA are also involved in signalling pathways associated with inflammation and cell function.

Research has investigated omega-3 supplementation in relation to mood, cognition and brain health. Some findings are encouraging in particular groups, but results vary depending on the population, dose and type of omega-3 used.

Eating oily fish regularly can help provide these essential fats. Supplementation may be worth exploring in some circumstances, but it isn't a universal solution for mental wellbeing.

### Sleep, Stress and the Nervous System

The nervous system continually balances alertness and recovery. The sympathetic nervous system helps us respond to demands, while parasympathetic activity supports rest and recovery.

Sleep, breathing practices and physical activity can all influence this balance. Chronic sleep disruption and prolonged stress can make emotional regulation more difficult, while restorative routines may help us recover.

Slow breathing, for example, can influence heart rate variability (HRV), the natural variation in time between heartbeats. This is one reason breathing exercises are being studied for their potential role in stress management.

## What About Natural Supplements?

Once the foundations of good sleep, nutrition, movement and daily routines are in place, there may be some natural supplements worth exploring.

Nutrients such as omega-3 and vitamin D, for example, may be relevant where dietary intake is low or a deficiency exists. Other compounds, including certain probiotics and herbal preparations, are being researched for their potential effects on stress, mood and cognitive health.

The evidence varies considerably, and a supplement that may help one person isn't necessarily appropriate or beneficial for another. More isn't always better, and natural doesn't automatically mean safe.

The best approach is to understand what you're taking, why you're taking it and whether there is good evidence to support its use.

## Bringing It All Together

Mental wellbeing isn't built through one perfect routine or a collection of supplements. It develops through the interaction of many everyday factors: how we sleep, what we eat, how often we move, the light we experience, the time we spend away from screens and the relationships we maintain.

The science of the gut-brain axis, omega-3 and the nervous system offers fascinating insights into how the body and brain work together. But these areas should complement, rather than distract from, the foundations.

Perhaps the most useful place to start is also the simplest: get outside, move regularly, eat well, protect your sleep, make time for people you care about and allow yourself moments to do nothing.

Sometimes, going back to basics is one of the most meaningful ways to look after your mind.

# Mitochondrial Health

#### How mitochondrial health affects energy – and everyday habits that may support it

Mitochondria are often described as the powerhouses of our cells. But their role goes far beyond simply producing energy. They influence inflammation, cellular repair, metabolism, ageing and even whether a cell survives or dies.

I am passionate about mitochondrial health because I believe it is one of the most important, yet often overlooked, foundations of long-term health.

Most human cells contain mitochondria, although their number varies with the cell’s energy needs (mature red blood cells are an exception). These tiny structures convert energy from the food we eat into ATP – the energy currency that powers almost every cellular function.

When mitochondria work efficiently, our cells have the energy they need to function, repair and adapt. When their performance deteriorates, energy production can decline, oxidative stress and inflammation can increase, and cellular repair becomes less efficient.

Changes in mitochondrial function have been observed in many chronic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders and cancer. They may contribute to disease, result from it, or be part of the body’s response to stress.

The encouraging news is that mitochondria are not static. They constantly adapt, repair, divide and renew themselves. This means our everyday lifestyle choices can influence how well they function throughout life.

Let's explore why this matters, what can put mitochondria under strain and what may help support them.

---

## Part 1 – The Big Picture
### Why should we care about our mitochondria?

Think of mitochondria as the energy generators inside our cells.

Our heart needs enormous amounts of energy to keep beating. Our brain requires a constant supply to support thinking, memory and communication. Our muscles need energy to move, and virtually every tissue relies on ATP to maintain and repair itself.

But mitochondria do much more than generate energy.

They also help to:

- Regulate inflammation and cellular signalling.
- Maintain calcium balance within cells.
- Support antioxidant defences.
- Control programmed cell death, helping remove damaged or abnormal cells.
- Support cellular repair, adaptation and renewal.

In other words, healthy mitochondria are essential not only for energy, but also for maintaining healthy tissues and supporting the body's ability to respond to stress.

When mitochondrial function becomes compromised, the effects can extend far beyond feeling tired. It may influence metabolic health, inflammation, ageing and our vulnerability to chronic disease.

This is why I believe mitochondrial health deserves far more attention.

## Part 2 – What Is Going Wrong?
### Modern pressures on mitochondria

Our mitochondria evolved in an environment very different from the one we live in today.

Modern lifestyles can place them under continuous pressure. Excess calorie intake, poor metabolic health, environmental pollutants, chronic psychological stress, insufficient sleep and certain medications can all contribute to mitochondrial stress.

Over time, multiple small stressors can accumulate, reducing mitochondrial efficiency and resilience.

#### The main pressures on mitochondrial health

**1. Poor metabolic health**

Excess calorie intake, frequent consumption of highly processed foods and impaired metabolic health can place additional demands on the body's energy systems.

**2. Environmental pollution**

Air pollution and particulate matter may increase mitochondrial oxidative stress and inflammation.

**3. Heavy metals and chemicals**

Exposure to heavy metals such as mercury, lead and cadmium can interfere with mitochondrial enzymes and energy metabolism. Certain environmental chemicals may also disrupt mitochondrial signalling.

**4. Chronic psychological stress**

Persistent stress and elevated cortisol can impair mitochondrial function and increase oxidative damage.

**5. Insufficient sleep**

Poor sleep reduces the body's opportunity to repair and renew mitochondria and replenish antioxidant defences.

**6. Alcohol and certain medications**

Some medications, alcohol and environmental chemicals can increase mitochondrial stress and reduce ATP production.

**7. Endocrine-disrupting chemicals**

Chemicals found in plastics and industrial compounds may negatively influence mitochondrial signalling and metabolism.

**8. Electromagnetic fields and radiofrequency radiation**

There is a growing body of research exploring whether electromagnetic fields and radiofrequency radiation from mobile phones, WiFi networks, power lines and cellular infrastructure may influence mitochondrial function.

These are emerging areas of research, and some proposed mechanisms have yet to be confirmed in human studies.

The important point is that these stressors do not necessarily act in isolation. Individually, their effects may be modest, but together they may create a cumulative burden that gradually reduces mitochondrial efficiency.

### Why this matters for chronic disease

Mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly being investigated in relation to a wide range of chronic health conditions.

As mitochondrial performance declines, ATP production may fall, oxidative stress can increase, inflammation can become more difficult to regulate and cellular repair processes may become less efficient.

This creates an environment in which healthy tissues may become progressively more vulnerable to damage.

Cancer is one particularly interesting area of mitochondrial research, which we will explore in more detail in Part 4.

## Part 4 – The Science Deep Dive
### For those who want to understand what is happening inside the cell

So far, we've looked at why mitochondrial health matters and what we can do to support it.

But what is actually happening inside these tiny cellular structures?

Let's take a closer look.

#### How mitochondria produce energy

Inside mitochondria is a sophisticated biological energy system called the electron transport chain.

It extracts energy from food molecules and uses this energy to pump protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane.

This creates an electrical and chemical gradient – rather like charging a biological battery.

The stored energy is then used by an enzyme called ATP synthase.

ATP synthase works much like a tiny molecular turbine. As protons flow back across the membrane, it uses that energy to produce ATP.

This is how mitochondria generate the energy that powers almost every function in our bodies.

This system produces ATP and naturally generates reactive oxygen species, which also have normal signalling roles; problems arise when oxidative stress outpaces the body’s ability to regulate it.

However, when the electron transport chain becomes impaired, energy production can become less efficient and the generation of reactive oxygen species can increase.

#### Oxidative stress: when energy production becomes a problem

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are naturally produced during mitochondrial energy metabolism.

At normal levels, they are not simply harmful waste products. They also play important roles in cellular signalling and adaptation.

The problem arises when ROS production becomes excessive relative to the body's antioxidant defences.

Under normal circumstances, antioxidant systems neutralise excess reactive oxygen species.

However, chronic metabolic and environmental stress can overwhelm these defences.

This can create a damaging cycle:

- Mitochondrial stress increases ROS production.
- Excess ROS damages mitochondrial components.
- Damaged mitochondria produce energy less efficiently.
- Further ROS production and inflammation may follow.
- The cycle can progressively compromise cellular function.

Over time, this may reduce ATP production and the cell's ability to repair and maintain itself.

This is one of the reasons mitochondrial health is so important to the wider picture of ageing and chronic disease.

#### Two different mitochondrial priorities

An interesting aspect of mitochondrial biology is that mitochondria can support different metabolic priorities depending on what a cell needs.

Two broad functional states can be described:

**Energetic mitochondria**

These prioritise ATP production and are particularly important in tissues with high energy demands, including:

- The heart.
- The brain.
- Skeletal muscles.
- The retina.

**Biosynthetic mitochondria**

These prioritise the production of cellular building blocks, including amino acids, nucleotides and lipids.

These compounds are essential for cell growth, proliferation and tissue repair.

They generate less ATP relative to their biosynthetic activity but play an important role in supporting cellular growth and renewal.

In healthy tissues, cells can move between these metabolic priorities according to their needs.

When mitochondrial health deteriorates, however, this balance can become disrupted, contributing to wider metabolic dysfunction.

#### Cancer and mitochondrial dysfunction: the Warburg effect

One of the most fascinating areas of mitochondrial research is its relationship with cancer.

In 1924, biochemist Otto Warburg observed that many cancer cells rely heavily on glycolysis for energy production, even when oxygen is available.

This phenomenon is now known as the **Warburg Effect**.

At first glance, this seems rather inefficient.

Glycolysis produces only around 2 ATP molecules per glucose molecule, whereas complete mitochondrial oxidation can produce more than 30.

So why would cancer cells favour a seemingly less efficient energy pathway?

One explanation is that cancer cells have altered metabolic requirements.

Changes in mitochondrial function can impair normal oxidative metabolism, encouraging greater reliance on glycolysis.

However, glycolysis also provides something else that rapidly dividing cancer cells need: the molecular building blocks required to manufacture new cells.

These include components used to make DNA, proteins and cell membranes.

In this way, the metabolic shift may support rapid growth and division, even if ATP production per molecule of glucose is lower.

**Mitochondria and programmed cell death**

Mitochondria also play a critical role in apoptosis – the programmed cell death process that removes damaged or abnormal cells.

Healthy mitochondria help regulate the signals that determine whether a cell should be repaired or eliminated.

When mitochondrial signalling becomes impaired, abnormal cells may be better able to evade these quality-control mechanisms and survive longer than they should.

This has important implications for cancer biology.

It does not mean mitochondrial dysfunction is the sole cause of cancer. Cancer is a complex disease involving genetic mutations, immune dysfunction, environmental exposures, inflammation and altered cellular metabolism.

However, mitochondrial dysfunction appears to be an important contributor that can create conditions favourable to cancer development, progression and resistance to treatment.

Viewed through this lens, cancer can be understood partly as a disease involving disrupted cellular energy and signalling.

Genetic mutations undoubtedly matter, but the metabolic environment in which those mutations occur may also be important.

Mitochondrial dysfunction may therefore help explain why chronic inflammation, obesity, insulin resistance, environmental toxins and ageing are all associated with increased cancer risk, despite arising from very different origins.

I find this an especially exciting area of research, and I look forward to seeing how our understanding develops through further human studies.

#### Mitophagy: the importance of removing damaged mitochondria

Mitochondria are constantly changing.

They divide, fuse, adapt to the energy demands of the cell and undergo renewal.

But what happens when a mitochondrion becomes damaged?

This is where mitophagy comes in.

Mitophagy is the selective removal of damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria through the cell's recycling systems.

Rather than allowing inefficient mitochondria to accumulate, the cell identifies and removes them so their components can be recycled.

This is an essential part of maintaining a healthy mitochondrial network.

Exercise, periods of fasting and other metabolic signals can influence the pathways involved in mitochondrial quality control.

Sleep also plays an important role in the wider processes of cellular repair and renewal.

**Where does urolithin A fit in?**

Urolithin A is a compound produced when certain gut bacteria metabolise plant compounds called ellagitannins, found in foods such as pomegranates and walnuts.

It has attracted scientific interest because of its potential to activate pathways involved in mitophagy and mitochondrial function.

By potentially supporting the removal of damaged mitochondria and encouraging mitochondrial renewal, urolithin A may offer another avenue for supporting cellular health and healthy ageing.

The broader principle is important: mitochondrial health isn't simply about producing more energy. It is also about maintaining a healthy population of functioning mitochondria.

## The Bigger Picture
### Protecting our cellular energy for long-term health

Mitochondria sit at the centre of cellular energy production, repair, adaptation and survival.

Modern life exposes them to a wide range of pressures: excess calories, sedentary behaviour, pollution, chronic stress, sleep disruption, environmental toxins and potentially other emerging stressors.

Individually, these challenges may seem relatively small. Together, they can create a cumulative burden that gradually compromises cellular energy production and resilience.

As mitochondrial function declines, ATP production may fall, oxidative stress can rise, inflammation may increase and the body's ability to maintain healthy tissues can become compromised.

Over time, this dysfunction may contribute to many of the chronic diseases associated with ageing.

But there is reason to be optimistic.

Mitochondria are dynamic. They constantly adapt, repair, divide and renew themselves.

Through better nutrition, regular exercise, restorative sleep, stress management, sensible toxin reduction, natural light exposure and support for mitochondrial quality-control processes, we may be able to maintain healthier and more efficient mitochondrial networks throughout life.

Rather than searching for a single cure or miracle supplement, I believe the most powerful approach is to reduce the total burden placed on our mitochondria while supporting the body's natural capacity for repair and renewal.

**Ultimately, protecting our mitochondria means protecting the energy systems that allow our cells – and therefore our bodies – to function, recover and thrive.**

# Omega 3

*3 min read*

Omega-3 fatty acids are essential nutrients that play an important role in the health of the brain, heart and wider body. They contribute to the structure of our cells and help regulate processes involved in inflammation, circulation and normal nervous system function.

Despite their importance, many people don't get enough omega-3 through their diet. Understanding the different types, how they work and where to find them can help us make more informed choices about supporting long-term health.

## Understanding the Different Types of Omega-3

There are three main types of omega-3 fatty acids:

- **EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid):** involved in cell signalling and the regulation of inflammatory processes.
- **DHA (docosahexaenoic acid):** an important structural component of cell membranes, particularly in the brain and retina.
- **ALA (alpha-linolenic acid):** found mainly in plant foods. It is essential in the diet, although the body converts only small amounts into EPA and DHA.

EPA and DHA are found primarily in oily fish and marine sources, while ALA is found in foods such as flaxseeds, chia seeds and walnuts.

## Health Benefits: Supporting the Body

Omega-3s contribute to several important physiological processes:

- **Heart health:** EPA and DHA can help lower blood triglyceride levels, particularly at higher doses. Their effects on wider cardiovascular health vary depending on the individual and the preparation used.
- **Inflammation:** They help regulate the production of signalling molecules involved in the body's inflammatory response and its natural resolution.
- **Brain and nervous system:** DHA is highly concentrated in the brain and supports the structure and normal function of nerve cell membranes.
- **Eye health:** DHA is an important component of the retina and contributes to normal visual function.
- **Joint health:** Some studies suggest omega-3 supplementation may modestly improve joint discomfort and stiffness, particularly in people with rheumatoid arthritis, although results vary.

## Omega-3: Supporting the Body's Natural Inflammatory Response

Inflammation is a natural and essential part of the body's defence and repair systems. It helps us respond to injury, infection and physical stress. Problems can arise when inflammatory activity becomes excessive or persists for too long.

EPA and DHA play an interesting role here. Rather than simply switching inflammation off, they help regulate the body's response and support the processes involved in bringing it towards resolution.

They do this in several ways:

- **Regulating inflammatory signals:** EPA and DHA influence the production of signalling molecules called eicosanoids, which help coordinate inflammatory responses.
- **Supporting inflammation resolution:** They are used to produce specialised compounds called resolvins, protectins and maresins. These help coordinate the body's natural processes for resolving inflammation.
- **Influencing cell membranes:** By becoming incorporated into cell membranes, omega-3s can change how cells respond to inflammatory signals.
- **Supporting immune regulation:** They help regulate aspects of immune cell activity and the balance of inflammatory responses.

This is particularly interesting because persistent, low-grade inflammation is being investigated in relation to a range of long-term health conditions.

Clinical research has found benefits in certain areas, including reduced triglyceride levels and modest improvements in symptoms associated with rheumatoid arthritis. Some studies have also found reductions in inflammatory markers, although results are mixed and depend on the population and dose studied.

Omega-3 is therefore better understood as an inflammation-modulating nutrient than a general-purpose anti-inflammatory treatment.

## How Omega-3s Work at a Cellular Level

Omega-3s do more than provide energy. They become incorporated into the membranes surrounding our cells, helping maintain the structure and flexibility needed for cells to function and communicate.

DHA is particularly important in the nervous system, where it contributes to the membranes of brain cells and supports normal neural function. EPA and DHA also influence the production of specialised molecules involved in regulating inflammation.

These processes help explain why omega-3s are considered important for maintaining healthy tissues and supporting normal cellular activity throughout the body.

However, getting enough omega-3 to support normal function does not mean that taking additional supplements will necessarily provide extra benefits if dietary intake is already adequate.

## Best Dietary Sources

Oily fish is one of the richest dietary sources of EPA and DHA. Useful options include:

- Salmon
- Mackerel
- Sardines
- Herring
- Anchovies

Plant foods such as flaxseeds, chia seeds and walnuts provide ALA. Although the body can convert some ALA into EPA and DHA, this conversion is limited.

For those who don't eat fish, algae-based supplements can provide a direct source of EPA and DHA.

A varied, balanced diet is generally a good starting point. Whether supplementation is worthwhile depends on dietary intake, individual needs and health circumstances.

## Should You Consider a Supplement?

Omega-3 supplements can be useful for people who don't regularly eat oily fish or have a specific clinical reason to increase their intake. However, more is not necessarily better, and different products can vary considerably in their EPA and DHA content.

High-dose omega-3 preparations are sometimes used under medical supervision to manage elevated triglycerides. These are different from ordinary over-the-counter fish oil supplements.

**Always speak with a doctor or pharmacist before starting a new supplement**, particularly if you take medication, have an existing health condition or are considering higher doses. Omega-3 supplements can interact with some medicines, including anticoagulants, and may increase bleeding risk in certain circumstances.

# Physical Wellbeing

#### How regular movement and muscular strength can support healthy ageing, everyday independence and metabolic health.

## Healthspan Over Lifespan

Living longer matters, but so does how we live those years. **Healthspan** describes the years spent in good health, with the capacity to do the things that matter to us. Maintaining strength, balance and mobility can help support everyday independence as we grow older, although no routine can guarantee freedom from illness or disability.

Movement is not a punishment or a test of how hard we can push ourselves. It is one way of caring for our bodies over time. Regular activity is associated with healthier ageing, and well-designed exercise programmes can improve strength and physical function. Benefits depend on the activity, the person and their circumstances; movement is one part of health alongside nutrition, rest, relationships and appropriate healthcare.

## Understanding Sarcopenia

Muscle mass and strength often decline with age, with considerable variation between people. **Sarcopenia** is a clinical condition involving low muscle strength, with reduced muscle quantity or quality used to confirm the diagnosis; it is not simply another name for every age-related change in muscle.

Its causes are multifactorial. Ageing biology, reduced activity, illness and inadequate nutrition can all contribute. It is not solely a consequence of inactivity, and developing it is not a personal failure.

Muscular strength helps us rise from a chair, carry everyday items and move with greater control. Resistance training, adapted to the individual, can improve strength and function in older adults and is an important part of sarcopenia management. Muscles can remain responsive to training later in life, but the amount of improvement and the safest starting point differ from person to person.

## Muscle Is More Than Movement

Skeletal muscle is an active **metabolic and signalling organ**, not just tissue that moves our joints. It uses and stores fuel and communicates with other tissues through signalling molecules, including myokines.

- **Everyday capability:** Maintaining strength supports practical tasks and can contribute to balance and mobility. Fall prevention generally benefits from a combination of balance, functional and strength work tailored to the person.
- **Metabolic health:** Active muscle uses glucose and other fuels. Regular physical activity can improve insulin sensitivity, although responses vary and exercise does not replace medical treatment.
- **Communication between tissues:** Muscle-derived signals are being studied for their roles in metabolism and inflammatory regulation. Much of the detail comes from laboratory and mechanistic research; it should not be translated into promises about immunity or brain health.

## Muscle, Mitochondria and ATP

Muscle contraction requires **ATP**, the molecule cells use to transfer energy. Muscles do not simply hold a large permanent store of ATP: stored ATP is limited and must be continually replenished as energy demand changes.

Mitochondria help regenerate ATP using fuels such as fats and carbohydrates. Other energy pathways also contribute, particularly during brief or demanding activity. Muscle mitochondrial content varies with muscle fibre type, training and health; skeletal muscle is not uniquely the body's most mitochondria-rich tissue.

Regular training can improve the ability of muscle to produce and use energy. Endurance and resistance training have different, sometimes overlapping adaptations. This helps explain why varied, appropriate movement can be useful, rather than making one type of exercise a universal answer to fatigue.

## Building Up Safely from Where You Are

There is no single starting point that suits everyone. Someone returning after illness or living with pain may need a very different approach from someone already active. Progress should fit your current capacity rather than a fixed target or someone else's routine.

> **Before beginning:** Consult an appropriately qualified healthcare or exercise professional to choose activity that suits your health and abilities. Seek hands-on guidance before starting resistance or unfamiliar exercise. Begin at a manageable level and increase gradually; stop and seek advice for pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness or other concerning symptoms.

- **Start with what is manageable:** For some people, this may mean a brief, comfortable walk or breaking up long periods of sitting. Others may need supported or seated movement chosen with professional guidance. These are possibilities, not requirements.
- **Build strength with support:** Bodyweight, resistance bands, machines, dumbbells and kettlebells are possible tools. Compound movements involve several joints and muscle groups together, but no tool or movement is essential. Both compound and more targeted exercises can have a place in an individual programme.
- **Change gradually:** Increase duration, frequency or resistance in small, manageable steps, with time to assess how you feel and recover. There is no need to rush into heavier loads or dynamic movements.
- **Allow for recovery:** Rest, sleep and adequate nutrition matter. Persistent pain, excessive fatigue or worsening symptoms are reasons to pause and seek advice, not signs that you need to push harder.

Consistency at an appropriate level matters more than proving intensity. The aim is to preserve and develop capacity for daily life, safely and sustainably.

## Deep Dive: Muscle as a Glucose Sink

Skeletal muscle is a major site of glucose uptake and storage, which is why it is sometimes described as a **glucose sink**. Glucose can be used to generate energy or stored as glycogen for later use.

Muscle accounts for a large share of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal in research settings. Frequently quoted figures of around 70–80% refer to particular experimental conditions, not a fixed percentage of every meal's glucose in every person. The liver and other tissues also play important roles in blood glucose regulation.

**At rest**, insulin signalling helps move GLUT4 glucose transporters towards the muscle-cell surface, allowing more glucose to enter the cell. This is a regulated process, not an all-or-nothing lock.

**During contraction**, muscle also activates signalling pathways that can increase GLUT4 transport to the cell surface independently of insulin signalling. Changes in calcium and energy-sensing pathways, including AMPK, contribute; the mechanisms are complex and not explained by one switch alone.

> Muscle contraction can increase glucose uptake through pathways that do not require insulin signalling. This does not mean insulin becomes unnecessary, nor that muscle is the only tissue capable of taking up glucose without insulin: the brain and red blood cells, among others, also use insulin-independent transport mechanisms.

Studies suggest that breaking up prolonged sitting or doing light activity after meals can reduce post-meal glucose rises in some people. Effects depend on the person, timing and activity. This is not a guaranteed blood-sugar treatment, and it does not replace prescribed medicines.

If you use insulin or medicines that can cause low blood glucose, activity can change your glucose levels and treatment needs. Discuss changes in activity with your healthcare team rather than adjusting medicines yourself.

# Polyphenols

#### The Colourful Side of Nutrition

Spend enough time reading about nutrition and you start to notice a pattern. The conversation almost always revolves around a handful of familiar topics. Protein gets discussed endlessly. Carbohydrates are either praised or demonised depending on the latest trend. Fibre is finally receiving some of the recognition it deserves. Even vitamins and minerals, despite being discovered over a century ago, still dominate health headlines and supplement advertisements.

Yet there's a fascinating omission in all of this.

Many of the foods most consistently associated with long-term health benefits contain compounds that rarely appear on a nutrition label, aren't tracked in diet apps and are seldom mentioned in mainstream dietary advice. Coffee, berries, cocoa, olive oil, herbs, spices and colourful vegetables all seem to offer benefits that can't be fully explained by their vitamin content, calorie count or macronutrient profile alone.

Scientists have spent the last few decades trying to understand why.

Polyphenols, a vast family of plant compounds, may contribute to these associations, alongside fibre, vitamins, minerals, fats and other bioactive compounds.

## So What Exactly Are Polyphenols?

If you've never heard of polyphenols, you're not alone.

They're not regarded as essential nutrients in the same way as vitamin C or magnesium, which means most people can go through life without ever encountering the term. Yet we're exposed to them every day. Every cup of coffee, handful of berries, splash of olive oil or sprinkle of herbs brings hundreds of different plant compounds into the body.

That should make us pause for a moment.

Because if foods with vastly different nutrient profiles are producing broadly similar health outcomes, perhaps we're looking beyond traditional nutrition labels for answers.

In many ways, polyphenols remind us that food is far more complicated, and far more interesting, than the numbers printed on the back of a packet.

## Polyphenols Are Not Just Antioxidants

If you've come across polyphenols before, chances are they were described as antioxidants.

That's technically correct.

It's also where the story usually stops.

For years, nutrition messaging told us that oxidative stress damages cells, antioxidants reduce oxidative stress and therefore consuming more antioxidants must be a good thing. It was a simple explanation, easy to understand and even easier to market.

The supplement industry embraced the idea enthusiastically.

The problem was that reality didn't always cooperate.

When researchers isolated antioxidant compounds and tested them in supplement form, the results often failed to match the benefits seen in populations eating diets naturally rich in polyphenol-containing foods.

If polyphenols were simply acting as little chemical shields against oxidation, those outcomes should have been much more consistent.

Instead, scientists began to suspect that something more sophisticated was happening.

Laboratory and human studies have investigated how different polyphenols interact with pathways involved in inflammation, immune signalling and metabolism. Effects vary between compounds, and how much these mechanisms explain particular foods’ health associations remains uncertain.

And that conversation becomes even more interesting when we start looking at the gut microbiome.

## The Part Of The Story Most People Never Hear

For many years, nutrition science focused on absorption.

That seems perfectly reasonable. We eat food, digest it, absorb the nutrients and use them to build and maintain the body. Nutritional value was often judged by how much of something entered the bloodstream.

Polyphenols presented researchers with a puzzle.

Many of these compounds aren't absorbed particularly well in the small intestine. By conventional thinking, that should have reduced their importance.

Instead, it turns out their journey may only just be beginning.

Further down the digestive tract lies the colon, home to trillions of microorganisms collectively known as the gut microbiome. Rather than disappearing unused, many polyphenols arrive there largely intact, where they interact with an incredibly diverse ecosystem of bacteria.

Some polyphenols reach the large intestine, where gut microbes can transform them into smaller metabolites. These interactions may also influence which microbes thrive, although responses vary considerably between individuals.

This represents quite a shift in how we think about nutrition. Perhaps the question isn't simply what a food does for us. Perhaps it's also what that food does for the trillions of organisms living alongside us.

## Sometimes The Benefit Isn't What Gets Absorbed

Turmeric offers a fascinating example of this idea. Most discussions around turmeric quickly turn into discussions about curcumin, the bright yellow polyphenol that gives the spice its characteristic colour. The conversation usually centres around absorption. How much gets into the bloodstream? How can absorption be increased? Should it be taken with black pepper?

These are interesting questions, but they may not tell the whole story.

A significant proportion of curcumin isn't absorbed in the small intestine at all. Instead, it continues into the colon where it encounters the trillions of microbes that make up the gut microbiome. There, gut bacteria begin transforming curcumin into a range of smaller compounds that may themselves contribute to its biological effects. Researchers increasingly believe that part of turmeric's value may come from this interaction with the microbiome rather than absorption alone.

In other words, we've become conditioned to think that if a compound isn't absorbed, it's somehow wasted. But many fibres and polyphenols seem to exert some of their most interesting effects precisely because they reach the colon. Rather than feeding us directly, they feed the ecosystem living within us.

## What Coffee Taught Us About The Microbiome

Coffee has had a strange journey through the world of nutrition.

Depending on the decade, it has been labelled harmful, beneficial or somewhere in between. One year the focus is caffeine, another year it's stress hormones, and another it's athletic performance.

Yet when researchers began examining the microbiome in greater detail, coffee revealed something rather unexpected.

A study published in Nature Microbiology reported an association between regular coffee consumption and a greater abundance of the gut bacterium Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus across several populations. This is an association and does not show that the bacterium is responsible for coffee’s health links.

The study wasn't important because it proved coffee was a superfood.

It was important because it highlighted something much bigger.

Food isn't simply delivering calories, protein, vitamins and minerals. It's interacting with a living ecosystem. Every meal creates opportunities for certain microbial species to flourish while others become less abundant.

Coffee simply provided one of the clearest examples.

## Polyphenols And Beneficial Gut Bacteria

Much of the current excitement around polyphenols comes from their ability to shape the microbial environment within the gut.

Researchers are increasingly interested in how polyphenol-rich foods influence microbial diversity, gut barrier integrity and the production of beneficial compounds. Rather than acting through a single pathway, polyphenols appear to participate in a complex network of interactions involving both the host and the microbiome.

One bacterium attracting particular attention is **Akkermansia muciniphila**.

Although research is still evolving, higher levels of Akkermansia have been associated with favourable metabolic health and a healthier gut environment in numerous studies. Interestingly, there doesn't appear to be a single food responsible for increasing it. Instead, Akkermansia seems to thrive in the context of diverse, plant-rich diets containing a wide variety of fibres and polyphenol-rich foods. That idea comes up repeatedly in microbiome research. The gut rarely responds to a single miracle ingredient. It responds to variety.

## The Richest Sources Of Polyphenols

The encouraging news is that polyphenols aren't hidden away in obscure superfoods costing a fortune online.

Many of the richest sources are foods that have been part of traditional diets for centuries.

**Berries** provide colourful compounds known as anthocyanins. **Coffee** is one of the largest contributors of polyphenols in many Western diets. **Extra virgin olive oil** contains unique polyphenols often linked to the benefits of Mediterranean-style eating. **Cocoa, tea, herbs, spices, legumes and deeply coloured vegetables** all contribute their own collection of compounds.

The foods richest in polyphenols tend to look remarkably similar to the foods your grandparents would recognise. Not powders. Just plants.

## How To Eat More Polyphenols Without Thinking About It

One of the best things about polyphenols is that increasing your intake doesn't require complicated meal plans or expensive supplements.

Most people could substantially increase their intake simply by making meals a little more colourful and a lot more flavourful.

The biggest missed opportunity often isn't fruit or vegetables. It's the spice cupboard.

Herbs and spices are among the most concentrated sources of polyphenols in the human diet. They contribute very few calories, yet deliver an impressive diversity of plant compounds. Small habits can make a surprisingly big difference.

- Add cinnamon to your morning coffee.

- Stir cocoa powder into Greek yoghurt or kefir.

- Use sumac on salads, eggs, roasted vegetables, hummus or grilled meat.

- Add paprika to tomato-based sauces, soups and stews.

- Cook with turmeric, cumin and coriander more often.

- Be generous with rosemary, oregano, thyme and fresh herbs.

- Scatter fresh parsley over dishes rather than using it as decoration.

- Swap bland meals for colourful, aromatic ones.

Without really trying, you've increased both the flavour and the diversity of compounds reaching your gut.

A polyphenol-rich day doesn't need to look unusual. It could be coffee and yoghurt with berries, cinnamon and cocoa powder for breakfast. A large salad drizzled with extra virgin olive oil and topped with herbs and sumac for lunch. Lentils, beans or colourful vegetables cooked with turmeric, cumin and paprika for dinner. Nothing exotic. Nothing expensive.

Yet across the course of a day you've exposed your body and your microbiome to hundreds of different plant compounds that would never appear on a nutrition label.

## Why "Eat The Rainbow" Is Better Advice Than It Sounds

Health messages like "eat the rainbow" can sound simplistic.

The phrase has been repeated so often that it's easy to dismiss it as another nutritional cliché.

Yet the science behind it is surprisingly compelling.

The deep blue pigments in berries, the reds in cherries, the purples in red cabbage and the vibrant colours of herbs and vegetables often reflect entirely different groups of plant compounds. Different colours frequently mean different polyphenols, and different polyphenols interact with different microbial species. Viewed through that lens, colour becomes about more than appearance. Colour becomes diversity.

And diversity appears to be one of the defining characteristics of a healthy microbiome.

## The Supplement Trap

Whenever science identifies a potentially beneficial compound, it's usually only a matter of time before somebody extracts it, concentrates it and puts it into a capsule.

Polyphenols are no exception. The challenge is that nature rarely packages nutrients in isolation.

A blueberry doesn't arrive with a single active ingredient. It arrives with fibre, polyphenols, water, vitamins, minerals and countless other compounds that interact with one another in ways we're still trying to understand.

Perhaps this helps explain why nutritional science repeatedly arrives at the same conclusion.

Whole dietary patterns tend to outperform isolated nutrients. The further we zoom in, the more complicated nutrition becomes. The further we zoom out, the clearer the message seems to be. Eat more real food.

The more we learn about nutrition, the less it seems to be about finding the perfect nutrient and the more it appears to be about creating the right environment.

An environment rich in diversity.

Diverse plants. Diverse fibres. Diverse polyphenols. Diverse microbes.

That's not a flashy message. It won't sell many supplements.

But it might explain why the foundations of good health still look remarkably similar to what they did before nutrition became obsessed with labels, macros and marketing claims.

Sometimes progress isn't discovering something new.

It's finally understanding why the old wisdom worked in the first place.

# Probiotics

The beneficial bacteria that support our health are known as probiotics. These microscopic allies play a vital role in our well-being by:

- Producing essential vitamins and anti-inflammatory compounds
- Protecting our gut from harmful infections
- Supporting our mood and mental health
- Powering up our immune system—70% of which resides in the gut
Unfortunately, modern lifestyles have disrupted the delicate balance of our gut microbiome. Poor diets, overuse of antibiotics, pesticides, and an obsession with sterility have reduced our probiotic populations and allowed harmful microbes to thrive. This imbalance has been linked to a wide range of health issues—from obesity and inflammation to dementia and mental health disorders.

### Why Probiotics Matter

I’ve touched on probiotics throughout this website and in various blog posts, and I hope by now it’s clear: probiotics are essential for your health.

These beneficial microbes primarily live in our digestive tract, where they feed on components of the food we eat and transform them into powerful compounds our bodies can’t produce on their own—like vitamins and anti-inflammatories.

Some strains are so effective, they act like natural antibiotics, producing substances that kill harmful bacteria. Others strengthen the gut lining or even produce compounds similar to oxytocin, helping us feel calm and relaxed.

We live in a symbiotic relationship with these tiny organisms. Together, we’ve evolved over millennia. The microbiome—the vast community of microbes living inside us—is crucial to nearly every aspect of our health.

### The Modern Threat to Our Microbiome

Today’s world is tough on our microbiome. Major disruptors include:

- Highly processed diets
- Overuse of antibiotics
- Pesticides in our food
- Excessive cleanliness and fear of bacteria
With refrigeration, we’ve lost touch with traditional food preservation methods like fermentation. We throw away anything that shows signs of bacterial or fungal growth, forgetting that many of our favorite foods—yogurt, cheese, wine, beer, even chocolate—are the result of fermentation.

Ironically, in trying to avoid harmful bacteria, we’ve created the perfect environment for them to flourish inside us.

### Rebuilding the Balance

While we can’t undo the past, we can take steps to restore and support our gut health. One of the most powerful ways to do this is by reintroducing cultured and fermented foods into our diets.

By re-learning the art of fermentation, we can begin to repopulate our gut with the probiotics we’ve lost—and reclaim our health in the process.

# Protein

## Feed Your Muscles, Not the Marketing

Protein has become one of the most heavily marketed nutrients in the food industry. Supermarket shelves are filled with protein-enriched cereals, bars, puddings and drinks, often suggesting that more protein automatically means better health.

But protein isn't a new discovery. It is one of the body's fundamental building materials, involved in everything from muscle maintenance and tissue repair to immune function and the production of enzymes and hormones.

The science behind protein is well established. What's more interesting is how our understanding is developing, particularly around ageing, muscle health and female physiology.

So what do we actually know?

## What's Proven? Protein and Muscle Health

Protein supplies the amino acids needed to build and maintain tissues throughout the body.

One of its most important roles is supporting skeletal muscle. Muscle is constantly being broken down and rebuilt, with protein providing the raw materials needed for repair and maintenance.

Research clearly demonstrates that protein intake, alongside resistance exercise, supports muscle growth and helps preserve muscle mass.

This matters throughout life, but becomes increasingly relevant as we age.

Muscle mass and strength naturally change over time. Significant age-related loss can contribute to sarcopenia, a condition involving reduced muscle strength, muscle quantity and physical function.

Its effects reach well beyond appearance. Muscle supports mobility, balance, physical independence and everyday activities.

Protein also contributes to:
- Tissue growth and repair.
- Production of enzymes and hormones.
- Normal immune system function.
- Maintenance of bones and other body structures.

## What's Emerging? Protein and Female Health

For many years, much of the sports nutrition research was conducted predominantly in men. There is now growing interest in understanding how female physiology may influence muscle maintenance and protein metabolism.

Dr Stacy Sims, a researcher specialising in female exercise physiology, has helped bring attention to the potential differences associated with menstrual cycles, perimenopause and menopause.

### The Menopause Connection

Oestrogen has several roles in the body, including processes associated with muscle and bone health. During the menopausal transition, changing hormone levels coincide with changes in body composition and muscle function.

Researchers are investigating whether these hormonal changes influence how muscle responds to protein and exercise.

One concept attracting attention is *anabolic resistance*: the possibility that muscle becomes less responsive to the signals that normally stimulate repair and growth.

Sims has highlighted the importance of considering female life stages when studying nutrition and exercise. However, research into specific female protein requirements is still developing. We cannot yet say that every woman needs a substantially different amount of protein because of her menstrual cycle or menopausal status.

### What Else Are Researchers Exploring?

Other areas of interest include:
- Whether protein needs change across the menstrual cycle.
- Whether hormonal changes influence muscle protein synthesis.
- How protein and resistance exercise interact during and after menopause.
- Whether protein timing has meaningful long-term benefits for women.

These questions are important, particularly because women have historically been underrepresented in some areas of sports and nutrition research.

## What's Marketing?

The food industry has taken a genuine nutritional need and turned it into a major selling point.

Protein is important, but that doesn't mean every food needs extra protein added to it.

A protein-enriched biscuit is still a biscuit. Protein chocolate is still chocolate. Adding protein doesn't automatically make an ultra-processed food nutritionally balanced.

There is also a tendency online to promote very high protein targets as though they apply to everyone.

The commonly quoted reference intake of around 0.8g per kilogram of body weight is designed to meet the needs of most healthy adults. Requirements can differ with age, activity, health and individual circumstances.

Higher intakes are studied in athletic populations and older adults, but figures promoted for bodybuilders or performance athletes should not be treated as universal health targets.

Nor does consuming more protein automatically lead to more muscle. Muscle adaptation depends on several interacting factors, including physical activity, age, overall nutrition and health.

## The Takeaway

Protein is essential, but its importance goes far beyond fitness trends and food marketing.

Its role in muscle maintenance, tissue repair and normal body function is firmly established. Research into ageing, anabolic resistance and female physiology is helping us understand how these processes may change throughout life.

The emerging work on women's health is particularly valuable because it highlights areas that have historically received less attention.

The real science isn't about making every food high in protein or assuming that more is always better. It's about understanding how this essential nutrient supports the body at different stages of life.

## The Science Deep Dive: How Protein Works

### Amino Acids: The Building Blocks

Proteins are made from 20 amino acids. Nine are classed as essential because the body cannot make them in sufficient amounts, so they must come from the diet.

Different protein sources provide different combinations and proportions of these amino acids. Animal proteins generally contain all nine essential amino acids, while plant sources vary. A varied plant-based diet can still provide all the essential amino acids.

### Leucine and the Muscle-Building Signal

One amino acid that receives particular attention is leucine.

Leucine helps activate a cellular signalling pathway called mTOR, which plays a central role in regulating muscle protein synthesis.

Think of it as part of the signal that tells muscle cells the materials needed for rebuilding are available.

However, leucine doesn't work alone. All essential amino acids are needed to construct new muscle proteins.

### Muscle Protein Synthesis and Anabolic Resistance

Muscle is in a constant state of turnover. Muscle protein synthesis builds new proteins, while muscle protein breakdown removes existing ones.

The balance between these processes influences whether muscle is maintained, gained or lost.

As we age, the muscle-building response to protein and exercise may become less sensitive. This is known as anabolic resistance.

It is one of the reasons researchers are interested in how nutrition and resistance exercise interact in older adults, including women experiencing the menopausal transition.

The mechanisms are still being investigated, but the underlying biology helps explain why protein is such an important part of the conversation around healthy ageing.

# Resistant Starch

#### Why a Cold Potato Isn't the Same as a Hot Potato

Most people think of starch as a straightforward source of carbohydrate. But starch is surprisingly dynamic. How it's prepared can dramatically alter the way your body digests it.

Starch is made up of two glucose polymers: amylose and amylopectin. When foods such as potatoes, rice, oats and pasta are cooked in water, the starch granules absorb water, swell and lose their tightly packed crystalline structure. This process, known as gelatinisation, makes starch far more accessible to digestive enzymes.

As the food cools, some of these starch molecules begin to realign. The relatively straight amylose chains form tightly packed crystalline structures held together by hydrogen bonds. This process is called retrogradation. Once these crystals form, they become much more difficult for digestive enzymes to break apart. The result is resistant starch type 3 (RS3), a form of starch that largely escapes digestion in the small intestine.

In simple terms:

#### Cook → starch becomes highly digestible

#### Cool → starch recrystallises

#### Recrystallised starch → resistant starch

This explains why cooled potatoes, rice and pasta often produce a lower blood glucose response than their freshly cooked equivalents.

## The Five Types of Resistant Starch

Resistant starch isn't a single substance. It's a group of starches that resist digestion for different reasons.

### RS1: Physically Protected Starch

RS1 is starch that digestive enzymes simply cannot easily access because it is trapped within the physical structure of the food.

Examples include:

- Intact whole grains
- Beans and lentils
- Seeds
- Coarsely milled cereals
Grinding, blending and extensive processing can reduce RS1 because the starch becomes more accessible.

### RS2: Native Resistant Starch

RS2 occurs naturally when starch granules have a tightly packed crystalline structure that human digestive enzymes struggle to break down.

Examples include:

- Green bananas
- Raw potatoes
- Some legumes
- High-amylose maize
As bananas ripen, much of this resistant starch is converted into sugar. This is why a green banana behaves very differently metabolically from a ripe one.

### RS3: Retrograded Resistant Starch

This is the form most people encounter when discussing resistant starch.

When foods such as potatoes, rice or pasta are cooked, the starch becomes digestible. As the food cools, some of the starch recrystallises into a new structure that resists digestion. This process is known as retrogradation.

Examples include:

- Cooled potatoes
- Potato salad
- Cooled rice
- Sushi rice
- Cooled pasta
- Overnight oats
This is arguably the easiest form of resistant starch to increase in an everyday diet.

### RS4: Chemically Modified Resistant Starch

RS4 is created through industrial food processing. Manufacturers chemically modify starch molecules to make them less digestible.

These starches are commonly used in:

- Processed foods
- Fibre-enriched products
- Some protein bars
- Low-carbohydrate manufactured foods
Although technically a resistant starch, it differs substantially from the naturally occurring forms found in whole foods.

### RS5: Amylose-Lipid Complexes

RS5 forms when amylose binds to fats and creates a structure that digestive enzymes struggle to access.

This can occur when starches are cooked in the presence of fats such as:

- Butter
- Olive oil
- Coconut oil
This is a relatively newer classification but is becoming an area of increasing research interest because it appears to combine some of the benefits of resistant starch formation with everyday cooking practices.

## Which Types Should We Be Eating?

From a health perspective, the most practical sources are generally:

- RS1 from legumes and minimally processed whole grains
- RS2 from foods such as green bananas
- RS3 from cooled potatoes, rice and pasta
These forms occur naturally and have been consumed throughout human history. They also appear to be readily fermented by gut microbes, producing beneficial compounds such as butyrate.

While resistant starch is often discussed as a single nutrient, the reality is that a green banana, a lentil and a cold potato all contain resistant starch for completely different structural reasons. Understanding those differences helps explain why food preparation can be just as important as food choice itself.

### Which Foods Form The Most?

Foods rich in amylose generally produce more resistant starch after cooling because amylose retrogrades more readily than amylopectin.

Good examples include:

- Potatoes
- Rice, particularly higher-amylose varieties such as basmati
- Pasta
- Beans and lentils
- Oats
Interestingly, repeated heating and cooling cycles may increase resistant starch formation even further, as each cycle provides another opportunity for starch molecules to reorganise into stable crystalline structures.

### What Happens When You Reheat It?

Contrary to popular belief, resistant starch isn't completely destroyed by reheating.

Once RS3 has formed, a significant proportion remains intact during gentle reheating. This means reheated leftover potatoes, rice or pasta typically still contain more resistant starch than when they were first cooked and eaten hot.

### So Would Eating A Raw Potato Have The Same Effect?

From a resistant starch perspective, surprisingly, yes.

Raw potatoes contain large amounts of RS2 resistant starch. However, this doesn't make eating raw potatoes a good strategy.

Raw potatoes are:

- Difficult to digest
- Less palatable
- More likely to cause gastrointestinal discomfort
- Potentially contain higher levels of naturally occurring compounds such as glycoalkaloids
Cooking destroys most RS2 but greatly improves digestibility and nutrient availability. Cooling subsequently creates RS3. For most people, cooled cooked potatoes are the more practical option.

Interestingly, green bananas are one of the richest natural sources of resistant starch available. As they ripen, much of that resistant starch is converted into sugar.

### What About Uncooked Pasta?

Not really.

Uncooked pasta is certainly harder to digest because the starch granules remain largely ungelatinised, but it doesn't provide the same benefits as retrograded resistant starch formed after cooking and cooling.

The starch is physically inaccessible rather than intentionally transformed into a beneficial fermentable substrate. Eating dry pasta is also unlikely to be particularly enjoyable and may cause digestive discomfort.

### Why Does Any Of This Matter?

Resistant starch behaves much more like a fermentable fibre than a conventional carbohydrate.

Instead of being digested and absorbed as glucose in the small intestine, it reaches the colon where it is fermented by gut microbes. This process produces short-chain fatty acids, particularly butyrate, which serves as a primary fuel source for colon cells and is associated with improved gut and metabolic health.

Perhaps the most fascinating takeaway is that a potato doesn't need to become a different food to behave differently in the body. Simply allowing it to cool changes the physical arrangement of the starch molecules, altering how your digestive system and gut microbiome interact with it.

# Sleep Strategies

*Why quality sleep matters for the brain and body, what healthy sleep looks like, and how light, daily rhythms, nutrition and lifestyle could help us get more from our nights.*

Sleep is one of the foundations of good health, yet it is often the first thing we sacrifice when life gets busy. We tend to think of it as simply switching off for the night, but sleep is anything but passive. While we rest, the brain and body carry out a remarkable range of processes that support our health, performance and wellbeing.

Neuroscientist Dr Matthew Walker, author of *Why We Sleep*, has helped bring this science into the mainstream. His central message is that sleep is not a luxury or an optional recovery tool: it is a fundamental biological requirement. Research increasingly supports this view, showing that the quantity, quality and regularity of our sleep influence almost every major system in the body.

## Why Good Quality Sleep Matters

Sleep supports much more than feeling refreshed. It plays a role in brain function, emotional health, immunity, metabolism and cardiovascular health.

**Brain function, learning and memory**

During sleep, the brain processes and consolidates information gathered throughout the day. Different stages appear to support different aspects of memory: deep non-REM sleep is associated with consolidating factual memories, while REM sleep contributes to emotional processing, learning and aspects of creativity.

Sleep also prepares the brain for the following day, supporting attention, concentration and decision-making. Even one night of poor sleep can make us more emotionally reactive and less able to regulate our responses.

**Immune health**

Sleep helps coordinate the immune system's response to infection. Insufficient sleep can affect immune cell activity and inflammatory signalling, and research has found that people who regularly sleep too little can have weaker responses to some vaccinations.

**Metabolism and appetite**

Sleep influences hormones and signals involved in appetite, hunger and energy balance. Too little sleep can affect insulin sensitivity and glucose regulation, while increasing hunger and cravings for energy-dense foods. Habitual short sleep is associated with a higher risk of weight gain and type 2 diabetes.

**Heart and cardiovascular health**

During healthy sleep, heart rate and blood pressure generally fall, giving the cardiovascular system a period of reduced activity. Repeatedly getting too little or disrupted sleep is associated with higher blood pressure and increased cardiovascular risk.

**The brain's overnight maintenance**

An intriguing area of research concerns the brain's waste-clearance systems. Fluid movement through the brain appears to increase during sleep, potentially helping remove metabolic waste products. This is often discussed in relation to the glymphatic system and proteins such as beta-amyloid, which are associated with Alzheimer's disease. The possible link between sleep and long-term brain health is exciting, although it is not yet established that improving sleep directly prevents dementia.

## What Does Healthy Sleep Look Like?

**Why it matters:** Sleep duration is important, but it is not the only measure of good sleep. Regularity, continuity and how we feel during the day also offer useful clues.

**What good looks like:**
- Most adults need around **7–9 hours** of sleep each night, although individual needs vary.
- Sleep and wake times are reasonably consistent across the week.
- Falling asleep feels relatively straightforward, without regularly lying awake for long periods.
- There may be brief awakenings during the night, but sleep is generally continuous.
- You usually wake feeling reasonably refreshed and can maintain alertness during the day.

Sleep naturally moves through cycles of non-REM and REM sleep, with the balance changing throughout the night. Deep sleep tends to be more prominent earlier, while REM periods become longer towards morning. There is no need to consciously manage these stages or chase a particular sleep score.

**Strategies to explore:**
- Allow enough time for sleep rather than regularly cutting it short.
- Keep wake-up times relatively consistent, including at weekends.
- Create a relaxing wind-down period that helps separate the day from bedtime.
- Look at patterns over several weeks rather than judging sleep by one night or a wearable score.

## Morning Light: Setting the Body Clock

**Why it matters:** Our circadian rhythm is the internal timing system that helps coordinate sleep and wakefulness over roughly 24 hours. Light is its most important environmental signal. Specialised cells in the retina detect light and send information to the brain's central body clock, helping it stay aligned with the day-night cycle.

Morning daylight helps signal that the day has begun. It supports daytime alertness and helps set the timing of the body's evening melatonin rise — the hormonal signal that prepares us for sleep.

**What good looks like:** A clear contrast between brighter days and darker evenings. Natural daylight, particularly in the morning, helps reinforce the body's sense of when daytime begins.

**Strategies to explore:**
- Spend some time outdoors in the morning, ideally soon after waking.
- Make daylight part of an existing routine, such as having breakfast outside or walking in the morning.
- Get outside during the day where possible, rather than relying entirely on indoor lighting.

The circadian benefits of daylight are well established. The precise amount of light needed varies with season, weather and individual circumstances, but outdoor daylight is generally much brighter than typical indoor lighting.

## Sunset and Evening Light

**Why it matters:** The body clock responds to changing light levels throughout the day. Bright light late in the evening can suppress melatonin and delay the timing of sleep, particularly when exposure is intense and prolonged.

Sunset is an interesting part of this natural transition. Spending time outdoors as daylight fades may provide a relaxing end to the day, although the specific sleep benefits of deliberately watching the sunset have not been firmly established.

**What good looks like:** A gradual shift towards a darker, calmer environment in the hours before bed, rather than remaining under bright lighting until the moment you try to sleep.

**Strategies to explore:**
- Dim indoor lighting as the evening progresses.
- Reduce the brightness of screens and devices, especially close to bedtime.
- Choose relaxing activities in the evening, such as reading, gentle stretching or spending time outdoors.
- Avoid making screens the only focus: overall light intensity, timing and duration all matter.

There is no need to avoid every screen or artificial light source. The aim is to give the body a clearer contrast between daytime and night.

## Herbs: Traditional Remedies, Emerging Evidence

**Why it matters:** Stress and mental activity can make it harder to settle at night. Some herbs contain compounds that may influence pathways involved in relaxation, which is why they have been used traditionally as sleep aids.

**What good looks like:** A calming evening routine that helps you unwind. Herbal teas may be a pleasant part of this, but they should not be viewed as proven treatments for persistent insomnia.

**What the research suggests:**

- **Chamomile:** Contains apigenin, a plant flavonoid that interacts with certain receptors in the brain involved in calming activity. Small studies suggest it may help with subjective sleep quality, but strong evidence for treating insomnia is lacking.
- **Valerian root:** Traditionally used as a sleep aid and thought to influence GABA-related signalling in the nervous system. Research has produced inconsistent results, with no convincing evidence of a reliable effect on insomnia.
- **Passionflower:** Contains compounds being studied for their potential effects on GABA activity. A few small studies suggest it could support relaxation or sleep quality, but the evidence remains limited.

**Strategies to explore:**
- Try a caffeine-free herbal tea as part of a relaxing evening routine.
- Treat supplements and concentrated herbal extracts differently from an ordinary cup of tea: strength, quality and potential interactions can vary.
- Check with a healthcare professional or pharmacist if you take medication or have an existing health condition.

These are interesting areas of research, but natural does not automatically mean effective or risk-free.

## Magnesium and L-Theanine

**Why it matters:** Both magnesium and L-theanine are often included in evening supplements because of their potential effects on nervous system activity.

**What good looks like:** Meeting nutritional needs through a balanced diet and supporting a relaxed evening routine. Supplementation may be relevant in some circumstances, but more is not necessarily better.

**What the research suggests:**

- **Magnesium:** This essential mineral is involved in hundreds of processes throughout the body, including muscle and nerve function. Low magnesium status may be relevant to sleep and general health, but this does not mean that taking extra magnesium will necessarily improve sleep. Clinical trials have produced mixed results, and the overall evidence for magnesium supplementation as a sleep treatment remains uncertain.
- **L-theanine:** An amino acid naturally found in tea leaves. It has been studied for its potential to promote a relaxed but alert state, with possible effects on brain activity and neurotransmitter signalling. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found modest improvements in some self-reported sleep measures, including sleep quality and time taken to fall asleep. These findings are encouraging, but further research is needed.

**Strategies to explore:**
- Include magnesium-rich foods such as leafy greens, nuts, seeds and whole grains in your diet.
- Consider whether a supplement is actually needed rather than assuming it will improve sleep.
- Be mindful that supplements can interact with medication and may not be suitable for everyone.

Both magnesium and L-theanine are worth further study, rather than established treatments for sleep problems.

## Daily Habits and Sleep Disruptors

**Why it matters:** Our sleep is influenced by both the circadian rhythm — the timing of our internal clock — and sleep pressure, which builds the longer we are awake. Caffeine, alcohol and irregular routines can interfere with these processes.

**What good looks like:** A reasonably consistent daily rhythm, with enough time for sleep and limited disruption from stimulants or alcohol.

**Strategies to explore:**

- **Caffeine:** Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors. Adenosine helps build sleep pressure while we are awake, so blocking its effects can make us feel alert even when the body is ready for rest. Research shows that caffeine can reduce total sleep time, delay falling asleep and reduce deep sleep. Its effects can last for many hours, and sensitivity varies. Experimenting with an earlier caffeine cut-off may help.
- **Alcohol:** Although alcohol can initially make people feel sleepy, it can disrupt normal sleep architecture as the night progresses. It is associated with more fragmented sleep and changes to REM sleep. Reducing alcohol, particularly close to bedtime, may help improve sleep continuity.
- **Exercise:** Regular physical activity is associated with better sleep and can support overall health. The relationship with exercise timing is more individual, although vigorous activity very close to bedtime may affect some people's ability to settle.
- **Stress:** Mental and emotional stress can make it harder to fall asleep and contribute to waking during the night. A consistent wind-down routine, relaxation exercises or journalling may help create a more settled transition to bed.
- **Sleep environment:** A dark, quiet and comfortably cool bedroom can make it easier to maintain sleep. Temperature preferences vary, but overheating can be disruptive.

## Bringing the Evidence Together

The strongest foundations for healthy sleep are sufficient sleep duration, a reasonably regular schedule, natural daylight during the day and a darker environment in the evening. Limiting caffeine and alcohol close to bedtime can also make a meaningful difference.

Other approaches are worth exploring, but with more measured expectations. Sunset exposure may offer a relaxing transition into the evening, while chamomile, passionflower, magnesium and L-theanine have varying levels of preliminary or mixed evidence. Some may help certain people, but none should be treated as a guaranteed solution.

Sleep is shaped by the interaction of biology, environment and behaviour. Rather than looking for one perfect supplement or routine, it is useful to understand the foundations first, then experiment with additional approaches that could offer benefits.

If sleep difficulties are persistent or affecting daily life, it is worth seeking professional advice rather than relying on supplements or lifestyle changes alone.

# Milk Kefir

**Kefir is arguably the most powerful probiotic food you can make at home, and one of the simplest.**

Milk kefir is a traditional fermented food made by adding kefir grains, a living community of beneficial bacteria and yeasts, to milk. Over 24 hours, these microorganisms consume much of the milk's lactose and transform it into a tangy, yoghurt-like drink packed with probiotics, organic acids, bioactive peptides and other health-promoting compounds.

Unlike most commercial yoghurts, homemade kefir contains an incredibly diverse microbial community. Depending on the grains and fermentation conditions, homemade kefir can contain around **30 to 60+ different strains of beneficial bacteria and yeasts**, compared with the handful of strains typically found in many shop-bought products. More diversity means a broader range of microbial metabolites and a potentially greater impact on the gut microbiome.

Regular consumption of kefir has been associated with improved gut microbial diversity, digestive health, immune function and metabolic health. The fermentation process also breaks down much of the lactose naturally present in milk, making kefir easier to tolerate for many people who struggle with regular dairy.

Making kefir couldn't be easier. Simply add kefir grains to milk and leave it at room temperature for around 24 hours. During this time, the microbes multiply and convert the lactose into a wide range of beneficial compounds. The finished kefir thickens into a creamy, slightly tangy drink rich in probiotics and nutrients. The grains are then strained out and added to fresh milk to start the process again.

One glass of kefir provides a wealth of nutrients, including calcium, phosphorus, vitamin B12, riboflavin (B2), magnesium and other bioactive compounds. Beyond its nutritional profile, kefir has also demonstrated antimicrobial properties and may help support a healthier balance of microorganisms within the gut.

Perhaps most importantly, kefir is more than just a source of probiotics. It is a living fermented food that introduces beneficial microbes and their fermentation products into the diet, helping to restore some of the microbial exposures that have become increasingly scarce in our modern, ultra-sanitised food environment.

Water Kefir is my newest fermentation venture. I love kombucha, but I wanted an alternative to drink in the evening that did not contain caffeine.

# Fermented Vegetables

#### Pickles, Preserves and Probiotics

Fermentation is one of humanity's oldest and most effective ways of preserving food. By creating the right environment, naturally occurring lactic-acid bacteria convert the starches and sugars in vegetables into lactic acid. This gentle acidification prevents harmful microbes from taking hold while allowing beneficial, probiotic bacteria to flourish—enhancing digestibility and unlocking bioactive compounds like vitamin C along the way.

Traditional fermentation gives foods unique flavours, textures and nutritional profiles.

The fungus _Rhizopus, for example_ ferments soybeans into tempeh

Most lactic acid bacteria such as Lactobacilli, convert milk into cheese and yogurt

And did you know that a lot of vitamin supplements use the art of fermentation to produce vitamins such as B12.

Classic Lacto-fermentation uses the lactic-acid bacteria naturally on the surface of the vegetables to convert the starches and sugars of the vegetables into lactic acid. This is a natural preservative that inhibits the growth of harmful bacteria.

This acid makes it too acidic for harmful bacteria to survive but allows the beneficial, probiotic lactic-acid loving bacteria to thrive.

This also starts to break down the foods, making the vegetables easier to digest. This also releases and converts compounds into a whole host of beneficial vitamins, enzymes and bioactive chemicals such as vitamin C. Far more than the original starting vegetable.

# Probiotic Yogurts

Not all yoghurt is created equal. And that lovely-looking pot with *probiotic* splashed across the label? It might not be quite the gut-friendly superstar you think it is.

Yoghurt can be a wonderfully nutritious food, providing protein, calcium and beneficial live bacteria. But here's the catch: **the yoghurt you choose really matters.**

Some are little more than milk and live cultures. Others come with a surprisingly long list of added ingredients. So let's take a closer look at what's actually in the pot.

## First things first: what makes a proper yoghurt?

Traditional yoghurt starts with milk and two particular bacteria: *Streptococcus thermophilus* and *Lactobacillus bulgaricus*.

These little workers ferment the milk, producing lactic acid. That's what gives yoghurt its familiar tang and thick texture.

That's yoghurt in its simplest form. Milk, cultures and a little fermentation magic!

But turn over some supermarket pots and you'll find a rather different story. Added sugar, sweeteners, flavourings, starches, gums and thickeners can all make an appearance.

These ingredients don't automatically mean a product isn't yoghurt, but they do make it worth asking: **what else am I actually eating?**

My preference is a plain, natural yoghurt with a short ingredient list. Ideally, just milk and live yoghurt cultures. You can add your own fresh fruit, berries, nuts or a little honey if you fancy something sweeter.

And don't be fooled by the word *natural* or *probiotic* on the front of a packet. The ingredient list tells you much more.

One other thing to check: some yoghurts are heat-treated after fermentation. This can kill the live cultures, so don't assume every yoghurt contains living bacteria.

<text>**A little word about children's yoghurts…**</text>

Those colourful little pots and pouches are often marketed as healthy snacks for children. But take a look at the ingredients and you may find added sugar, fruit concentrates, flavourings, starches, gums and other additives.

Many of these products are highly processed and fall into the ultra-processed food (UPF) category. They can be a far cry from a simple yoghurt.

Don't assume that because it's made for children, it's automatically a healthy choice. A plain natural yoghurt with some fresh fruit added is often a much simpler option.

## What's so good about yoghurt anyway?

Yoghurt has a lot going for it:

- **It's nutritious.** It can provide protein, calcium, phosphorus and B vitamins.
- **It may be easier to digest than milk.** Live yoghurt cultures help break down lactose, which is why some people who struggle with milk find yoghurt easier to tolerate.
- **It can provide live bacteria.** Depending on the product and cultures used, these may help support digestion and the gut microbiome.

And that last point is where things get particularly interesting.

Your gut is home to a huge community of microorganisms. It's not simply about having lots of bacteria, but having a varied community that can carry out lots of different jobs.

Some research suggests fermented foods may help support this diversity. It's one reason I think they're worth making room for in the diet.

## Kefir: yoghurt's more adventurous cousin

If you've tried kefir, you'll know it's usually thinner, tangier and sometimes a little fizzy compared with yoghurt.

But the difference isn't just the texture.

Traditional milk kefir is made using kefir grains. These aren't grains in the usual sense – they're little clusters of bacteria and yeasts living together in a rather clever partnership.

They ferment the milk and create a varied mix of microorganisms and fermentation products.

You'll find plenty of kefir drinks in supermarkets now. Some are made using selected starter cultures rather than traditional kefir grains. They can still be fermented foods, but they aren't necessarily the same as traditional grain-fermented kefir.

If you want to try the traditional version, look for kefir made with real grains – or have a go at making your own.

## Yakult and Actimel: what's the attraction?

Those little bottles have become familiar sights in supermarket fridges. They're convenient, easy to drink and marketed around their added bacteria.

Yakult contains a particular strain known as *L. casei* Shirota. Actimel also uses selected bacterial cultures, including its own proprietary strains.

The important thing here is that probiotic effects are often **strain-specific**. One strain may have been studied for a particular benefit, but that doesn't mean every member of the same bacterial species does the same thing.

These drinks can be a convenient way to consume particular cultures. But check the label, too. Some versions contain quite a bit of added sugar, and they're not a substitute for a varied diet rich in fibre and different plant foods.

## The rise of specialist probiotic yoghurts

There's another side to all this: yoghurts made with particular bacterial strains, chosen for their potential effects rather than simply for taste or texture.

Dr William Davis brought a lot of attention to this idea in his book *Super Gut*. He explores the use of specific bacteria, including *L. reuteri*, in homemade fermented yoghurt.

It's an interesting area because different bacteria can have very different effects. Researchers are looking at strains that may influence the gut lining, immune activity and even communication between the gut and other parts of the body.

But there's an important distinction. A study showing that a particular strain has an effect doesn't automatically mean that eating any yoghurt containing that species will give you the same result. The strain, amount, fermentation process and final number of live bacteria all matter.

So, specialist yoghurts are an exciting area to explore – just be wary of big health claims that go further than the evidence.

And one bacterium that's attracting plenty of attention is *L. reuteri*. It's interesting enough to deserve a closer look of its own.

---

## What does the research say?

**Fermented foods and the gut microbiome**

A Stanford study published in *Cell* in 2021 compared a diet rich in fermented foods with a high-fibre diet in 36 healthy adults. The fermented-food group increased the diversity of their gut microbiome and showed reductions in several inflammatory markers.

It's an interesting finding, although it was a relatively small study and doesn't mean fermented foods are a cure-all – or that fibre is any less important.

**Yoghurt and lactose digestion**

There is good evidence that live yoghurt cultures help people with lactose maldigestion digest lactose. This is one reason yoghurt may be better tolerated than milk by some people.

**Why the bacterial strain matters**

Probiotic research is increasingly focused on individual strains rather than treating all bacteria of one species as interchangeable. A result for one strain cannot automatically be applied to another.

# Kombucha

Kombucha was the first fermented food, well, drink, that I made at home. I'd tried a few shop-bought versions and enjoyed the taste, but quickly realised that many commercial kombuchas are pasteurised. While this improves shelf life, it also removes the live microbes that make traditional kombucha so special. That was enough to convince me to start brewing my own.

Making kombucha is surprisingly simple once you've done it once. If you'd like help getting started, drop me a message and we can arrange a call to talk it through. If you're local, I'd be happy to share a SCOBY and help you get your first batch underway!

Kombucha starts with sweetened tea and a SCOBY (Symbiotic Culture of Bacteria and Yeast). Over the next 7-14 days, the microorganisms ferment the tea, transforming it into a lightly sparkling, sweet-and-sour drink rich in organic acids, polyphenols and other beneficial compounds.

What makes kombucha particularly interesting is that it's not just a source of probiotics. During fermentation, the microbes produce a wide range of postbiotics: beneficial compounds created by the fermentation process itself. These include organic acids, polyphenol metabolites and other bioactive compounds that can help support gut health, immune function and the integrity of the gut lining.

Think of probiotics as the microbes, and postbiotics as the beneficial substances they leave behind. Both are thought to play a role in supporting a healthy microbiome. Increasingly, researchers believe that many of the benefits of fermented foods come not only from the live organisms themselves, but also from these fermentation-derived compounds.

Tea is naturally rich in polyphenols, and fermentation further modifies these compounds, creating new metabolites that may enhance their biological activity. This is one reason kombucha is often recognised for its antioxidant potential and its role in supporting metabolic and digestive health.

For me, though, one of the biggest benefits is much simpler: it's delicious. Kept chilled in the fridge, kombucha makes a refreshing, naturally fizzy alternative to soft drinks and can be flavoured endlessly. Some of my favourites include:

Ginger & Lemon

Raspberry & Mint

Mixed Berry

Orange & Turmeric

Cherry & Vanilla

Lavender

Kombucha isn't a miracle drink, but it is a fantastic way to introduce both beneficial microbes and fermentation compounds into your diet. In a world dominated by ultra-processed foods and sugary beverages, a living fermented drink is a simple and enjoyable step towards supporting your microbiome.

Once you've made your first batch, you'll wonder why it ever seemed complicated.

## Steps for continuous brewing

#### NOURISH THE STARTER

Once you have your starter SCOBY (and/or pellicle) you want to get it super strong before diving into a full brew.

DO NOT MISS THIS STEP!!!

The most common reason for contamination is the kombucha not being strong and acidic enough to ward off any yeast or contaminants from the air/surfaces.

#### WHAT YOU'LL NEED

- A brewing vessel - to start, you'll only need a 1L jar, but make sure you have around a 5L vessel with a tap.
This tap will need to be food grade stainless steel. If you vessel does not come with it, they are not terribly expensive on [Amazon](https://amzn.eu/d/cAxMyTI).

- Starter SCOBY culture
- Organic cane sugar
- Good quality plain tea - bags or loose tea - normal black or green tea, no herbal, rooibos, flavourings, decaf etc. This may affect the fermentation.
- A large glass jug
- Wooden spoon
- A clean muslin cloth or breathable covering for the top of the vessel & elastic band
- ~1L Clip top or Screw top glass bottle/s for the finished product <3

#### GETTING YOUR STARTER READY ~12 DAYS

Let's assume you have received about 200ml of starter and a small pellicle. This can also apply for the store bought kombucha.

#### Stage 1

1. Make sure you sterilise everything. Ideally a hot dishwasher with just the kombucha utensils and small 1l vessel.
2. Mix around 2 teabags in around 300ml hot water in the glass jug
3. Add ~50g sugar and dissolve
4. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags
5. Add ~300ml of cool water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
6. Add the SCOBY to your small vessel and cover with your cloth.
7. Leave this for about 3-4 days.
#### Stage 2

Basically repeating it all

1. Make sure you sterilise everything. This time, the large vessel you will be using for your continuous brew.
2. Mix around 2 teabags in around 300ml hot water in the glass jug
3. Add ~50g sugar and dissolve
4. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags
5. Add ~300ml of cool water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
6. Add the previous brewing Kombucha including the scoby to your large vessel
7. Add the new sugar tea
8. Cover with your cloth.
9. Leave this for about 3-4 days. You should get a super thin, white-ish pellicle forming on the top of this brew now.
Check for no weird white or green circles on the top!

#### Stage 3

Just adding more sweet tea.

1. Mix around 2 teabags in around 300ml hot water in the glass jug
2. Add ~50g sugar and dissolve
3. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags
4. Add ~300ml of cool water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
5. Add to the continuous brewing Kombucha
6. Leave for another 3-4 days. You should get a thicker, pellicle forming on the top of this brew now.
Now you have a lovely strong starter!

You should have about 2L of Kombucha now.

Depending on the size of your vessel, this will determine how much you add.

#### LETS MAKE KOMBUCHA

For every 1L I usually add around 90g of Cane Sugar and 4Tea Bags

So if you want to get your brew to 4 liters, let's do the following

1. Use your existing ~2L of Kombucha as your super strong starter. Keep it in the brewing vessel.
2. Mix around 8 teabags in around 500ml hot water in the glass jug
3. Add ~180g sugar and dissolve
4. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags

6. Add to the continuous brewing Kombucha

9. You should get a lovely thick, pellicle forming on the top of this brew now.

#### FIRST OR SECOND BREW

1. Once the kombucha is to your taste, you can draw off the 1L into your bottle and drink away.

# The Art of Doing Nothing

#### Take back your attention…

We are living in a world of constant stimulation. Your phone is always there, notifications, emails, WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, news, podcasts, music and television. There is always something to look at, something to listen to, something new to consume and something demanding our attention.

How often do you find yourself reaching for your phone without even really knowing why? Waiting in a queue, waiting for the kettle to boil, sitting on the toilet, having your morning coffee or simply having a few minutes to yourself. It has become almost automatic to fill those little gaps in our day with something.

But perhaps those gaps are actually quite important.

Our attention is valuable, and there are huge companies whose business models depend on keeping us looking at a screen for as long as possible. The longer we stay scrolling, watching and interacting, the more opportunities there are to show us advertising, recommend content and learn about our behaviour.

This doesn't necessarily require some sinister conspiracy. It is simply how many of these businesses make money. Our attention has value, and these companies have become extremely good at capturing it.

## Why we can't sit still

Think about how social media works. There is rarely a natural end point. You don't reach the bottom of Instagram or TikTok and get told that you have finished. There is always another video, another post, another headline, another notification and something else being recommended specifically for you.

You are constantly being offered something new.

And this is where I think we need to start thinking about dopamine.

## You don't need a dopamine detox

But let's get one thing straight. You don't need a “dopamine detox”. Dopamine isn't a toxin that you use up by looking at Instagram and then replenish by sitting in a dark room for 24 hours. It is a normal and important neurotransmitter involved in motivation, learning, movement, reward and many other functions.

The more interesting question is what we are repeatedly training our brains to do.

Our brains are remarkably adaptable and change in response to what we repeatedly practise. This is part of what we mean when we talk about neuroplasticity. If you spend your day constantly switching between different things, your brain becomes very practised at switching. A notification arrives, you look at a message, have a quick look at social media, return to work, see another headline, watch a short video and then try to return to what you were doing.

We are constantly interrupting ourselves, and then we wonder why sitting down and concentrating on one thing for an extended period can sometimes feel difficult.

What we practise matters.

If we repeatedly give our brains tiny bursts of novelty and stimulation, ordinary life can start to feel comparatively slow. A ten-minute wait can feel unbearable, a walk without a podcast can feel boring, eating dinner without watching something can feel strangely quiet and sitting with our own thoughts can become uncomfortable.

So we reach for the phone again, and the cycle continues.

## Being comfortable doing nothing

I think this is one of the things we have lost: being comfortable doing nothing.

I'm not talking about another productivity technique or telling you that you need to meditate for an hour every day. I mean simply sitting outside with a cup of tea, going for a walk without your headphones, driving without a podcast, standing in a queue without getting your phone out or looking out of a window and letting your mind wander.

Sometimes our best thoughts come when we aren't actively trying to think.

When we give ourselves some space, our minds can wander, make connections, process things and reflect on what is happening in our lives. We don't necessarily need to fill every quiet moment with information or entertainment. In fact, perhaps we need more of those quiet moments.

So try reducing the stimulation.

You don't have to throw your phone away. Technology is incredibly useful and, like most people, I use it every day. The point isn't to become anti-technology. The point is to make sure that **you are using your technology rather than your technology using you.**

## Start small

Start small.

Turn off unnecessary notifications. Your phone doesn't need to tell you every time someone posts something or that something new has happened somewhere in the world. Move social media apps away from your home screen so that accessing them requires a little more intention. If there is an app you don't actually need, consider deleting it.

Try having your morning coffee without your phone. Don't automatically reach for it when you wake up. Get outside instead. Give yourself some time before you allow the outside world to start demanding your attention.

Go for a walk without a podcast or music and just walk. See what you notice. Listen to what is happening around you. Allow your mind to wander.

Try eating a meal without watching television or scrolling. Have a conversation without putting your phone on the table. Create a period in the evening where your phone is simply somewhere else.

## Learn to be bored again

And perhaps most importantly, learn to tolerate being bored again.

Try sitting for ten minutes without doing anything. No phone, no television, no music and no podcast. Just sit and see what happens.

You might be surprised how quickly your brain starts asking for stimulation. That is okay. You don't have to respond to it. Let the thought come and go. See where your mind wanders.

Over time, you may find that you become more comfortable with periods of quiet and that you don't automatically need to fill every spare moment with something.

This isn't about becoming perfectly productive or removing all entertainment from your life. It is about creating some balance and remembering that your attention is yours.

## Your attention is yours

Your attention is one of the most valuable things you have. Where your attention goes, your time follows, and where your time goes, much of your life follows.

There will always be another notification, another video, another headline, another argument and another thing that apparently needs your immediate attention.

It doesn't.

You are allowed to be unavailable. You are allowed to be bored. You are allowed to sit with your thoughts. You are allowed to do nothing.

You don't need to detox from dopamine. Perhaps you simply need to give your brain a little less to process and a little more time to think.

# Breathwork

#### From ancient breathing traditions to modern neuroscience, discover how different breathing techniques can influence stress, energy, mood and the nervous system.

Breathing is something we do around 20,000 times a day, mostly without thinking. Yet it is one of the few functions of the body that operates automatically while also being under our conscious control.

For thousands of years, traditions including Ayurveda, yoga, meditation and religious practices have used the breath to influence the mind and body. Today, science is beginning to explain why changing the way we breathe can have such a noticeable effect on how we feel.

Breathing is closely connected to the autonomic nervous system, which helps regulate functions such as heart rate, blood pressure and digestion. It also influences heart rate variability (HRV) — the natural variation in time between individual heartbeats. HRV is affected by many factors, including sleep, fitness, stress and recovery, and is often used as one indicator of how the body is adapting to demands.

One reason breathing is so interesting is that it provides a direct route into this system. Slow, steady breathing can synchronise changes in breathing and heart rate, often increasing HRV in the moment and supporting the body's relaxation response. Regular practice may also help with emotional regulation and recovering from stress. The wider health benefits are still being researched, but the connection between breathing, the nervous system and cardiovascular regulation is an exciting area of study.

Different breathing patterns can produce different responses. Some help us relax; others deliberately stimulate the body and increase alertness. Here are four techniques you could explore.

## 4 Breathing Techniques

### 1. The Physiological Sigh

A simple technique for calming the body, the physiological sigh involves taking two consecutive inhales through the nose — the second a shorter top-up breath — followed by a long, slow exhale through the mouth.

The double inhale helps reinflate tiny air sacs in the lungs, while the longer exhale encourages a calmer breathing rhythm. It can be a useful way to settle yourself when feeling stressed or overwhelmed.

**Try it:** Repeat for one to five minutes, keeping the breaths comfortable rather than forcing them.

### 2. Slow or Coherent Breathing

This involves breathing slowly and steadily, often at around five to six breaths per minute. It can help coordinate breathing with the natural fluctuations in heart rate, supporting relaxation and emotional regulation.

**Try it:** Breathe in gently for five seconds and out for five seconds. Continue for around five minutes, adjusting the pace to remain comfortable.

### 3. Box Breathing

Often used by athletes and in high-pressure environments, box breathing provides a simple structure for bringing attention back to the breath.

It involves four equal stages: inhale, hold, exhale and hold again.

**Try it:** Inhale for four seconds, hold for four, exhale for four, then hold for four. Repeat for a few minutes. Shorten the counts if you feel uncomfortable; there is no need to force the breath holds.

### 4. Cyclic Hyperventilation

This more stimulating approach involves repeated rounds of deeper, faster breathing followed by a period of breath retention. It is used in some breathwork practices, including methods popularised by Wim Hof.

Unlike slower techniques, this deliberately activates the body's stress response. It can produce a temporary feeling of energy or alertness, sometimes accompanied by tingling or light-headedness.

**Try it with caution:** This is an intense technique, not a necessary part of everyday breathwork. Practise only in a safe, seated or lying position, never in or near water, while driving, or anywhere that fainting could cause injury. Avoid it if you have relevant health conditions unless you've discussed it with a healthcare professional.

## Which technique could you choose?

- **To settle stress:** Physiological sigh
- **For calm and relaxation:** Slow or coherent breathing
- **For focus and composure:** Box breathing
- **For a stimulating experience:** Cyclic hyperventilation

## The Science Behind Breathwork

Breathing affects the autonomic nervous system, which has two broadly opposing branches: the sympathetic system, associated with alertness and the stress response, and the parasympathetic system, associated with rest and recovery.

The vagus nerve is an important part of this connection. During a slow exhale, activity through the vagus nerve can help slow the heart rate. This contributes to respiratory sinus arrhythmia — the natural rise and fall in heart rate as we breathe in and out. Slow, rhythmic breathing can amplify this pattern and increase HRV during practice.

**Why the physiological sigh works**

There are two interesting parts to its physiology:

- **The lungs:** The second inhale helps reinflate alveoli — tiny air sacs where oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged. This can help improve the distribution of air through the lungs.
- **Blood chemistry:** Breathing out removes carbon dioxide. Because carbon dioxide contributes to the formation of carbonic acid in the blood, changes in breathing can produce small, temporary shifts in blood pH. The longer exhale also supports the calming effects associated with slower breathing.

A 2023 study found that brief daily breathing practices improved mood and reduced physiological arousal. Cyclic sighing, in particular, produced encouraging improvements in positive mood and reductions in breathing rate.

**What happens during cyclic hyperventilation?**

Rapid, deep breathing removes more carbon dioxide than the body produces. This lowers blood CO₂ levels, temporarily raising blood pH — a state known as respiratory alkalosis. It can also constrict blood vessels in the brain, contributing to sensations such as dizziness and tingling.

Research into the Wim Hof Method has found that intensive breathing practices can produce a marked increase in adrenaline. One well-known study also observed changes in inflammatory responses during an immune challenge. However, participants combined breathing with meditation and cold exposure, so the effects cannot be attributed to breathing alone. The study did not find a significant rise in cortisol during the breathing exercise.

## A Note on Safety

Gentle breathing exercises are generally accessible, but intensive hyperventilation and breath-holding practices carry additional risks. They can cause dizziness, fainting and loss of consciousness. Never practise them in water, while driving, standing, at height or anywhere a loss of consciousness could be dangerous. People who are pregnant or have epilepsy, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular conditions, or significant respiratory problems should seek medical advice before trying intensive techniques. Stop if you experience chest pain, severe dizziness or feel faint.

Breathwork is not a replacement for medical treatment, but it can be a useful way to build awareness of the body's responses and experiment with simple tools for managing stress and energy.

# Flow Rope

*Two alternative ways to build on the movement you already do, challenging coordination, balance and body awareness while exploring new ways to move.*

## Building on the Movement You Already Do

Walking, running, strength training and everyday activity provide a great foundation for health. But movement doesn't have to stop there. Exploring different patterns and directions can introduce new challenges for the body and brain, helping develop coordination, balance, mobility and body awareness.

Backward walking and flow rope are two interesting examples. They aren't replacements for established forms of exercise, but can be a useful evolution of an already active lifestyle — adding variety and encouraging the body to move in ways it may be less accustomed to.

## Backward Walking

Walking backwards might seem simple, but it changes the demands placed on the body. It challenges balance and coordination, encourages different muscle recruitment patterns and asks the brain to process movement in a less familiar way.

Research has explored backward walking as an addition to rehabilitation for knee osteoarthritis, with some studies finding improvements in pain, mobility and physical function. There is also early research into its potential role in balance and gait training, although more evidence is needed.

**How to explore it:** Start slowly on a flat, clear surface, taking small steps and building confidence before increasing your pace. Avoid roads, stairs and uneven ground.

## Flow Rope

Flow rope uses a lightweight rope to create continuous, rhythmic patterns around the body. Through repeated movements, it challenges timing, hand-eye coordination, rhythm and whole-body awareness.

Many movements involve the hips and torso working together with the shoulders and arms. This encourages coordinated movement through the spine and pelvis, while learning new patterns gives the brain an opportunity to develop coordination and motor skills.

The practice can also feel meditative. Following the rhythm of the rope encourages attention to the present moment, making it an enjoyable way to combine movement with focus.

Movement practitioner **Lawrence van Lingen** explores this idea through his approach to movement, emphasising relaxed, fluid patterns that begin around the body's centre and connect the hips, spine and limbs.

You can explore flow rope on YouTube, where there are many beginner demonstrations to follow.

**How to explore it:** Begin with a lightweight rope and simple movements, such as a relaxed figure-eight pattern. Focus on smooth, comfortable movement rather than speed or complexity. Allow the hips and torso to move naturally, without forcing rotation.

## Deep Dive: Understanding the Posterior Chain

The posterior chain describes the interconnected muscles along the back of the body. It includes the calves, hamstrings, glutes and muscles supporting the spine. Together, these muscles help extend the hips, support an upright posture, stabilise the pelvis and transfer force through the body.

These muscles are important in everyday activities such as walking, climbing stairs, running and lifting. They also work alongside the muscles at the front of the body to maintain balance and control.

Backward walking and flow rope offer different ways to explore this system. Backward walking changes the demands on the legs and challenges the muscles involved in controlling each step. Flow rope encourages coordinated movement between the hips, pelvis, spine and upper body, with the posterior chain helping provide stability and control.

Neither practice is necessarily a substitute for targeted strength training, but they can complement it by introducing different movement patterns, coordination demands and opportunities to develop body awareness.

## What Does the Science Tell Us?

Backward walking has a growing research base, particularly in rehabilitation. Studies suggest it may help improve pain, strength and physical function when added to conventional knee osteoarthritis programmes. Early research also suggests possible benefits for balance and gait, although the evidence is still developing.

Flow rope itself has received very little direct scientific study. However, research into motor learning, coordination and rhythmic movement helps explain why learning unfamiliar movement patterns may be valuable. Practising new sequences challenges the nervous system to coordinate muscles, timing and sensory information.

## A Note on Safety

Backward walking is best practised on a clear, level surface, away from traffic and obstacles. Anyone with balance difficulties may benefit from support or supervision. With flow rope, start gently, leave plenty of space and avoid forcing movements through the spine or shoulders. Stop if you experience pain, dizziness or discomfort.

The evidence is more established for backward walking in certain rehabilitation settings than for flow rope specifically. Both can be enjoyable ways to explore movement, but their wider health benefits are still being investigated.

# Sitting on the Floor

#### Encouraging Natural Mobility, Strength and Postural Awareness

Sitting on the floor instead of relying exclusively on chairs encourages greater flexibility, joint mobility and postural awareness. Regularly changing my seated position – from cross-legged to kneeling or a deep squat – keeps my body engaged, encourages movement through different ranges of motion and helps prevent stiffness.

Unlike sitting in a chair, floor sitting requires us to continually make small adjustments to maintain balance and comfort. This naturally engages the muscles around the hips, pelvis and trunk, helping to develop core stability and improve postural control.

## The Importance of Getting Down – and Getting Back Up

One of the most overlooked benefits of regularly sitting on the floor is maintaining the ability to get down to ground level and, importantly, get back up again without assistance.

As we age, muscle mass and strength naturally decline, a process known as sarcopenia. This can affect our balance, mobility and ability to perform everyday activities. Maintaining lower-body strength, particularly in the hips, thighs and legs, becomes increasingly important for preserving independence.

Getting up from the floor requires a combination of leg strength, core stability, balance, coordination and joint mobility. Regularly practising this movement helps us maintain these abilities and provides a useful way of assessing our functional fitness.

The core muscles also play an important role. They help stabilise the spine and pelvis while we shift our weight, rotate, push through our limbs and transition between sitting, kneeling, squatting and standing.

Being able to get up from the floor may seem like a simple thing, but it represents something much bigger: the ability to remain physically capable and independent as we get older.

## A Simple Habit Worth Developing

Try incorporating some floor sitting into your daily routine – perhaps while reading, relaxing or spending time with family. Explore different positions, including cross-legged sitting, kneeling and squatting, and occasionally practise getting down and back up again.

Start with positions and movements that feel comfortable, using a stable support if needed, and gradually build confidence and ability.

The key is variety rather than holding one position for too long. Alternating between sitting, squatting, kneeling, standing and walking helps prevent prolonged periods of inactivity while keeping the body accustomed to moving naturally.

Sometimes, improving our movement doesn't require more complicated exercise. It simply means rediscovering the natural movements our bodies were designed to perform – and maintaining the strength and mobility to keep doing them throughout life.

## A Note on Safety

The most important rule is simple: **don't do anything that could put yourself at risk.** Floor sitting, getting down to the ground and getting back up should feel manageable, not painful or frightening. Work at a pace that suits your body today, and stop if anything hurts.

- **If getting down or up is difficult for you right now, work with a health care professional.** Your GP, a physiotherapist or another appropriately qualified professional can look at what is limiting you and suggest safe, progressive ways to build strength, balance and mobility.
- **Protect your joints:** If you have knee, hip, ankle or lower-back problems, or have had a joint replacement, avoid forcing deep squats or cross-legged positions, and seek advice from a physiotherapist or healthcare professional.
- **Raise your hips:** Sitting on a firm cushion, bolster or yoga block can make floor sitting more comfortable and reduce strain on the lower back and hips.
- **Support your transitions:** Use sturdy furniture, such as a solid chair or sofa, for support when getting down to or up from the floor until you feel stable and confident.
- **Rise gradually:** Stand up slowly to reduce the risk of dizziness or light-headedness.
- **Never push through pain:** A gentle stretch is normal, but sharp joint pain, tingling or numbness is a signal to stop.

# The Power of Compound Movement

#### Why multi-joint resistance training builds real-world strength, joint resilience, and functional longevity.

Maintaining lean muscle mass and bone mineral density is one of the clearest markers of healthy ageing and metabolic resilience. As the decades advance, natural age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia) accelerates unless we regularly challenge our muscular and skeletal systems with resistance.

Yet much of modern gym culture remains focused on isolation – sitting on padded machines and curling or pressing along fixed tracks to target a single muscle.

Real life does not move along fixed tracks. Daily life requires picking up children or groceries from the floor, carrying heavy bags while walking, reaching overhead, stabilising against a sudden slip, and getting up easily from the ground. To prepare for everyday demands, our training needs to mirror everyday mechanics. This is the role of compound movement.

## What Is a Compound Movement?

A compound exercise is any movement that requires **multiple joints and muscle groups to work together in a coordinated chain**.

Rather than isolating the bicep or quadricep, compound movements engage entire functional systems:

- **Whole-body coordination:** Muscles do not work as solitary islands. Compound patterns teach the nervous system to coordinate the core, hips, back, and limbs as an integrated unit.
- **Joint and connective tissue resilience:** Distributing load across several joints stimulates tendons, ligaments, and bone density naturally, rather than concentrating repetitive stress on a single joint.
- **Metabolic & cardiovascular efficiency:** Recruiting large muscle masses demands substantial energy, supporting insulin sensitivity, cardiovascular conditioning, and mitochondrial vitality in less training time.
- **Direct carry-over to everyday life:** The strength built through multi-joint movement directly translates into effortless daily tasks and independence as we age.

## The Foundational Movement Patterns

Effective functional training is not about collecting dozens of exercises; it revolves around five fundamental human movement patterns:

1. **The Hip Hinge:** The foundation of posterior-chain strength (glutes, hamstrings, and spinal support). Hinging deeply at the hips rather than rounding the lower back protects the spine and underpins safe lifting.
2. **The Squat:** Preserving hip, knee, and ankle mobility under load, ensuring you retain the strength to stand up effortlessly throughout life.
3. **The Push & Pull:** Moving weight away from or toward your torso – both horizontally and vertically – to support upper-body posture, shoulder health, and back stability.
4. **The Loaded Carry:** Simply walking upright under load (such as a farmer's walk) builds robust grip strength, core bracing, and postural endurance with minimal joint impact.
5. **Ground-to-Standing:** The ability to lower yourself to the floor and get back up independently is a proven clinical marker of long-term mobility and vitality.

## Tools for Compound Training: Where Kettlebells Fit In

Compound patterns can be performed with body weight, barbells, or dumbbells. However, **kettlebells** are uniquely suited to functional training at home:

- **Offset centre of mass:** Because a kettlebell's weight sits outside the handle, your core and stabilizing muscles must constantly work to maintain balance and control.
- **Dynamic & fluid motion:** Kettlebells excel at rhythmic, fluid transitions – such as moving from a squat into a press, or controlling dynamic hip-hinge patterns.
- **Compact and versatile:** A single bell allows you to train hinges, squats, carries, and rotational core stability without the need for large equipment.

> **Important safety note:** Because compound and free-weight movements require multi-joint coordination and spinal control, correct technique is essential. Always master unweighted movement patterns first. Before adding load or dynamic momentum – especially with kettlebells – seek hands-on instruction from a certified coach, personal trainer, or physiotherapist to ensure your form is sound and your joints are protected.

## Things to Consider Before Starting

- **Individual health and history:** Any past or present medical condition, spinal concern, joint pain, or cardiovascular issue warrants professional clearance before beginning resistance work.
- **Quality over load:** More weight is never worth poor form. If your posture deteriorates or your movement breaks down, stop the set immediately.
- **Progress gradually:** Begin with simple bodyweight patterns and light, controlled loads before introducing more complex or dynamic variations.

# Childrens Nutrition

Walk through the supermarket and you'll find shelves of food designed specifically for children. Brightly coloured cereals, yoghurt pouches, snack bars and fruit-flavoured treats all promise to support growing bodies.

Many carry claims about added vitamins, calcium, fibre or real fruit. But how much do these claims tell us about the overall quality of the food?

A product can contain added nutrients and still be highly processed or high in sugar. With so much choice and clever marketing, it's not always easy to tell what we're really buying.

So, what should parents know about ultra-processed foods, and why does it matter during childhood?

## How much ultra-processed food are children eating?

The figures are quite startling.

A UK study published in 2024 found that ultra-processed foods (UPFs) provided **46.9% of calories at 21 months**, rising to **59.4% by age seven**.

That's almost six in every ten calories coming from UPFs in the seven-year-olds studied.

Common examples included yoghurts, breakfast cereals and breads, as well as sweet cereals and puddings. Children with higher UPF intakes also tended to consume more free sugars and sodium.

These figures come from a particular UK cohort, rather than a survey of every child in the country, but they show just how early highly processed foods can become a major part of children's diets.

## What exactly are ultra-processed foods?

Not all processing is bad. Freezing vegetables, pasteurising milk and making yoghurt are all forms of food processing, and can be part of a healthy diet.

Ultra-processed foods are different. The NOVA classification describes them as industrial formulations often made with refined ingredients and additives such as flavourings, colours, emulsifiers and sweeteners.

Examples can include fizzy drinks, confectionery, many packaged snacks, some breakfast cereals and instant meals.

Importantly, not all UPFs have the same nutritional value, and the presence of an additive doesn't automatically mean a food is harmful. The classification looks at how food is made, not simply whether it is healthy or unhealthy.

But when these products make up such a large part of children's diets, it's worth asking what they are replacing.

## Why childhood nutrition matters

Childhood is a period of rapid growth and development. Food provides the energy and building blocks needed for growing bodies, developing brains and healthy digestion.

A varied diet helps provide protein, healthy fats, fibre and the many vitamins and minerals children need. Fruit, vegetables, pulses, wholegrains, eggs, fish, meat and dairy foods all have a part to play.

A diet dominated by highly processed products can leave less room for this variety.

Researchers are also investigating how dietary patterns influence the gut microbiome – the community of microorganisms living in our digestive system. Different plant foods provide fibres that help nourish gut bacteria, which is another reason to encourage variety.

## What is the research showing about children's wellbeing?

Research into UPFs is increasingly looking beyond weight and dental health.

A Canadian study published in 2026 followed more than 2,000 children, examining their diets at age three and their emotional and behavioural development at age five.

UPFs provided around **45.5% of the children's energy intake**. Higher consumption was associated with more emotional and behavioural difficulties, including symptoms relating to anxiety, withdrawal and hyperactivity.

The researchers also modelled replacing 10% of energy from UPFs with minimally processed foods. This was associated with better behavioural and emotional outcomes.

The study cannot prove that UPFs caused these difficulties. However, it adds to growing research exploring how early dietary patterns may influence children's health and development.

Other studies have linked higher UPF consumption with poorer metabolic health, including concerns around excess weight and blood sugar regulation. This is an evolving area of research, but one that deserves attention.

## The influence of food marketing

Children's food is big business. Cartoon characters, bright packaging, brand partnerships and carefully worded health claims all help sell products.

A cereal can be fortified with vitamins and still contain plenty of sugar. A fruit-flavoured snack may sound wholesome while containing very little actual fruit.

The scale of advertising is striking. A 2026 UK parliamentary report found that around **£196 million** was spent advertising sweets, chocolate and crisps between August 2024 and July 2025, compared with just **£19 million** for fruit and vegetables.

That's more than ten times as much spent promoting these less healthy foods.

Parents are making choices in an environment where heavily marketed products are highly visible and often designed for convenience. The responsibility shouldn't rest entirely on families to navigate this. Food companies have considerable influence over what children see, request and come to regard as normal food.

## Making sense of children's food labels

One simple habit is to look beyond the front of the packet.

Check the ingredients and sugar content, and don't be distracted by claims about added vitamins or minerals. They don't tell the whole story.

It's also worth paying attention to products specifically marketed for children. A children's version isn't automatically healthier than the adult equivalent. Some contain surprisingly high amounts of sugar, so comparing labels can be revealing.

For example, plain natural yoghurt may contain little more than milk and live cultures, while a children's yoghurt can include added sugar, flavourings and fruit preparations. Check the label rather than assuming the children's version is the better choice.

## Returning to simple, nourishing foods

Children don't need elaborate products with cartoon characters and impressive health claims. A varied diet built around familiar foods is a good foundation.

Think of simple options such as:

- Eggs, fish, chicken, meat, beans and lentils.

- A variety of fresh, frozen or tinned fruit and vegetables.

- Oats, wholegrains and other fibre-rich foods.

- Plain natural yoghurt and traditional kefir.

- Healthy fats from foods such as olive oil, avocado and oily fish.

Plain yoghurt with berries or mashed banana, porridge with fruit, scrambled eggs or homemade soup can all be useful alternatives to heavily marketed snacks.

The aim isn't to make every meal perfect, or to turn food into another source of parental pressure. It's simply to make nourishing everyday foods a familiar part of family life.

**A note on supplements:** This article is about food choices, not a recommendation to give children additional vitamins, minerals or other supplements. Children's needs vary with age, diet and individual circumstances. Follow relevant public health guidance and seek advice from a GP, health visitor or other qualified healthcare professional if you're unsure.

## A balanced approach

This isn't about eliminating every packaged food or expecting families to prepare everything from scratch. Convenience has its place, and occasional treats are part of normal family life.

What matters is the overall pattern of eating: whether children are getting a good variety of foods, and whether highly processed products are taking up so much space that more nourishing options are being pushed aside.

Being aware of food marketing and learning to read labels can help parents make more informed choices.

Sometimes, the simplest foods are still the best place to start.

# L Reuteri

Could one tiny bacterium influence our gut, our hormones and even the way we connect with other people?

It might sound far-fetched, but researchers are discovering some fascinating things about *Limosilactobacillus reuteri*, better known as *L. reuteri*.

This bacterium has been found in human breast milk, is now rarely detected in many industrialised populations, and is being studied for its surprising links with oxytocin and serotonin.

So what's so special about this little microbe?

## Where have all the L. reuteri bacteria gone?

*L. reuteri* is a bacterium found in humans and many other mammals. It has been isolated from human breast milk, suggesting it may be part of the microbial community shared between mother and baby.

Yet researchers have found that it is now rarely detected, or present at very low levels, in some modern industrialised populations.

Why? We don't know for certain. Changes in diet, antibiotic exposure, lifestyle and the wider environment may all have played a part. It's an intriguing reminder that the microbial world inside us is not necessarily the same as it was for previous generations.

## The oxytocin connection

This is where the story gets particularly interesting.

Oxytocin is often called the *love hormone* because of its role in bonding, trust and social connection. It also has wider functions in the body, including effects on digestion, stress responses and tissue repair.

Research has suggested that *L. reuteri* may influence oxytocin signalling.

A 2023 study found that *L. reuteri* stimulated oxytocin release from human intestinal tissue and laboratory-grown human gut models. The researchers identified a pathway involving secretin, a gut hormone that helped trigger the release.

Other research, particularly in animal models, has linked *L. reuteri* with social behaviour and wound healing.

This doesn't mean taking a probiotic will automatically make us feel more connected or improve our relationships. But it does reveal a fascinating possible link between our gut microbes and the hormones involved in how we function and interact.

## And then there's serotonin…

Serotonin is another important chemical messenger, often associated with mood and emotional wellbeing.

Around 90% of the body's serotonin is produced in the gut. It's important to remember, though, that serotonin made in the gut does not simply travel into the brain. The two systems are connected in more complex ways.

A 2025 laboratory study found that *L. reuteri* influenced the release of serotonin and other hormones from intestinal cell models. The researchers studied particular strains and their effects on gut hormone signalling.

We're still at an early stage of understanding what this means for human health, but it adds another interesting piece to the gut-brain puzzle.

## Can we bring L. reuteri back?

The microbiologist and author Dr William Davis has helped bring attention to *L. reuteri*, particularly through his book *Super Gut*. He explores ways of making fermented yoghurt using selected bacterial strains, including particular strains of *L. reuteri*.

These preparations are not the same as ordinary yoghurt, and the results depend on the strain, preparation and fermentation conditions. Research on one strain doesn't automatically apply to every product labelled *L. reuteri*.

It's an area of growing interest, but it's important to distinguish promising early research from proven health benefits.

## Small bacterium, big questions

The more we learn about the gut microbiome, the more we appreciate that these tiny organisms do much more than help digest food.

*L. reuteri* is a particularly fascinating example. From its presence in human breast milk to its emerging links with oxytocin and serotonin, it raises some remarkable questions about the relationship between our microbes, our hormones and our wider wellbeing.

We're only just scratching the surface of what lives inside us – and what it might be doing.

# Gut Brain Connection

We tend to think of the brain as the body's control centre. But there's another system quietly sending it messages all day long: your gut.

That familiar feeling of butterflies before a big event, or a churning stomach when you're stressed, is no coincidence. Your digestive system and brain are in constant conversation, sharing information that can influence digestion, appetite, stress responses and even mood.

Scientists call this communication network the **gut–brain axis**. And research increasingly suggests that gut health and mental wellbeing are closely connected.

## Your gut has a mind of its own

Your gut contains an extensive network of nerve cells called the **enteric nervous system (ENS)**. Sometimes described as the body's "second brain", it contains around 100–200 million neurons.

These nerve cells coordinate digestion, controlling the movement of food and the release of digestive secretions. The ENS can manage much of this work independently, but it also communicates constantly with the brain and spinal cord.

## A two-way conversation

The gut–brain connection works in both directions.

One important communication route is the **vagus nerve**, which carries signals between the brain and digestive system. But it's not the only route. Messages also travel through other nerves, hormones, immune-system signals and chemicals produced by gut microbes.

This means the brain can influence digestion, while activity in the gut can send signals that affect how the brain responds.

## Why stress affects your stomach

Have you ever lost your appetite before an important event, felt nauseous with nerves or experienced digestive discomfort during a stressful period?

When the brain detects stress, it activates systems that prepare the body to respond. These changes can affect gut movement, digestive secretions and how sensitive the gut feels. Stress can also influence the gut lining, immune activity and the balance of microbes living there.

For some people, this means a faster-moving digestive system; for others, it can mean slower digestion, bloating or discomfort.

The communication works the other way, too. Signals travelling from the gut can influence brain areas involved in emotion and stress responses.

## Gut health and mental health: what does the evidence show?

The connection is more than a theory. Research has found striking associations between digestive conditions and mental health.

People with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), for example, experience anxiety and depressive symptoms more often than those without the condition. Researchers are also finding differences in gut bacteria among people with depression, anxiety and other psychiatric conditions.

These findings don't prove that gut problems cause mental health conditions. The relationship may work both ways: mental health can affect digestion, appetite and gut function, while signals from the gut may influence the brain.

But the growing body of research suggests that **gut health may be one influence on mental wellbeing**, and an important part of the wider picture.

## Your gut microbes are part of the conversation

Your gut is home to a vast community of bacteria and other microorganisms, collectively known as the **gut microbiome**.

These microbes help break down parts of food we can't digest ourselves, interact with the immune system and produce substances that influence the gut and wider body.

One important group is **short-chain fatty acids**, produced when gut bacteria ferment certain types of dietary fibre. Butyrate is one example. It provides energy for cells lining the colon and helps support the gut's protective barrier. Short-chain fatty acids also influence immune activity.

Scientists are exploring how these microbial products and other gut-derived signals may affect the brain through nerve, hormonal and immune pathways.

## What about serotonin – the 'happy chemical'?

Serotonin is a chemical messenger involved in mood, sleep, appetite and digestion. Around 90% of the body's serotonin is found in the gut, where it helps regulate digestive activity.

But gut-produced serotonin doesn't simply travel into the brain. The brain makes and regulates its own serotonin, and serotonin circulating in the body cannot readily cross the blood–brain barrier.

So, while gut serotonin is important, producing more of it in the gut doesn't automatically increase serotonin in the brain. The relationship is more complex, involving several chemical and nerve signalling pathways.

## The gut, inflammation and mental wellbeing

The gut is closely connected to the immune system. Its lining acts as a protective barrier, while gut microbes and immune cells help maintain a healthy balance.

When this system is disrupted, immune signalling and inflammation can change. Research increasingly suggests that inflammation may play a role in mental health. Studies have found links between inflammatory activity and conditions such as depression, highlighting a possible connection between the gut, immune system and brain.

Scientists are now exploring how changes in gut health and immune activity may influence brain function and mental wellbeing.

## Why the gut–brain connection matters

The gut and brain are part of an interconnected network, constantly exchanging information through nerves, hormones, immune signals and microbial activity.

This helps explain why stress can affect digestion, why gut health is receiving growing attention in mental health research, and why scientists are increasingly interested in the microbiome.

**The conversation between gut and brain is happening all the time. Understanding it is helping us build a broader picture of health – one that recognises just how connected the body really is.**

# Eating for a Healthy Mind

Food supports the brain in two important ways:

1. **Directly, through energy and nutrients.** The brain needs a constant supply of energy to function, along with nutrients that help build and maintain brain cells, support chemical messengers and regulate processes involved in mood, memory and concentration.

2. **Indirectly, through the gut microbiome.** The food we eat helps shape the community of microbes living in our gut. These microbes produce substances that support the gut lining, influence immune activity and communicate with the brain through the gut–brain axis.

Both pathways matter. What we eat helps fuel the brain itself, while also supporting the wider systems that influence brain health and mental wellbeing.

## Why a whole-food diet is a good place to start

A varied, whole-food diet provides a broad range of nutrients and natural plant compounds, without relying heavily on highly processed foods. Vegetables, fruit, beans, lentils, nuts, seeds, fish and eggs all bring something different to the table.

Eating a variety of these foods helps provide the protein, healthy fats, vitamins and minerals the brain needs, as well as the fibre and plant compounds that support gut health.

Rather than looking for one special food or nutrient, it's the overall pattern — and the variety within it — that matters.

## Your gut microbes need feeding too

Your gut is home to trillions of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi and other microbes. Together, they form the gut microbiome — a living community that helps with digestion, produces useful substances and interacts with the immune system.

And, just like us, these microbes need food.

Many beneficial gut bacteria thrive on the fibres and plant compounds found in beans, lentils, chickpeas, peas, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds. Different microbes use different types of fibre and plant compounds, so eating a wide variety of plant foods helps support a diverse microbiome.

When gut bacteria break down these fibres, they produce substances called short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate. These help nourish the cells lining the gut, support the gut barrier and influence immune activity. Other microbial products can also affect metabolism and send signals through pathways involved in communication between the gut and brain.

### What happens when the balance is disrupted?

The microbiome is influenced by many things, including diet, medication, illness, stress, sleep and our environment. When its composition or activity becomes disrupted — often described as *dysbiosis* — some beneficial microbes may become less abundant, while other organisms or microbial activities become more dominant.

This can change the substances microbes produce, affect the gut barrier and influence the way the immune system responds. In some circumstances, it may contribute to increased inflammation and altered signalling between the gut and brain.

Researchers have found differences in gut microbiome composition in people with conditions such as depression and anxiety. Some studies have identified changes in bacteria involved in producing short-chain fatty acids and other potentially important compounds.

We don't yet know whether these microbial changes contribute to mental health conditions, result from them, or reflect other influences such as diet, medication and stress. The relationship is likely to work in several directions.

It's an important area of research because it helps us understand how food, gut microbes, the immune system and the brain may be connected.

## Foods that support brain health

Some foods are particularly interesting because they provide nutrients and natural compounds involved in brain function.

**Oily fish**

Salmon, sardines, mackerel, trout and herring provide omega-3 fats, particularly EPA and DHA. DHA is an important structural component of brain cell membranes, while EPA is involved in signalling and inflammatory processes.

**Nuts and seeds**

Walnuts, flaxseeds and chia seeds provide plant-based omega-3 fat called ALA. The body can convert some ALA into EPA and DHA, although this conversion is limited. Nuts and seeds also provide fibre, minerals, vitamin E and other plant compounds.

**Berries and colourful fruit**

Blueberries, strawberries, raspberries and other colourful fruits contain polyphenols, including anthocyanins. These plant compounds are being studied for their potential effects on blood vessel function, inflammation and brain-cell signalling. Fruit also provides fibre and a range of vitamins.

**Leafy greens and other vegetables**

Spinach, kale, broccoli and other green vegetables provide folate, vitamin K and carotenoids, alongside fibre and other plant compounds. Eating a variety of vegetables gives us access to their different nutritional qualities.

**Eggs**

Eggs provide choline, which the body uses to make acetylcholine, a chemical messenger involved in memory and muscle control. They also contain vitamin B12 and high-quality protein.

**Olive oil, tea and cocoa**

Extra virgin olive oil contains monounsaturated fats and polyphenols. Tea provides flavonoids, while cocoa contains flavanols that have been studied for their effects on blood flow and vascular health.

## The Mediterranean and MIND diets

The Mediterranean and MIND diets are two eating patterns that have attracted interest in research into mental wellbeing and brain health. Both emphasise a variety of plant foods, healthy fats and minimally processed ingredients.

You can read more about the [Mediterranean diet](https://www.bhf.org.uk/informationsupport/heart-matters-magazine/nutrition/mediterranean-diet) and the [MIND diet](https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/what-is-the-mind-diet-2019031316195).

## Does processed food matter?

Not all processed foods are the same. Frozen vegetables, tinned beans and plain yoghurt are processed, but can be nutritious and convenient.

Ultra-processed foods are a different category. Many are formulated to be convenient, highly palatable and easy to overeat. Diets high in these foods can leave less room for a varied range of nourishing, fibre-rich foods.

Studies have found associations between higher ultra-processed food intake and poorer mental health, including depression and anxiety. But this doesn't mean every processed food is harmful, or that these studies prove one causes the other.

## Food is part of the bigger picture

Nutrition is one of several foundations of brain health and mental wellbeing, alongside sleep, physical activity, relationships, stress and our wider life circumstances.

A varied, whole-food diet helps provide the energy and nutrients the brain needs, while supporting the gut microbiome and the many systems that influence our health. It's not about individual superfoods or getting every meal right, but the benefits that can build from the way we eat over time.

## The Science: What does the research tell us?

### Dietary patterns and depression

A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis found that people who followed healthier dietary patterns tended to have a lower risk of depressive outcomes. The findings show an association rather than proof that diet prevents depression.

Lassale C et al. (2019). *Healthy dietary indices and risk of depressive outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies.* Molecular Psychiatry. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-018-0237-8

### Can changing diet improve depression?

The SMILES trial, published in 2017, explored whether improving diet could help people experiencing major depression. Participants receiving dietary support were encouraged to follow a modified Mediterranean-style diet, with more vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts, fish and olive oil, and fewer highly processed foods.

After 12 weeks, 32.3% of people in the dietary support group experienced remission from depression, compared with 8% in the social-support control group. It was a relatively small trial, but an important finding: improving diet may be a useful addition to mental health care for some people, rather than a replacement for established treatment.

Jacka FN et al. (2017). *A randomised controlled trial of dietary improvement for adults with major depression (the SMILES trial).* BMC Medicine. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-017-0791-y

### Ultra-processed foods and mental health

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis found that higher ultra-processed food intake was associated with a greater risk of depressive and anxiety symptoms. However, most of the available studies were observational, so they cannot establish that ultra-processed foods directly cause mental health problems.

Lane MM et al. (2022). *Ultra-processed food consumption and mental health: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* https://doi.org/10.1080/1028415X.2022.2110188

### The MIND diet and cognitive health

Observational research has linked closer adherence to the MIND diet with a lower risk of cognitive decline. However, a large three-year randomised trial published in 2023 did not find a statistically significant difference in cognitive decline between people following the MIND diet and those in the control group. Research is ongoing.

Barnes LL et al. (2023). *Trial of the MIND Diet for Prevention of Cognitive Decline in Older Persons.* New England Journal of Medicine. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2302368
