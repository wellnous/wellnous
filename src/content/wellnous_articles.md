# Adaptogens

#### Ashwagandha and Medicinal Mushrooms for Stress, Energy, and Resilience

In a world filled with constant stressors—whether from work, lifestyle, or environmental challenges—maintaining balance is more important than ever. This is where adaptogens come into play. These remarkable herbs and fungi help the body adapt to stress, regulate hormonal responses, and enhance overall resilience.

Among the most powerful adaptogens, ashwagandha and medicinal mushrooms stand out for their ability to support mental clarity, immune function, and energy levels.

Let's explore how adaptogens work, break down their physiological, physical, and mental benefits, and examine the scientific backing behind these ancient natural remedies.

#### What Are Adaptogens?

Adaptogens are natural substances that help the body adapt to physical, mental, or environmental stress. Their key functions include balancing cortisol levels to prevent excessive stress responses, supporting homeostasis by keeping physiological systems in equilibrium, and enhancing endurance to help the body recover from exertion and fatigue.

Unlike stimulants that provide short-term boosts which can lead to crashes, adaptogens work long term by nourishing the adrenal system and keeping energy levels steady and sustainable.

Common adaptogens include herbs such as ashwagandha, rhodiola, as well as functional fungi like reishi, lion’s mane, and cordyceps.

#### Ashwagandha:

**The Ancient Stress-Reliever and Nervous System Supporter**

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) is a powerful Ayurvedic herb known for its ability to reduce stress, enhance cognition, and support physical recovery. Used for thousands of years in Indian medicine, it is classified as a Rasayana, which means it rejuvenates and restores vitality.

#### Physiological Benefits

- Ashwagandha exerts profound effects on the endocrine and nervous systems, making it one of the best adaptogens for stress management.
- It regulates cortisol levels by lowering excess cortisol, which promotes calmness and resilience.
- It also helps balance thyroid function by supporting hormone production, aiding those with hypothyroidism or sluggish metabolism.
- In addition, ashwagandha acts as a potent antioxidant to reduce inflammation, support cell regeneration, and promote longevity.

#### Physical Benefits

- Athletes and physically active individuals often use ashwagandha for recovery and endurance.
- It can improve strength and muscle mass, support cardiovascular function through healthy blood pressure and circulation
- Boost libido and reproductive health, with studies showing improvements in fertility markers for both men and women.

#### Mental Benefits

- Ashwagandha also enhances cognitive function and emotional stability.
- It reduces anxiety and depression, acting as a natural alternative to pharmaceutical interventions while improving focus and memory.
- It supports neural regeneration and synaptic plasticity and promotes better sleep, assisting those who struggle with insomnia and restless sleep patterns.

#### Medicinal Mushrooms:

**Fungal Adaptogens for Immune Strength and Cognitive Clarity**

Medicinal mushrooms are functional fungi with adaptogenic properties that support stress resilience, immune health, and brain function. Different species target specific physiological functions. For example:

- Reishi: Supports stress reduction, enhances sleep, and boosts the immune system.
- Lion’s Mane: Improves cognitive function, supports nerve regeneration, and increases focus.
- Cordyceps: Enhances energy, stamina, and lung capacity.
- Chaga: Provides antioxidant benefits and cellular protection.
- Turkey Tail: Supports gut health with immune-boosting polysaccharides.

#### Reishi

Often called the "mushroom of immortality" because of its long history in traditional medicine as a stress reducer and overall wellness booster.

It contains compounds such as triterpenoids and polysaccharides that help modulate the immune system, reducing inflammation and promoting healthier sleep patterns. By influencing the body's stress response, reishi can help lower anxiety levels and foster a sense of calm, which contributes to better sleep quality and a more resilient immune system.

#### Lion’s Mane

Celebrated for its positive effects on brain health. This mushroom contains compounds like hericenones and erinacines, which encourage the synthesis of nerve growth factor (NGF). NGF is essential for the maintenance, growth, and repair of nerve cells, which in turn enhances focus, supports memory, and may even help slow down cognitive decline. Regular use of Lion’s Mane is associated with improved concentration and mental clarity, making it a popular choice among those looking to boost cognitive function naturally.

#### Cordyceps

Known for its ability to boost physical energy and endurance. It works by increasing the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the primary energy carrier in cells, and enhancing oxygen uptake during exercise. This dual-action helps improve stamina and performance, making cordyceps a valuable supplement for athletes or anyone looking to increase their energy levels and support better recovery after physical exertion.

#### Chaga

Rich in antioxidants, including melanin, superoxide dismutase, and various polyphenols, which help protect cells from oxidative stress and damage caused by free radicals. This antioxidant powerhouse plays a significant role in reducing inflammation throughout the body and supporting overall cellular health. By mitigating oxidative stress, Chaga can contribute to a slower aging process and support the body's natural detoxification efforts.

#### Turkey Tail

Known for its vibrant, colorful appearance, contains immune-boosting compounds such as polysaccharide-K (PSK) and polysaccharide-peptide (PSP). These compounds help modulate the immune response and support a healthy gut microbiome, which is crucial for immune function. Turkey Tail is also recognized for its potential to improve the balance of beneficial bacteria in the digestive system, leading to better overall gut health and a stronger immune response.

Each of these mushrooms offers a unique set of benefits, and incorporating them into your wellness routine—whether through supplements, powders, or teas—can help you address multiple aspects of health, from cognitive clarity and energy levels to immune balance and stress relief.

By integrating these adaptogens into your daily routine—whether through a morning mushroom-infused coffee or an evening ashwagandha tea—you create a foundation for long-term wellness, sustained energy, and mental clarity. I tend to take ashwaganda at night if I am stressed or need a good night sleep. I use mushroom powder in my daily morning protein coffee to set me up for a great day!

# Added Sugar

#### The Sweet Problem Hiding in Plain Sight

We tend to think of added sugar as a weight issue. A few extra calories. A little indulgence. Something to be balanced out with a walk or a trip to the gym.

But what if the real concern isn't the calories at all?

What if the bigger issue is what happens when one particular component of sugar, fructose, arrives in the liver?

Understanding this changes the conversation. It takes us beyond calories and into metabolism.

## Not All Sugar Is Metabolised Equally

Table sugar (sucrose) is roughly half glucose and half fructose.

Glucose is the body's preferred fuel. Every cell can use it. Your muscles use it during exercise, your brain relies on it daily, and excess amounts can be stored for later use. The metabolic workload is shared across the body.

Fructose follows a very different path.

While glucose can be used almost anywhere, fructose is handled predominantly by the liver. This means that every sweetened drink, dessert, syrup, confectionery product or sugar-laden snack delivers a fructose load that the liver must process.

And this is where things become interesting.

## The Liver: Your Metabolic Processing Plant

Think of the liver as your body's chemical processing factory.

Every day it sorts, packages, stores, converts and detoxifies thousands of compounds. It's remarkably resilient, but it isn't infinitely adaptable.

When small amounts of fructose arrive, this system works perfectly well.

The challenge arises when fructose arrives in large quantities, repeatedly, day after day.

Unlike glucose, fructose bypasses many of the body's normal regulatory controls. The liver rapidly processes it, and when energy stores are already sufficient, much of that fructose can be diverted into a process called **de novo lipogenesis**. Literally translated, this means "making new fat."

In other words, excess fructose can be converted directly into fat within the liver.

## When Sugar Becomes Liver Fat

This isn't a problem because fat is inherently bad.

The issue is location.

A certain amount of body fat is normal and healthy. Fat accumulating inside the liver is a different story entirely.

Researchers have shown that excess fructose intake can increase liver fat production, elevate triglycerides and contribute to insulin resistance. Over time, these changes may contribute to the development of fatty liver disease and wider metabolic dysfunction.

This is one reason rates of fatty liver disease have risen dramatically in populations where sugar-sweetened beverages and ultra-processed foods have become dietary staples.

## Why Scientists Compare Fructose to Alcohol

This is where the comparison that captures so many people's attention comes from.

Researchers such as Robert Lustig have highlighted that excessive fructose and excessive alcohol share several important metabolic pathways. Both are processed primarily by the liver. Both can stimulate fat production in the liver. Both can contribute to oxidative stress, inflammation and fatty liver disease when consumed in excess over time.

Of course, fructose is not alcohol.

It won't impair judgment, affect coordination or leave you with a hangover.

But from the liver's perspective, excessive fructose can create a surprisingly similar workload. This is why fructose has sometimes been described as "alcohol without the buzz."

## The Problem Isn't Just Sugar. It's Delivery.

One of the biggest misconceptions in nutrition is that nutrients exist in isolation.

They don't.

What matters is how they are delivered.

Our ancestors encountered fructose packaged inside whole fruits, alongside fibre, water and thousands of plant compounds. These naturally slowed consumption and absorption.

Modern food manufacturing has changed that equation.

Today's added sugars are often delivered in forms that are rapidly consumed and rapidly absorbed:

- Soft drinks
- Energy drinks
- Fruit juices
- Flavoured coffees
- Breakfast cereals
- Confectionery
- Sweetened yoghurts
- Snack bars
- Desserts
The result is a fructose load that can arrive in the liver far faster, and in far greater quantities, than would ever occur from eating whole fruit.

## This Is Not an Argument Against Fruit

At this point it's important to separate added sugar from naturally occurring sugar.

An apple is not a fizzy drink.

A handful of berries is not a chocolate bar.

Whole fruits contain fibre, polyphenols, water and structure. These elements slow digestion, influence how sugars are absorbed and provide benefits that extend far beyond their sugar content.

You would struggle to eat six oranges in a few minutes.

You could drink the sugar equivalent of those oranges in one glass of juice.

The fructose molecule may be the same, but the metabolic experience is not.

## Looking Beyond Calories

For decades, sugar was often discussed as a simple calorie issue.

Eat too much, expend too little, gain weight.

But emerging understanding of fructose metabolism suggests the story is more complex than that.

The liver doesn't respond to marketing claims. It doesn't know whether fructose arrived in a sports drink, a breakfast cereal, a smoothie or a fizzy drink. It simply processes what arrives.

And when that fructose load becomes excessive, one of its responses is to turn some of it into fat.

Perhaps that's why the most important question isn't whether added sugar is "good" or "bad".

It's whether we've normalised levels of sugar consumption that our metabolism was never designed to handle.

# Barefoot Walking

#### How Barefoot Walking Enhances Perception, Neuroplasticity, and Overall Wellbeing

Barefoot walking is more than just an exercise for the body—it’s a multi-dimensional practice that boosts sensory perception, drives neuroplasticity, improves foot function, gait, and posture, and offers powerful grounding benefits for both mental and physical wellbeing.

**Enhanced Sensory Perception and Neuroplasticity**

When you walk barefoot, the nerve receptors in your feet are exposed to a variety of textures, temperatures, and pressures. These receptors send a stream of sensory data to your brain, informing it about the details of your environment. Each step acts as a stimulus for neural pathways involved in balance, motor control, and spatial awareness. This consistent sensory input helps to strengthen existing neural connections and encourages the formation of new synapses—a process known as neuroplasticity. Over time, these improvements can lead to better motor coordination, sharper cognitive function, and enhanced overall mental clarity.

**Physical Benefits for the Foot, Gait, and Posture**

Walking without shoes allows the intricate muscles, ligaments, and tendons of your feet to work naturally, contributing to a host of benefits:

- **Stronger Feet:** Natural movement forces your foot muscles to engage fully. This increased activation can help improve the strength and stability of the arches, reducing the risk of issues like flat feet and plantar fasciitis.
- **Enhanced Gait:** With shoes removed, your body adjusts to a more natural stride. Improved proprioception—the sense of where your body is in space—helps refine your gait pattern, leading to smoother, more efficient movement.
- **Better Posture:** Improved foot mechanics translate upward. When your feet are strong and correctly aligned, the resulting posture adjustments relieve pressure on your knees, hips, and back, contributing to overall spinal alignment and balance.
- **Injury Prevention:** A more stable foot and enhanced balance reduce the likelihood of falls and repetitive stress injuries. By sensing the ground better, your body can react more quickly to uneven surfaces, minimizing the risk of sprains or strains.
**The Grounding Effect: Mental and Physical Benefits**

Grounding—also known as earthing—is the practice of direct physical contact with the Earth’s surface. Walking barefoot on natural terrain provides a unique connection that offers several benefits:

- **Physical Benefits of Grounding:** Research suggests that the electrons from the Earth’s surface may help reduce inflammation and oxidative stress. This process can promote better blood circulation and a reduction in muscle soreness, accelerating recovery after exercise.
- **Mental Benefits of Grounding:** The simple act of connecting with the natural ground has a calming effect on the nervous system. It can lower cortisol levels (the stress hormone) and foster a more relaxed state, deeply enhancing mindfulness and reducing anxiety. By engaging in grounding, you're likely to experience a heightened sense of presence and emotional balance.
- **Synergistic Effects:** When combined with the sensory benefits of barefoot walking, grounding creates a feedback loop that bolsters overall wellbeing. Not only do you improve your physical coordination and gait, but you also establish a deeper mind-body connection that facilitates recovery, reduces fatigue, and cultivates a more resilient mental state.

# Creatine

#### Enhancing Mental Clarity and Physical Performance Through ATP

Creatine has long been celebrated in the world of strength training and athletic performance, but its benefits extend far beyond muscle building. Recent research highlights creatine's profound influence on both mental and physical health. At the heart of these benefits is its critical role in the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the primary energy currency of our cells.

#### Understanding ATP and Creatine's Role

ATP is the molecule that powers nearly every cellular activity. Whether it's muscle contraction during intense exercise or supporting the firing of neurons in the brain, ATP provides the necessary energy to keep these systems operating effectively. When we engage in high-intensity activities—such as sprinting or heavy lifting—our muscles rapidly deplete their stored ATP. This is where creatine steps in.

Stored in the muscles as phosphocreatine, creatine donates a phosphate group to adenosine diphosphate (ADP), rapidly regenerating ATP. This process is essential not only for prolonged physical exertion but also for maintaining peak mental performance during tasks that demand high cognitive effort.

**Mental Benefits: Cognitive Function and Neuroprotection**

While creatine's role in physical performance is well-documented, its impact on brain health is equally compelling. Neurons in the brain also rely on ATP for optimal function. With a high-energy demand, especially during tasks that require memory, reasoning, and quick decision-making, the brain benefits from the additional ATP provided by creatine supplementation. Studies have shown that creatine can help improve short-term memory and cognitive processing, particularly in situations where the brain is operating under stressful conditions or facing fatigue.

Moreover, creatine’s role in supporting energy metabolism may offer neuroprotective benefits. By helping maintain ATP levels in brain cells, creatine can potentially mitigate the effects of neurodegenerative processes, making it a promising supplement for long-term brain health. This energy boost can result in sharper focus, enhanced mood, and improved cognitive resilience in both everyday activities and during periods of intense mental strain.

Creatine’s dual impact on physical strength and mental clarity underscores its value as a holistic supplement. By enhancing ATP regeneration, it not only powers your muscles during intense workouts but also supports the dynamic energy needs of your brain. This efficient energy metabolism makes creatine a key ally for both athletes and anyone seeking to enhance their mental performance and resilience.

# Cultured Foods

#### Probiotics

The beneficial bacteria that support our health are known as probiotics. These microscopic allies play a vital role in our well-being by:

- Producing essential vitamins and anti-inflammatory compounds
- Protecting our gut from harmful infections
- Supporting our mood and mental health
- Powering up our immune system—70% of which resides in the gut

#### Milk Kefir

**Kefir is arguably the most powerful probiotic food you can make at home, and one of the simplest.**

Milk kefir is a traditional fermented food made by adding kefir grains, a living community of beneficial bacteria and yeasts, to milk. Over 24 hours, these microorganisms consume much of the milk's lactose and transform it into a tangy, yoghurt-like drink packed with probiotics, organic acids, bioactive peptides and other health-promoting compounds.

#### Kombucha

Kombucha is a lightly sparkling fermented tea that has been enjoyed for centuries and is one of the easiest fermented foods to make at home.

Beyond its taste, kombucha offers a unique combination of probiotics, postbiotics and tea polyphenols. Fermentation not only introduces beneficial microbes but also creates a wide range of bioactive compounds that may help support gut health, microbial diversity and overall wellbeing. Kombucha is a simple and enjoyable way to incorporate more fermented foods into your diet, helping to reconnect your microbiome with the living foods that have become increasingly absent from modern lifestyles.

#### Water Kefir

Water kefir is a refreshing, naturally fermented drink that offers many of the same microbiome-supporting benefits as milk kefir, but without the dairy. In fact, it was my latest fermentation obsession. I love kombucha, but I wanted a caffeine-free alternative for the evenings. Made using translucent water kefir grains, a living culture of bacteria and yeasts, water kefir transforms simple sugar water into a lightly sparkling drink containing a diverse range of beneficial microorganisms. Depending on the grains and fermentation conditions, water kefir can contain dozens of probiotic strains, making it a fantastic option for anyone looking to support their gut health, particularly those following a vegan or dairy-free lifestyle.

#### Pro-biotic Yogurts

Traditional yogurt is one of the world's oldest fermented foods, transforming milk into a nutrient-rich food that's often easier to digest than milk itself. During fermentation, beneficial bacteria consume lactose, produce lactic acid and create compounds that contribute to yogurt's flavour, texture and health benefits. Alongside providing protein, calcium and other nutrients, yogurt can be a valuable source of live microbes that help support a healthy gut microbiome.

Inspired by Dr. William Davis's _Super Gut_ and the work of Cultured Food Life, these probiotic "yogurts" take the concept a step further. Rather than relying solely on traditional yogurt cultures, they use specific probiotic strains such as **L. reuteri**, **L. casei** and **L. gasseri**, fermented for up to 36 hours to achieve exceptionally high concentrations of beneficial bacteria.

#### Cultured Vegetables

Traditional fermentation have unique flavours, textured and nutritional profiles.

Classic Lacto-fermentation uses the lactic-acid bacteria naturally on the surface of the vegetables to convert the starches and sugars of the vegetables into lactic acid. This is a natural preservative that inhibits the growth of harmful bacteria.

This acid makes it too acidic for harmful bacteria to survive but allows the beneficial, probiotic lactic-acid loving bacteria to thrive.

This also starts to break down the foods, making the vegetables easier to digest. This also releases and converts compounds into a whole host of beneficial vitamins, enzymes and bioactive chemicals such as vitamin C. Far more than the original starting vegetable.

#### Kimchi

Kimchi is well known for it's probiotic content. As indicated in previous sections, these probiotics have a range of health benefits including helping your immune system, improving your mental health as well as helping your digestion and reducing inflammation. It's various methods and ingredients as well as traditional methods of fermentation results in a very unami, spicy, weirdly addictive flavour and crunchy texture. As Kimchi usually includes far more vegetables than other fermented products, it should contain a wide range of vitamins, such as A, B, C, and K, and minerals like calcium, iron, phosphorus, and selenium. There is also a high amount of fibre in Kimchi which will support your microbiome. More importantly, is delicious!

# Deliberate Cold Exposure

I love deliberate cold exposure, particularly through cold showers. I find it, offers a range of fascinating physiological benefits that extend beyond simply feeling refreshed. When you subject your body to cold water, a cascade of biological processes kicks in, priming your system for improved performance both mentally and physically.

Taking cold showers or immersing in cold water stimulates:

- **Dopamine & Norepinephrine Release** – Elevates mood, improves alertness, and builds stress tolerance.

- **Controlled Discomfort** **Training** – Strengthens my ability to remain calm under stress, making challenges in daily life feel more manageable.

- **Improved Circulation** – Boosts oxygen delivery to the brain, supporting cognitive function and focus.

Here is more detail:

#### Physiological Responses to Cold Exposure

When cold water hits your skin, it immediately activates the sympathetic nervous system. This response leads to the rapid release of hormones like adrenaline (epinephrine) and norepinephrine. These hormones make you feel more alert and focused, as they increase blood flow to critical organs and enhance overall metabolic activity. Unlike many stressors that trigger a rise in cortisol—the hormone often associated with chronic stress—cold exposure appears uniquely to stimulate adrenaline without a significant cortisol response. This distinct hormonal profile may help reduce the negative impacts of stress while simultaneously boosting mental clarity.

#### Impact on Brown Fat and Metabolic Function

Cold exposure also plays a key role in brown adipose tissue (BAT) activation. Brown fat is a special type of fat that burns calories to generate heat, unlike white fat, which stores energy. When exposed to cold, the body releases a protein called uncoupling protein-1 (UCP-1) in brown fat cells. This protein helps convert energy into heat, thereby elevating your metabolism. Over time, repeated cold exposure can even promote the "browning" of white fat, meaning parts of it become more metabolically active, supporting overall energy balance and potentially aiding in weight management.

#### Cellular Energy and Mitochondrial Adaptations

At the cellular level, cold exposure acts as a hormetic stressor—meaning a small, controlled amount of stress that encourages the body to adapt beneficially. The increased adrenaline promotes ATP regeneration, ensuring your cells have a ready supply of energy for repair and regeneration. This process not only facilitates rapid muscle contractions during physical performance but also supports the maintenance of brain cell function. Additionally, there is evidence that regular cold exposure can encourage mitochondrial biogenesis—the process of creating new mitochondria in cells—which further enhances energy production and overall cellular resilience.

#### Improving Circulation and Reducing Inflammation

The immediate effect of cold water on your blood vessels is vasoconstriction, where the small vessels near your skin narrow. This helps to conserve heat and redirect blood flow to vital organs. Once you warm up again, your body experiences reactive hyperemia—a boost in blood flow that can help clear out metabolic waste and improve nutrient delivery. This improved circulation may contribute to reduced inflammation at a systemic level. The controlled nature of cold exposure means it serves as a stressor that builds resilience without triggering the inflammatory, cortisol-driven responses typical of other stressors.

#### Recommended Exposure and Practical Application

Experts often recommend that the cumulative duration of cold exposure be around 13 minutes per week to experience these physiological benefits. Whether you choose to spread these minutes across daily quick cold showers or opt for one or two longer sessions, the goal is to maintain a consistent, gradual approach. Starting with shorter durations allows your body to adapt slowly, minimizing any initial shock and maximizing long-term adaptation.

By consistently exposing myself to cold, I train my nervous system to handle discomfort with greater ease. This daily practice fosters resilience, discipline, and a heightened mental edge.

# Feeding Your Body

## Everyday Nutrition

#### Whole Foods First

Before worrying about carbohydrates, fats or protein, it makes sense to start with the quality of the food itself.

Whole foods come packaged with fibre, vitamins, minerals, antioxidants and thousands of naturally occurring compounds that work together in ways we still don't fully understand. An apple is far more than the vitamin C it contains. Beans are far more than a source of carbohydrate. Food is often much more complex than the sum of its nutrients.

This is one reason concerns around ultra-processed foods have grown so rapidly.

A large umbrella review published in The BMJ linked higher intakes of ultra-processed foods with poorer health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and increased mortality risk.

That doesn't mean you need perfection. It simply means that if most of your diet is built around foods that still resemble their natural form, you're likely heading in the right direction.

#### Ultra-Processed Foods UPFs

Many UPFs are also built around commercial objectives rather than biological ones. Long shelf life, consistency, convenience and repeat purchases are all priorities for manufacturers.

The result is often products that are highly palatable, heavily marketed and remarkably easy to overconsume. Additives such as emulsifiers are also attracting growing scientific interest for their potential effects on the gut microbiome and intestinal barrier, although the full implications in humans are still being investigated.

Rather than obsessing over whether a food is technically a UPF, focus on what processing has changed. Foods that have lost their fibre, can be consumed rapidly, rely on health claims for credibility and contain long lists of unfamiliar ingredients deserve closer scrutiny.

After all, an apple doesn't need a marketing campaign to convince you it's food.

#### Feed Your Microbiome

For years, nutrition has focused almost exclusively on feeding the human body. We’ve counted calories, tracked protein, measured vitamins and debated fats and carbohydrates. But we're not the only organisms being fed when we eat. Trillions of bacteria, fungi and other microbes live within our digestive tract, forming a complex ecosystem known as the gut microbiome. Increasingly, scientists believe that many of the health benefits associated with fruits, vegetables, coffee, herbs, spices and other plant foods may come not only from the nutrients they provide us, but from the compounds they provide to the microbes living alongside us.

This is where polyphenols become so fascinating. Found naturally in foods such as berries, cocoa, olive oil, tea, coffee and colourful vegetables, these plant compounds appear to help shape the microbial environment within the gut. Rather than being valued solely for their antioxidant properties, polyphenols are now being studied for their ability to influence microbial diversity, support beneficial bacteria and generate compounds that may impact inflammation, metabolism and overall health. In many ways, feeding your microbiome may be just as important as feeding yourself. The good news is that the foods your microbes seem to love are the same real, minimally processed foods that have formed the foundation of healthy diets for generations.

#### Reduce Added Sugar

Added sugar has become so normalised that we barely notice it anymore. It turns up in soft drinks, cereals, sauces, protein bars, flavoured yoghurts and even foods marketed as healthy. Yet the real concern isn't simply that added sugar provides calories. It's that half of the sugar we consume is fructose, a molecule that is processed predominantly by the liver and follows a very different metabolic path from glucose.

When the liver is repeatedly flooded with fructose, particularly from sweetened drinks and ultra-processed foods, it can convert that surplus into fat through a process known as de novo lipogenesis. Over time, this may contribute to liver fat accumulation, insulin resistance and broader metabolic dysfunction. It's this unique relationship between fructose and the liver that has led researchers such as Robert Lustig to draw comparisons with alcohol metabolism and describe fructose as "alcohol without the buzz". While an apple is not a can of cola and sugar is not alcohol, understanding what happens to fructose once it reaches the liver sheds light on why added sugar deserves far more attention than its calorie count alone.

#### Fibre Does More Than Keep You Regular

Fibre has probably suffered from a marketing problem. It rarely receives the attention given to protein, yet it may be one of the most important components of a healthy diet.

Part of the confusion is that fibre isn't a single substance. Different types of fibre perform different jobs within the body.

Soluble fibres found in foods such as oats, beans and lentils help regulate blood sugar and cholesterol.

Insoluble fibres found in vegetables, nuts and whole grains support digestive function and bowel regularity.

Prebiotic fibres found in foods such as onions, garlic, leeks and asparagus act as fuel for beneficial gut bacteria.

#### Make Your Starches Work Harder

Resistant starch is considered a type of dietary fiber because it resists digestion in the small intestine and is fermented by gut bacteria in the colon.

Starchy foods often get caught up in nutritional debates, but the real question isn't whether you should eat starches. It's what type you're eating and what happens to them once they've been prepared.

Foods such as potatoes, rice, oats, beans and lentils can all play a role in a healthy diet. Even better, some can be transformed into a richer source of resistant starch simply by cooking and **cooling them before eating**.

Resistant starch escapes digestion in the small intestine and reaches the colon, where it becomes fuel for gut bacteria. This fermentation process helps produce beneficial compounds such as butyrate, which is associated with gut barrier integrity and metabolic health.

Something as simple as cooled potatoes in a salad or leftover rice used the following day can provide benefits beyond basic nutrition.

#### Fermented Foods Are Making a Comeback

Long before probiotics became a billion-pound industry, humans were consuming fermented foods as a normal part of life.

Foods such as kefir, live yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut and miso introduce beneficial compounds into the diet and may help support microbial diversity.

Modern research has renewed interest in these traditional foods, with studies suggesting that regular consumption can increase microbiome diversity while also influencing markers of inflammation.

The lesson here isn't that everyone needs another supplement. Sometimes the oldest approaches remain some of the most effective.

#### Prioritise Protein Quality

Protein remains important for maintaining muscle mass, supporting recovery and promoting satiety, particularly as we age.

However, quality matters just as much as quantity. Eggs, fish, seafood, meat, Greek yoghurt, tofu, tempeh, beans and lentils all provide valuable sources of protein alongside other nutrients that support health.

Rather than chasing ever-higher protein targets, a more balanced approach is to ensure each meal contains a meaningful source of protein while leaving plenty of room for vegetables, fibre-rich foods and plant diversity.

#### Intermittent Fasting

Another area that has attracted increasing attention is time-restricted eating.

Research suggests that leaving a longer gap between the evening meal and breakfast may support metabolic health, blood sugar regulation and circadian rhythm alignment.

Importantly, this isn't about starving yourself. A simple twelve-hour overnight fasting window is enough for many people to capture some of the potential benefits. Finishing dinner at 7pm and eating breakfast at 7am is hardly extreme, yet it gives the digestive system a valuable break from the constant cycle of eating that has become normal in modern life.

# Fibre

#### The Nutrient Everyone Talks About, But Few Truly Understand

Walk through any supermarket and you'll quickly find cereals, snack bars, yoghurts and even sweets proudly displaying "high fibre" claims. The implication is that fibre is something manufacturers add to foods to make them healthy.

The reality is almost the opposite.

Fibre isn't something that belongs in a factory. It's something that naturally exists within the structure of whole plant foods. Fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, seeds and whole grains evolved with fibre built into them. When food processing strips away that structure, much of the fibre disappears, and what remains is often a nutritionally inferior version of the original food.

In many ways, fibre is one of the clearest examples of why whole foods outperform heavily processed alternatives.

## What Actually Is Fibre?

Dietary fibre is the portion of plant foods that human digestive enzymes cannot break down. Unlike sugars and starches, fibre passes through the small intestine largely intact and reaches the colon, where it becomes food for trillions of microbes living in the gut.

For years, fibre was viewed simply as "roughage" that helped people stay regular.

Today we know it's far more than that.

Fibre influences cholesterol levels, blood sugar control, satiety, immune function, inflammation, gut barrier integrity and the composition of the microbiome itself. Many researchers now consider fibre one of the most important determinants of long-term gut health.

## Not All Fibre Is The Same

The word "fibre" is often used as if it's a single nutrient. In reality, fibre is a large family of compounds with very different functions.

### Soluble Fibre

Soluble fibre dissolves in water and forms a gel-like substance in the digestive tract.

Its benefits include:

- Slowing digestion
- Improving satiety
- Reducing blood sugar spikes
- Lowering LDL cholesterol
- Feeding beneficial gut bacteria
Common sources include oats, beans, lentils, apples, citrus fruits, avocados and carrots.

### Insoluble Fibre

Insoluble fibre does not dissolve in water.

Its main role is providing bulk and supporting healthy bowel movements. It acts like a structural scaffold that helps food move efficiently through the digestive tract.

Sources include:

- Vegetable skins
- Nuts and seeds
- Whole grains
- Green beans
- Cauliflower
- Potatoes

### Resistant Starch: The Forgotten Fibre

One of the most interesting forms of fibre isn't technically classified as fibre on many nutrition labels.

Resistant starch escapes digestion in the small intestine and travels to the colon where microbes ferment it.

Sources include:

- Cooked and cooled potatoes
- Cooked and cooled rice
- Green bananas
- Legumes
- Oats
Resistant starch is particularly effective at increasing production of beneficial short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate.

### Prebiotic Fibres

Some fibres act as highly selective fertiliser for beneficial bacteria.

Examples include:

- Inulin
- Fructooligosaccharides (FOS)
- Galactooligosaccharides (GOS)
These are found naturally in foods such as:

- Onions
- Garlic
- Leeks
- Asparagus
- Jerusalem artichokes
- Chicory root
Studies consistently show these fibres increase populations of beneficial microbes, including Bifidobacteria and Lactobacilli.

## The Problem With "Added Fibre"

Many processed foods now contain isolated fibres added after manufacturing.

Common examples include:

- Chicory root fibre
- Polydextrose
- Psyllium
- Inulin
- Resistant starch isolates
While some of these may have benefits, a fibre-fortified biscuit isn't equivalent to an apple.

Why? Because fibre never works alone in nature. A raspberry doesn't just provide fibre. It delivers:

- Polyphenols
- Micronutrients
- Water
- Natural sugars
- Thousands of bioactive plant compounds
- A physical structure that slows eating and digestion
When foods are processed, fibre is often stripped away alongside many of these components. Manufacturers may later add isolated fibre back in to improve the nutrition label, but that doesn't recreate the complexity of the original food.

In other words, a "high fibre" breakfast bar is often evidence that the food required fixing in the first place.

## The Hidden Cost Of Processing

Consider the difference between an orange and orange juice.

A whole orange requires chewing. Digestion occurs gradually. The fibre slows absorption and supports satiety.

Orange juice contains much of the sugar but very little of the original fibre structure. The result is faster absorption, reduced fullness and less material available for feeding beneficial gut bacteria.

The same pattern occurs across countless processed foods. The closer food remains to its natural state, the more of its original fibre architecture is preserved.

Nature packages nutrients together for a reason. Processing frequently separates them.

## What Happens When Fibre Reaches The Colon?

This is where things become truly fascinating.

Humans cannot digest fibre, but gut bacteria can.

When beneficial microbes ferment fibre, they produce compounds known as **short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)**, primarily:

- Butyrate
- Acetate
- Propionate
These compounds act as powerful signalling molecules throughout the body.

### Butyrate

Butyrate serves as the preferred fuel source for the cells lining the colon.

It helps:

- Maintain gut barrier integrity
- Regulate inflammation
- Support immune function
- Promote a healthy intestinal environment

### Propionate

Propionate appears to play important roles in:

- Appetite regulation
- Glucose control
- Metabolic health

### Acetate

Acetate is the most abundant SCFA and is utilised throughout the body as both an energy source and signalling molecule.

The remarkable thing is that these beneficial compounds are not found in food itself.

They are manufactured inside you by gut microbes when you feed them properly.

## Fibre Isn't The Whole Story

While fibre is arguably the most important nutrient for feeding the microbiome, it isn't acting alone.

Many plant foods also contain polyphenols, naturally occurring compounds that interact closely with gut microbes. Coffee is a fascinating example. Despite containing relatively little fibre compared with vegetables, legumes or berries, coffee appears to exert meaningful effects on the microbiome through its rich polyphenol content.

A study published in Nature Microbiology found one of the strongest diet-microbe associations reported to date, showing that regular coffee consumption was consistently linked with a greater abundance of the bacterium Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus across multiple independent populations. This finding highlights an important principle: a healthy microbiome thrives not only on fibre but on a diverse intake of naturally occurring plant compounds.

That doesn't mean coffee replaces vegetables or fibre-rich foods. Rather, it reinforces the idea that the healthiest diets provide a broad range of fibres, resistant starches and polyphenols that nourish different microbial species in different ways.

## Fibre And Akkermansia: A Key Gut Health Connection

One bacterial species generating enormous scientific interest is **Akkermansia muciniphila**.

Higher levels of Akkermansia have been associated with favourable metabolic health, improved gut barrier function and a healthier gut environment in numerous studies.

Akkermansia appears to flourish in individuals consuming diverse, plant-rich diets containing a variety of fibres and bioactive compounds.

While no single food magically increases Akkermansia, diets rich in:

- Vegetables
- Polyphenol-rich fruits
- Legumes
- Nuts and seeds
- Resistant starches
- Diverse fibre sources
All appear to create the conditions in which this beneficial bacterium can thrive. Think of fibre less as feeding you and more as feeding your microbial workforce. Healthy microbes help create healthy humans.

## Surprising Foods Rich In Fibre

When most people think fibre, they think bran cereals.

Some of the best sources may surprise you.

**High-Fibre Foods That Often Fly Under The Radar**

#### Avocado

- Approximately 10g of fibre per fruit

#### Raspberries

- One of the highest-fibre fruits available

#### Artichokes

- Among the most fibre-dense vegetables

#### Lentils

- Rich in both fibre and protein

#### Chia Seeds

- Packed with soluble fibre

#### Cocoa Powder

- Surprisingly fibre-rich and rich in polyphenols

#### Pistachios

- A useful source of both fibre and healthy fats

#### Cooked-and-Cooled Potatoes

- A meaningful source of resistant starch

#### Black Beans

- Excellent for satiety, glycaemic control and microbial diversity

## The Real Goal: Fibre Diversity

Perhaps the biggest mistake people make is focusing purely on grams.

Thirty grams of fibre from one source is not the same as thirty grams from twenty different plant foods.

Different species of bacteria specialise in digesting different fibres. The greater the diversity of fibres entering the gut, the greater the diversity of microbes that can flourish there.

This is why diets rich in varied fruits, vegetables, herbs, legumes, nuts and seeds consistently outperform fibre-fortified processed foods.

The question shouldn't simply be:

#### "How much fibre did I eat today?"

It should also be:

#### "How many different plants did I eat today?"

## Final Thoughts

Fibre isn't simply about preventing constipation. It is one of the primary ways in which the food you eat communicates with the microbes living inside you.

Many products marketed as "high fibre" contain isolated fibres added back into foods that processing stripped down in the first place. While these products may contribute some fibre, they rarely replicate the complexity of whole foods.

The goal shouldn't be to chase fibre-fortified cereals, snack bars or supplements.

Instead, aim to eat a diverse range of minimally processed plants. Fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, seeds, herbs, spices, resistant starches and even microbiome-friendly foods such as coffee all contribute to a richer ecosystem within the gut.

Because when you eat fibre, you're not just feeding yourself.

You're feeding the trillions of microbes that manufacture beneficial compounds, help maintain the gut barrier, influence immune function and may play a role in everything from metabolic health to healthy ageing.

And increasingly, the science suggests that those tiny organisms may have more influence over our health than we ever imagined.

# Hidden Toxins

## Creating a Healthier Environment for Wellbeing

Our surroundings directly impact our health, influencing everything from toxin exposure to air quality. By making intentional changes to my environment, I reduce harmful pollutants, eliminate disruptive chemicals, and create a space that supports my physical and mental well-being.

#### Removing Toxins: Eliminating Phthalates, BPA, and Microplastics

Toxins from plastics and industrial chemicals accumulate in the body, affecting hormonal balance, pregnancy outcomes, and long-term health. Here’s why I avoid them:

**Phthalates: Endocrine Disruptors That Interfere with Hormones**

Phthalates are commonly found in plastics, cosmetics, and household products. These chemicals mimic or block hormones, leading to imbalances in the endocrine system. Studies have linked phthalates to:

- **Reproductive Issues**– In both men and women, phthalates reduce fertility by disrupting testosterone and estrogen regulation.
- **Pregnancy & Foetal Development Risks**– Exposure during pregnancy can affect foetal development, increasing the risk of birth defects, preterm birth, and neurodevelopmental disorders in children.
- **Metabolic & Thyroid Disorders**– Phthalates interfere with thyroid hormones, contributing to metabolic dysfunction, weight gain, and insulin resistance.
**BPA (Bisphenol A): Toxic Effects on Brain and Hormone Function**

BPA, commonly found in food can linings, receipts, and plastics, has been shown to negatively impact:

- **Hormonal Health** – BPA acts as a xenoestrogen, meaning it mimics estrogen in the body, disrupting natural hormone cycles.
- **Neurological Development**– Prenatal exposure to BPA has been linked to behavioural changes and cognitive impairments in children.
- **Increased Risk of Cancer** – Some research suggests BPA exposure may elevate the risk of breast and prostate cancer due to its estrogen-mimicking properties.
**Microplastics: Hidden Pollutants That Accumulate in the Body**

Microplastics are tiny plastic fragments found in water sources, food, and even the air we breathe. Their impact on health includes:

- **Hormonal Disruption** – Microplastics often contain additives like phthalates and BPA, further contributing to endocrine disruption.
- **Inflammation & Cellular Stress** – Studies suggest microplastics may trigger chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, increasing the risk of diseases.
- **Gut Health & Microbiome Damage** – Consuming microplastics may alter gut microbiota, weakening digestive function and immune defence.

#### How I Reduce Exposure to These Toxins

- **Using Glass & Stainless Steel**– I’ve replaced plastic containers and bottles with glass and stainless steel to minimise exposure.
- **Being Mindful of Food Packaging**– I choose brands with BPA-free packaging or opt for fresh and frozen alternatives.
- **Filtering Water –** A high-quality water purifier removes heavy metals, chlorine, and microplastics, ensuring cleaner drinking water.

#### Food Additives: Avoiding Chemicals That Disrupt Gut Health

Many processed foods contain additives that negatively impact the gut microbiome, inflammation levels, and digestion. I avoid:

- **Emulsifiers**– Found in processed foods, emulsifiers disrupt gut bacteria, potentially contributing to inflammation and digestive discomfort.
- **Preservatives & Artificial Colours**– Certain preservatives (like sodium benzoate) and artificial dyes affect neurotransmitter function and gut health.
- **Chemical Flavour Enhancers**– MSG and other enhancers overstimulate neurotransmitters, affecting mood and cognition.
Instead, I prioritise whole, unprocessed foods with clean ingredients, supporting optimal digestion and microbiome health.

#### Air Quality: Reducing Pollution and Improving Indoor Air Stress Management

Indoor air pollution can be just as concerning as outdoor smog, with toxins from furniture, cleaning products, and environmental pollutants lingering in the air. To maintain a clean breathing space, I incorporate:

- **Air Purifiers** – A high-efficiency HEPA air filter reduces allergens, pollutants, and VOCs, keeping my home’s air fresh.
- **Non-toxic Cleaning Products** – Conventional cleaners release harmful chemicals, so I use natural alternatives to prevent exposure.
- **Ventilation & Plants** – Opening windows and adding indoor plants filters air naturally, improving oxygen levels and reducing airborne contaminants.

#### Skincare, Makeup & Sunscreen: Removing Toxic Ingredients from My Routine

Many personal care products contain endocrine disruptors, allergens, and harmful additives that absorb into the skin and bloodstream. Here’s what I avoid:

#### Harmful Chemicals in Skincare & Makeup

- **Parabens**– Found in moisturisers and cosmetics, parabens mimic estrogen, disrupting hormones and increasing breast cancer risk.
- **Sulfates** – Harsh surfactants like SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate) strip natural oils, leading to skin irritation and long-term sensitivity.
- **Synthetic Fragrances** – Many "fragrance" ingredients are phthalate-based, contributing to allergies, hormone disruption, and respiratory issues.
**Toxic Sun Lotion: The Hidden Dangers of Chemical Sunscreens**

Traditional sunscreens contain chemical UV filters that not only affect human health but also damage marine ecosystems. The worst offenders include:

- **Oxybenzone & Octinoxate**– These chemicals are absorbed into the bloodstream and disrupt hormone function, affecting thyroid balance and reproductive health.
- **Nanoparticles**– Some sunscreens use nano-sized titanium dioxide and zinc oxide, which may penetrate the skin barrier, leading to unknown systemic effects.
- **Coral Reef Damage** – Many chemical sunscreens contribute to marine pollution, harming coral reefs and ocean ecosystems.

#### Healthier Alternatives

- **Mineral Sunscreens**– I use non-nano zinc oxide sunscreens for effective UV protection without toxic additives.
- **Natural & Organic Skincare –** Choosing clean beauty brands helps me avoid parabens, synthetic fragrances, and endocrine disruptors.
- **Oil-Based Sun Protection**– Some natural oils, like red raspberry seed oil, offer mild UV protection while nourishing the skin.

# Infra Red Exposure

## Infrared Light, Mitochondria and Metabolic Health

I've become increasingly interested in something that is all around us but that we rarely think about — light.

Light isn't simply something that allows us to see. Different wavelengths interact with our biology in very different ways, and one of the most interesting is red and near-infrared light, particularly its relationship with the mitochondria.

Infrared has always been part of our natural environment. It is abundant in sunlight and is also produced by fire and traditional incandescent bulbs. Our ancestors would have been exposed to it naturally throughout the day, particularly during the lower sun of morning and evening.

## Why the mitochondria matter

Almost everything our bodies do ultimately depends on energy. Movement, brain function, hormone production, immune function, tissue repair, maintaining body temperature and even the basic processes that keep a cell alive all require energy. The mitochondria sit at the centre of this process. They convert the food we eat and oxygen we breathe into ATP, the energy currency used by our cells.

They are also involved in controlling oxidative stress, cellular signalling, inflammation, calcium regulation and whether damaged cells survive or are removed. This means that healthy mitochondria are fundamental to metabolic health and to the normal function of almost every tissue in the body.

When mitochondrial function is impaired, the consequences can extend far beyond energy levels. Mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with ageing and a wide range of metabolic and chronic diseases, ranging from diabetes to most cancers, which is why maintaining mitochondrial health has become such an important area of research.

## Where infrared comes in

This is where infrared becomes particularly interesting.

Research into photobiomodulation suggests that red and near-infrared wavelengths can interact with mitochondrial chromophores such as cytochrome c oxidase, an important component of the electron transport chain.

This interaction may influence electron transport, mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production, while also affecting nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species. These changes can act as signals that influence cellular repair, inflammation and antioxidant defence.

In other words, light can interact with the machinery responsible for producing cellular energy.

If the mitochondria are central to our metabolic health, and particular wavelengths of light can influence mitochondrial function, it makes sense that regular exposure to these wavelengths could have effects that extend well beyond the skin.

This is one of the reasons I find infrared so fascinating.

## Infrared as part of our natural environment

There is also something particularly interesting about _when_ we are exposed to infrared naturally.

The spectrum of sunlight changes throughout the day. When the sun is low in the morning and evening, the relative proportion of longer wavelengths, including infrared, is greater.

There is evidence that early exposure to near-infrared may also have a protective or photopreconditioningeffect, helping cells cope with the oxidative stress associated with subsequent UV exposure. Experimental research has shown that near-infrared exposure can induce cellular defences against later UV stress.

It raises an interesting possibility: perhaps the natural sequence of light throughout the day is not accidental. Morning light provides a broad spectrum containing infrared before the stronger midday UV exposure, while evening light once again shifts towards the longer wavelengths.

Our biology evolved within this environment.

## A very different modern light environment

Today, our relationship with light is very different.

We spend much of our lives indoors, surrounded by LEDs, screens and artificial lighting. We can wake up under artificial light, spend the entire day under it and then sit in front of a bright screen until the moment we go to bed.

Modern artificial lighting can create a very different spectral environment from natural daylight, fire and incandescent light. Traditional incandescent bulbs, for example, produce a broad, warm spectrum with substantial infrared because the filament becomes extremely hot. Fire is another natural source of infrared.

It is interesting that both of these familiar light sources are rich in the longer wavelengths that are largely absent from many modern artificial environments.

## Why this could matter for metabolic health

If mitochondria are fundamental to how our cells produce energy and regulate themselves, then anything capable of positively influencing mitochondrial function becomes interesting from a metabolic perspective.

Rather than thinking about infrared as simply something that makes us feel warm, we can think of red and near-infrared light as a biological signal that may influence one of the most fundamental systems in the body — the mitochondria.

And because mitochondria are present in almost every cell and are involved in energy production, metabolism, signalling and repair, the implications of supporting mitochondrial function could potentially extend across many aspects of health.

Perhaps this is why our natural environment has always contained so much infrared.

We didn't evolve under LED lighting. We evolved under the sun, around fires and in darkness at night. Light is part of our environment, and our cells have evolved to respond to it.

Infrared light can also pass through clothing., so one of the easiest ways to get more infrared exposure, is to go for a morning (or evening) walk. This also aligns with the many other benefits from this practice.

# Intermittent Fasting

## Giving Your Body Time To Catch Up

Most conversations around nutrition focus on what we eat. Protein, fibre, vitamins, calories and supplements dominate health headlines and social media feeds.

What receives far less attention is how often we eat.

For many people, the day begins with breakfast shortly after waking and ends with a snack in front of the television. In between are coffees, lunches, treats, drinks and the occasional handful of something grabbed from the cupboard. It's not unusual for food to be entering the body across a 14 or 15-hour window.

The body is remarkably good at processing food, but it was never designed to be in a constant fed state.

Intermittent fasting, particularly time-restricted eating, is built around a simple idea: spend less time eating and more time allowing the body to focus on everything else.

## The Modern Problem: We're Always Eating

Digestion is an active process.

Every meal triggers a cascade of hormonal and metabolic activity. Blood sugar rises, insulin is released, nutrients are absorbed and energy is either used immediately or stored for later.

There's nothing wrong with this. It's normal physiology.

The issue is that many of us move from one meal to the next with very little downtime. The body rarely gets an extended opportunity to shift from processing incoming energy to maintaining, repairing and recycling existing tissues.

Intermittent fasting creates that opportunity.

Rather than continuously handling a fresh influx of nutrients, the body spends longer periods relying on stored energy and carrying out some of its housekeeping functions.

## What Happens During A Fast?

A common misconception is that fasting means the body "shuts down".

In reality, the opposite happens.

As liver glycogen stores begin to fall, the body gradually becomes less reliant on glucose and starts increasing its use of stored fat for energy. This process generates molecules known as **ketones**.

Ketones are often discussed in the context of ketogenic diets, but they are also a natural consequence of fasting.

What's particularly interesting is that ketones aren't simply an alternative fuel source. They also act as signalling molecules that may influence inflammation, cellular resilience and brain function. Researchers continue to investigate their potential role in metabolic and neurological health.

## Blood Sugar Benefits

Another area attracting considerable attention is blood sugar regulation.

Every time we eat, blood glucose rises and insulin is released to help move that glucose into cells.

By reducing the amount of time spent eating, many people spend less time experiencing repeated blood sugar elevations throughout the day.

Research suggests time-restricted eating may improve insulin sensitivity and various markers of metabolic health, particularly in people who are overweight or metabolically unhealthy.

Importantly, that doesn't mean people should fear glucose or carbohydrates. Rather, it suggests that periods without food may be a normal and beneficial part of metabolic health.

## The Brain Appears To Benefit Too

Many people who practice intermittent fasting report improvements in concentration, mental clarity and energy levels.

While subjective experiences should always be interpreted cautiously, there are plausible biological explanations for these observations.

As ketone production rises, the brain gains access to an additional energy source. Fasting may also influence processes involved in neuronal maintenance and resilience. Researchers are increasingly interested in the relationship between fasting, brain health and healthy ageing, although many areas remain under investigation.

## Your Mitochondria Need Downtime Too

Mitochondria are often described as the powerhouses of the cell, but they're better thought of as tiny energy-processing factories.

Like any factory, they produce waste alongside useful output.

Periods without food appear to trigger cellular maintenance pathways that help remove damaged cellular components and recycle older material. This process, often referred to as autophagy, is one of the main reasons fasting has attracted interest from longevity researchers.

It's important not to overhype this. We are still learning exactly how these mechanisms translate into long-term human health.

However, the concept itself is compelling.

Sometimes doing less can give the body an opportunity to repair more.

## Don't Underestimate The Sleep Connection

One of the simplest benefits of intermittent fasting may have nothing to do with fasting itself.

It's stopping eating late.

Many people finish their final meal or snack shortly before bed, leaving the digestive system working while the body is attempting to enter its overnight repair phase.

Creating a larger gap between the last meal and sleep often leaves people feeling lighter, less bloated and more rested the following morning. Emerging research into chrononutrition also suggests that aligning eating patterns more closely with our circadian rhythms may provide metabolic advantages.

## The ZOE Big IF Study

Perhaps the most encouraging evidence comes from the real world.

In the Big IF Study, conducted by ZOE, more than 140,000 people signed up to test a 10-hour eating window. Participants reported improvements in energy, mood, hunger and body weight, and many chose to continue the approach after the study ended.

The significance wasn't that fasting produced miraculous results.

It was that a relatively simple change appeared sustainable for many people.

## The Takeaway

Intermittent fasting shouldn't be viewed as a detox, because your liver, kidneys, lungs and digestive system are already doing that job remarkably well.

But it may provide something many modern lifestyles lack: **time**.

Time for blood sugar to settle.

Time for insulin levels to fall.

Time for fat metabolism to increase.

Time for ketones to appear.

Time for cellular maintenance processes to occur.

And perhaps just as importantly, time where the body isn't constantly busy dealing with the next meal.

In a world where food is available 24 hours a day, one of the simplest health interventions might not be what we eat.

It might be giving our bodies a little longer between meals.

# Mitochondrial Health

#### Why cellular energy matters more than we think – and what we can do to protect it

Mitochondria are often described as the powerhouses of our cells. But their role goes far beyond simply producing energy. They influence inflammation, cellular repair, metabolism, ageing and even whether a cell survives or dies.

I am passionate about mitochondrial health because I believe it is one of the most important, yet often overlooked, foundations of long-term health.

Every cell in the body contains hundreds, sometimes thousands, of mitochondria. These tiny structures convert energy from the food we eat into ATP – the energy currency that powers almost every cellular function.

When mitochondria work efficiently, our cells have the energy they need to function, repair and adapt. When their performance deteriorates, energy production can decline, oxidative stress and inflammation can increase, and cellular repair becomes less efficient.

Increasing evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction plays an important role in many chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders and cancer.

The encouraging news is that mitochondria are not static. They constantly adapt, repair, divide and renew themselves. This means our everyday lifestyle choices can influence how well they function throughout life.

Let's explore why this matters, what threatens mitochondrial health and what we can do to support it.

---

## Part 1 – The Big Picture
### Why should we care about our mitochondria?

Think of mitochondria as the energy generators inside our cells.

Our heart needs enormous amounts of energy to keep beating. Our brain requires a constant supply to support thinking, memory and communication. Our muscles need energy to move, and virtually every tissue relies on ATP to maintain and repair itself.

But mitochondria do much more than generate energy.

They also help to:

- Regulate inflammation and cellular signalling.
- Maintain calcium balance within cells.
- Support antioxidant defences.
- Control programmed cell death, helping remove damaged or abnormal cells.
- Support cellular repair, adaptation and renewal.

In other words, healthy mitochondria are essential not only for energy, but also for maintaining healthy tissues and supporting the body's ability to respond to stress.

When mitochondrial function becomes compromised, the effects can extend far beyond feeling tired. It may influence metabolic health, inflammation, ageing and our vulnerability to chronic disease.

This is why I believe mitochondrial health deserves far more attention.

## Part 2 – What Is Going Wrong?
### The modern mitochondrial health crisis

Our mitochondria evolved in an environment very different from the one we live in today.

Modern lifestyles can place them under continuous pressure. Excess calorie intake, poor metabolic health, environmental pollutants, chronic psychological stress, insufficient sleep and certain medications can all contribute to mitochondrial stress.

Over time, multiple small stressors can accumulate, reducing mitochondrial efficiency and resilience.

#### The main threats to mitochondrial health

**1. Poor metabolic health**

Excess calorie intake, frequent consumption of highly processed foods and impaired metabolic health can place additional demands on the body's energy systems.

**2. Environmental pollution**

Air pollution and particulate matter may increase mitochondrial oxidative stress and inflammation.

**3. Heavy metals and chemicals**

Exposure to heavy metals such as mercury, lead and cadmium can interfere with mitochondrial enzymes and energy metabolism. Certain environmental chemicals may also disrupt mitochondrial signalling.

**4. Chronic psychological stress**

Persistent stress and elevated cortisol can impair mitochondrial function and increase oxidative damage.

**5. Insufficient sleep**

Poor sleep reduces the body's opportunity to repair and renew mitochondria and replenish antioxidant defences.

**6. Alcohol and certain medications**

Some medications, alcohol and environmental chemicals can increase mitochondrial stress and reduce ATP production.

**7. Endocrine-disrupting chemicals**

Chemicals found in plastics and industrial compounds may negatively influence mitochondrial signalling and metabolism.

**8. Electromagnetic fields and radiofrequency radiation**

There is a growing body of research exploring whether electromagnetic fields and radiofrequency radiation from mobile phones, WiFi networks, power lines and cellular infrastructure may influence mitochondrial function.

These are emerging areas of research, and some proposed mechanisms have yet to be confirmed in human studies.

The important point is that these stressors do not necessarily act in isolation. Individually, their effects may be modest, but together they may create a cumulative burden that gradually reduces mitochondrial efficiency.

### Why this matters for chronic disease

Mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly being investigated in relation to a wide range of chronic health conditions.

As mitochondrial performance declines, ATP production may fall, oxidative stress can increase, inflammation can become more difficult to regulate and cellular repair processes may become less efficient.

This creates an environment in which healthy tissues may become progressively more vulnerable to damage.

Cancer is one particularly interesting area of mitochondrial research, which we will explore in more detail in Part 4.

## Part 4 – The Science Deep Dive
### For those who want to understand what is happening inside the cell

So far, we've looked at why mitochondrial health matters and what we can do to support it.

But what is actually happening inside these tiny cellular structures?

Let's take a closer look.

#### How mitochondria produce energy

Inside mitochondria is a sophisticated biological energy system called the electron transport chain.

It extracts energy from food molecules and uses this energy to pump protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane.

This creates an electrical and chemical gradient – rather like charging a biological battery.

The stored energy is then used by an enzyme called ATP synthase.

ATP synthase works much like a tiny molecular turbine. As protons flow back across the membrane, it uses that energy to produce ATP.

This is how mitochondria generate the energy that powers almost every function in our bodies.

When this system works efficiently, it produces remarkable amounts of ATP with relatively little waste.

However, when the electron transport chain becomes impaired, energy production can become less efficient and the generation of reactive oxygen species can increase.

#### Oxidative stress: when energy production becomes a problem

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are naturally produced during mitochondrial energy metabolism.

At normal levels, they are not simply harmful waste products. They also play important roles in cellular signalling and adaptation.

The problem arises when ROS production becomes excessive relative to the body's antioxidant defences.

Under normal circumstances, antioxidant systems neutralise excess reactive oxygen species.

However, chronic metabolic and environmental stress can overwhelm these defences.

This can create a damaging cycle:

- Mitochondrial stress increases ROS production.
- Excess ROS damages mitochondrial components.
- Damaged mitochondria produce energy less efficiently.
- Further ROS production and inflammation may follow.
- The cycle can progressively compromise cellular function.

Over time, this may reduce ATP production and the cell's ability to repair and maintain itself.

This is one of the reasons mitochondrial health is so important to the wider picture of ageing and chronic disease.

#### Two different mitochondrial priorities

An interesting aspect of mitochondrial biology is that mitochondria can support different metabolic priorities depending on what a cell needs.

Two broad functional states can be described:

**Energetic mitochondria**

These prioritise ATP production and are particularly important in tissues with high energy demands, including:

- The heart.
- The brain.
- Skeletal muscles.
- The retina.

**Biosynthetic mitochondria**

These prioritise the production of cellular building blocks, including amino acids, nucleotides and lipids.

These compounds are essential for cell growth, proliferation and tissue repair.

They generate less ATP relative to their biosynthetic activity but play an important role in supporting cellular growth and renewal.

In healthy tissues, cells can move between these metabolic priorities according to their needs.

When mitochondrial health deteriorates, however, this balance can become disrupted, contributing to wider metabolic dysfunction.

#### Cancer and mitochondrial dysfunction: the Warburg effect

One of the most fascinating areas of mitochondrial research is its relationship with cancer.

In 1924, biochemist Otto Warburg observed that many cancer cells rely heavily on glycolysis for energy production, even when oxygen is available.

This phenomenon is now known as the **Warburg Effect**.

At first glance, this seems rather inefficient.

Glycolysis produces only around 2 ATP molecules per glucose molecule, whereas complete mitochondrial oxidation can produce more than 30.

So why would cancer cells favour a seemingly less efficient energy pathway?

One explanation is that cancer cells have altered metabolic requirements.

Changes in mitochondrial function can impair normal oxidative metabolism, encouraging greater reliance on glycolysis.

However, glycolysis also provides something else that rapidly dividing cancer cells need: the molecular building blocks required to manufacture new cells.

These include components used to make DNA, proteins and cell membranes.

In this way, the metabolic shift may support rapid growth and division, even if ATP production per molecule of glucose is lower.

**Mitochondria and programmed cell death**

Mitochondria also play a critical role in apoptosis – the programmed cell death process that removes damaged or abnormal cells.

Healthy mitochondria help regulate the signals that determine whether a cell should be repaired or eliminated.

When mitochondrial signalling becomes impaired, abnormal cells may be better able to evade these quality-control mechanisms and survive longer than they should.

This has important implications for cancer biology.

It does not mean mitochondrial dysfunction is the sole cause of cancer. Cancer is a complex disease involving genetic mutations, immune dysfunction, environmental exposures, inflammation and altered cellular metabolism.

However, mitochondrial dysfunction appears to be an important contributor that can create conditions favourable to cancer development, progression and resistance to treatment.

Viewed through this lens, cancer can be understood partly as a disease involving disrupted cellular energy and signalling.

Genetic mutations undoubtedly matter, but the metabolic environment in which those mutations occur may also be important.

Mitochondrial dysfunction may therefore help explain why chronic inflammation, obesity, insulin resistance, environmental toxins and ageing are all associated with increased cancer risk, despite arising from very different origins.

I find this an especially exciting area of research, and I look forward to seeing how our understanding develops through further human studies.

#### Mitophagy: the importance of removing damaged mitochondria

Mitochondria are constantly changing.

They divide, fuse, adapt to the energy demands of the cell and undergo renewal.

But what happens when a mitochondrion becomes damaged?

This is where mitophagy comes in.

Mitophagy is the selective removal of damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria through the cell's recycling systems.

Rather than allowing inefficient mitochondria to accumulate, the cell identifies and removes them so their components can be recycled.

This is an essential part of maintaining a healthy mitochondrial network.

Exercise, periods of fasting and other metabolic signals can influence the pathways involved in mitochondrial quality control.

Sleep also plays an important role in the wider processes of cellular repair and renewal.

**Where does urolithin A fit in?**

Urolithin A is a compound produced when certain gut bacteria metabolise plant compounds called ellagitannins, found in foods such as pomegranates and walnuts.

It has attracted scientific interest because of its potential to activate pathways involved in mitophagy and mitochondrial function.

By potentially supporting the removal of damaged mitochondria and encouraging mitochondrial renewal, urolithin A may offer another avenue for supporting cellular health and healthy ageing.

The broader principle is important: mitochondrial health isn't simply about producing more energy. It is also about maintaining a healthy population of functioning mitochondria.

## The Bigger Picture
### Protecting our cellular energy for long-term health

Mitochondria sit at the centre of cellular energy production, repair, adaptation and survival.

Modern life exposes them to a wide range of pressures: excess calories, sedentary behaviour, pollution, chronic stress, sleep disruption, environmental toxins and potentially other emerging stressors.

Individually, these challenges may seem relatively small. Together, they can create a cumulative burden that gradually compromises cellular energy production and resilience.

As mitochondrial function declines, ATP production may fall, oxidative stress can rise, inflammation may increase and the body's ability to maintain healthy tissues can become compromised.

Over time, this dysfunction may contribute to many of the chronic diseases associated with ageing.

But there is reason to be optimistic.

Mitochondria are dynamic. They constantly adapt, repair, divide and renew themselves.

Through better nutrition, regular exercise, restorative sleep, stress management, sensible toxin reduction, natural light exposure and support for mitochondrial quality-control processes, we may be able to maintain healthier and more efficient mitochondrial networks throughout life.

Rather than searching for a single cure or miracle supplement, I believe the most powerful approach is to reduce the total burden placed on our mitochondria while supporting the body's natural capacity for repair and renewal.

**Ultimately, protecting our mitochondria means protecting the energy systems that allow our cells – and therefore our bodies – to function, recover and thrive.**

# Nac

N-acetylcysteine, better known as NAC, is a form of the amino acid cysteine. It has been used in medicine for decades as a treatment for paracetamol overdose and as a mucolytic to help clear thick respiratory secretions. Increasingly, however, NAC is being recognised for its wider role in antioxidant defence, respiratory health and cellular resilience.

### Supporting the body’s master antioxidant

One of NAC’s most important roles is supplying cysteine for the production of glutathione, one of the body’s principal intracellular antioxidants. Glutathione helps neutralise reactive oxygen species, protect cellular structures and maintain the delicate balance between oxidation and reduction within the body.

NAC also possesses a sulphur-containing thiol group, written chemically as –SH. This allows it to participate directly in redox reactions and helps explain both its antioxidant and mucus-thinning properties. Rather than acting as a simple standalone antioxidant, NAC supports the body’s own antioxidant network by restoring the building blocks needed to make glutathione.

### Why NAC can help with thick mucus

Respiratory mucus contains large proteins called mucins, which are linked together by disulphide bonds. These molecular bridges contribute to the thickness and elasticity of mucus.

The free thiol group in NAC can disrupt or rearrange these disulphide bonds. This makes mucus less thick and sticky, allowing it to be cleared more easily through coughing and the normal movement of tiny hair-like structures called cilia. This may be particularly useful when a cold or respiratory infection produces a congested, productive cough.

It is important to clarify that disulphide bonds are not themselves antioxidants. NAC’s antioxidant action comes from its redox-active thiol group and its ability to replenish glutathione, while its interaction with disulphide bonds explains its mucolytic action.

### A different approach during cold and flu season

NAC is not a cure for colds or influenza, and the evidence for routinely using it to prevent uncomplicated seasonal infections remains limited. Its potential value lies in supporting the environment in which recovery takes place.

Unlike medicines that simply lower temperature, reduce pain or constrict nasal blood vessels, NAC works primarily by supporting antioxidant capacity and helping the respiratory system clear secretions. It may therefore be particularly relevant when thick mucus, chest congestion or a productive cough are prominent symptoms.

This does not make NAC a direct replacement for paracetamol or a decongestant. These medicines perform different jobs. NAC offers a more supportive role by working with mucus clearance rather than simply masking discomfort.

### Is NAC safe?

Oral NAC generally has a favourable safety profile. Reviews of respiratory studies have found NAC to be well tolerated across a range of doses, although taking more does not necessarily produce a greater benefit.

Anyone with asthma, a history of stomach ulcers or a bleeding disorder should seek individual advice before taking NAC. It may also interact with certain medicines, including nitrate treatments used for angina. Advice from a pharmacist or clinician is sensible during pregnancy or breastfeeding, before giving NAC to a child, or when taking regular medication.

### Conclusion

NAC is more than a conventional antioxidant supplement. By supplying cysteine for glutathione production and using its thiol group to interact with disulphide bonds in mucus, it supports two important processes at once: cellular antioxidant defence and respiratory clearance.

The strongest evidence supports its established mucolytic use and its role in specific respiratory conditions. Its benefits for general health is best regarded as a potentially useful support rather than a cure-all. Used appropriately, NAC offers a practical example of supporting the body’s own protective systems instead of merely silencing symptoms.

# Omega 3

#### Essential Fatty Acids for Brain and Emotional Health

Omega-3 fatty acids have long been recognised for their profound impact on human health, influencing everything from cardiovascular function to brain performance.

Unlike many other nutrients, omega-3s play both a structural and functional role, making them critical for physiological resilience, cognitive health, and emotional balance.

Research has even explored their effectiveness compared to pharmaceutical antidepressants like Prozac (fluoxetine) in treating depression—suggesting that omega-3 supplementation might provide a natural alternative or complement to traditional mental health treatments.

**Health Benefits – Supporting Long-Term Well-Being**

Omega-3s, primarily EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid), are essential for maintaining core body functions. The body cannot synthesise them adequately, meaning they must come from dietary sources such as fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), flaxseeds, chia seeds, and algae-based supplements.

Key Health Benefits:

- **Heart Health**: Omega-3s lower triglycerides, reduce blood pressure, and improve vascular function.
- **Anti-Inflammatory Effects**: They help regulate inflammation, which plays a role in autoimmune diseases, arthritis, and chronic pain conditions.
- **Joint and Muscle Recovery:** Athletes and those with inflammatory disorders report less stiffness and soreness when supplementing omega-3s.
- **Immune Support:** Omega-3s strengthen immune function and help regulate the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways.
**Physiological Benefits – Omega-3’s Role in Cellular and Neural Health**

Every cell in the body relies on omega-3s for optimal function, particularly those within the nervous system. DHA, in particular, is highly concentrated in the brain and retina, influencing cellular signalling, nerve conduction, and synaptic plasticity.

#### How Omega-3s Work at the Physiological Level:

- **Cell Membrane Integrity**: Omega-3s integrate into cell membranes, improving fluidity and function, ensuring efficient communication between cells.
- **Neuroprotection & Synaptic Growth:** DHA enhances neuronal structure, facilitating memory formation and cognitive processing.
- **Regulating Neurotransmitters**: Omega-3s influence neurotransmitter function—particularly dopamine and serotonin, both essential for mood regulation.
- **Reducing Neuroinflammation:** Chronic inflammation is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, and omega-3s help lower markers of brain inflammation linked to conditions such as Alzheimer’s.
In essence, omega-3s help the brain stay young, sharp, and adaptable—a crucial factor in preventing cognitive decline and maintaining mental acuity throughout life.

**Mental Health Benefits – Omega-3 as a Natural Antidepressant**

#### Omega-3 vs. Prozac: A Fascinating Comparison

In a groundbreaking study published in the Archives of General Psychiatry, researchers compared omega-3 fatty acid supplementation to Prozac (fluoxetine) in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD).

The findings were striking:

- Patients receiving high-dose omega-3 (EPA) showed significant reductions in depression symptoms, comparable to those taking Prozac.
- When omega-3 and Prozac were combined, the therapeutic effects were even greater than Prozac alone.
- Unlike Prozac, omega-3 supplementation had minimal side effects, making it a promising adjunct treatment.

#### Mental Health Mechanisms:

- Serotonin Regulation: Omega-3s enhance serotonin receptor function, improving mood stability.
- Lowering Inflammation: Depression has been linked to systemic inflammation, and omega-3s work as natural anti-inflammatory agents.
- Reducing Anxiety Symptoms: EPA supplementation decreases stress hormone levels, promoting emotional balance.
While more research is needed, these findings suggest that omega-3 supplementation could serve as a natural, complementary treatment for mood disorders, offering relief without the side effects of pharmaceutical interventions.

### Best Sources
- Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines)
- Chia seeds and flaxseeds (ALA, which can convert to EPA/DHA in small amounts)
- Algae-based supplements (ideal for plant-based diets)
- A good quality supplement
Always consult a healthcare professional before making significant dietary changes, especially when using high-dose omega-3s for mood-related benefits.

# Polyphenols

#### The Colourful Side of Nutrition

Spend enough time reading about nutrition and you start to notice a pattern. The conversation almost always revolves around a handful of familiar topics. Protein gets discussed endlessly. Carbohydrates are either praised or demonised depending on the latest trend. Fibre is finally receiving some of the recognition it deserves. Even vitamins and minerals, despite being discovered over a century ago, still dominate health headlines and supplement advertisements.

Yet there's a fascinating omission in all of this.

Many of the foods most consistently associated with long-term health benefits contain compounds that rarely appear on a nutrition label, aren't tracked in diet apps and are seldom mentioned in mainstream dietary advice. Coffee, berries, cocoa, olive oil, herbs, spices and colourful vegetables all seem to offer benefits that can't be fully explained by their vitamin content, calorie count or macronutrient profile alone.

Scientists have spent the last few decades trying to understand why.

Increasingly, the answer appears to involve a vast family of plant compounds known as polyphenols.

## So What Exactly Are Polyphenols?

If you've never heard of polyphenols, you're not alone.

They're not regarded as essential nutrients in the same way as vitamin C or magnesium, which means most people can go through life without ever encountering the term. Yet we're exposed to them every day. Every cup of coffee, handful of berries, splash of olive oil or sprinkle of herbs brings hundreds of different plant compounds into the body.

That should make us pause for a moment.

Because if foods with vastly different nutrient profiles are producing broadly similar health outcomes, perhaps we're looking beyond traditional nutrition labels for answers.

In many ways, polyphenols remind us that food is far more complicated, and far more interesting, than the numbers printed on the back of a packet.

## Polyphenols Are Not Just Antioxidants

If you've come across polyphenols before, chances are they were described as antioxidants.

That's technically correct.

It's also where the story usually stops.

For years, nutrition messaging told us that oxidative stress damages cells, antioxidants reduce oxidative stress and therefore consuming more antioxidants must be a good thing. It was a simple explanation, easy to understand and even easier to market.

The supplement industry embraced the idea enthusiastically.

The problem was that reality didn't always cooperate.

When researchers isolated antioxidant compounds and tested them in supplement form, the results often failed to match the benefits seen in populations eating diets naturally rich in polyphenol-containing foods.

If polyphenols were simply acting as little chemical shields against oxidation, those outcomes should have been much more consistent.

Instead, scientists began to suspect that something more sophisticated was happening.

Polyphenols appear to interact with cellular signalling pathways, inflammation, immune responses and metabolism in ways that extend well beyond traditional antioxidant activity. Rather than simply protecting the body from damage, they seem to participate in an ongoing conversation between our diet, our cells and our environment.

And that conversation becomes even more interesting when we start looking at the gut microbiome.

## The Part Of The Story Most People Never Hear

For many years, nutrition science focused on absorption.

That seems perfectly reasonable. We eat food, digest it, absorb the nutrients and use them to build and maintain the body. Nutritional value was often judged by how much of something entered the bloodstream.

Polyphenols presented researchers with a puzzle.

Many of these compounds aren't absorbed particularly well in the small intestine. By conventional thinking, that should have reduced their importance.

Instead, it turns out their journey may only just be beginning.

Further down the digestive tract lies the colon, home to trillions of microorganisms collectively known as the gut microbiome. Rather than disappearing unused, many polyphenols arrive there largely intact, where they interact with an incredibly diverse ecosystem of bacteria.

Some microbes break polyphenols down into smaller compounds that can then be absorbed and utilised by the body. At the same time, the polyphenols themselves help determine which microbial species thrive and which struggle. The relationship goes both ways. The food influences the microbes, and the microbes influence what happens to the food.

This represents quite a shift in how we think about nutrition. Perhaps the question isn't simply what a food does for us. Perhaps it's also what that food does for the trillions of organisms living alongside us.

## Sometimes The Benefit Isn't What Gets Absorbed

Turmeric offers a fascinating example of this idea. Most discussions around turmeric quickly turn into discussions about curcumin, the bright yellow polyphenol that gives the spice its characteristic colour. The conversation usually centres around absorption. How much gets into the bloodstream? How can absorption be increased? Should it be taken with black pepper?

These are interesting questions, but they may not tell the whole story.

A significant proportion of curcumin isn't absorbed in the small intestine at all. Instead, it continues into the colon where it encounters the trillions of microbes that make up the gut microbiome. There, gut bacteria begin transforming curcumin into a range of smaller compounds that may themselves contribute to its biological effects. Researchers increasingly believe that part of turmeric's value may come from this interaction with the microbiome rather than absorption alone.

In other words, we've become conditioned to think that if a compound isn't absorbed, it's somehow wasted. But many fibres and polyphenols seem to exert some of their most interesting effects precisely because they reach the colon. Rather than feeding us directly, they feed the ecosystem living within us.

## What Coffee Taught Us About The Microbiome

Coffee has had a strange journey through the world of nutrition.

Depending on the decade, it has been labelled harmful, beneficial or somewhere in between. One year the focus is caffeine, another year it's stress hormones, and another it's athletic performance.

Yet when researchers began examining the microbiome in greater detail, coffee revealed something rather unexpected.

A study published in Nature Microbiology identified one of the strongest dietary associations with a specific gut bacterium seen to date. Across multiple independent populations, people who regularly consumed coffee consistently showed a greater abundance of _Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus_ than those who did not. The finding was observed repeatedly across different cohorts, making it one of the most compelling examples of a food influencing the composition of the microbiome.

The study wasn't important because it proved coffee was a superfood.

It was important because it highlighted something much bigger.

Food isn't simply delivering calories, protein, vitamins and minerals. It's interacting with a living ecosystem. Every meal creates opportunities for certain microbial species to flourish while others become less abundant.

Coffee simply provided one of the clearest examples.

## Polyphenols And Beneficial Gut Bacteria

Much of the current excitement around polyphenols comes from their ability to shape the microbial environment within the gut.

Researchers are increasingly interested in how polyphenol-rich foods influence microbial diversity, gut barrier integrity and the production of beneficial compounds. Rather than acting through a single pathway, polyphenols appear to participate in a complex network of interactions involving both the host and the microbiome.

One bacterium attracting particular attention is **Akkermansia muciniphila**.

Although research is still evolving, higher levels of Akkermansia have been associated with favourable metabolic health and a healthier gut environment in numerous studies. Interestingly, there doesn't appear to be a single food responsible for increasing it. Instead, Akkermansia seems to thrive in the context of diverse, plant-rich diets containing a wide variety of fibres and polyphenol-rich foods. That idea comes up repeatedly in microbiome research. The gut rarely responds to a single miracle ingredient. It responds to variety.

## The Richest Sources Of Polyphenols

The encouraging news is that polyphenols aren't hidden away in obscure superfoods costing a fortune online.

Many of the richest sources are foods that have been part of traditional diets for centuries.

**Berries** provide colourful compounds known as anthocyanins. **Coffee** is one of the largest contributors of polyphenols in many Western diets. **Extra virgin olive oil** contains unique polyphenols often linked to the benefits of Mediterranean-style eating. **Cocoa, tea, herbs, spices, legumes and deeply coloured vegetables** all contribute their own collection of compounds.

The foods richest in polyphenols tend to look remarkably similar to the foods your grandparents would recognise. Not powders. Just plants.

## How To Eat More Polyphenols Without Thinking About It

One of the best things about polyphenols is that increasing your intake doesn't require complicated meal plans or expensive supplements.

Most people could substantially increase their intake simply by making meals a little more colourful and a lot more flavourful.

The biggest missed opportunity often isn't fruit or vegetables. It's the spice cupboard.

Herbs and spices are among the most concentrated sources of polyphenols in the human diet. They contribute very few calories, yet deliver an impressive diversity of plant compounds. Small habits can make a surprisingly big difference.

- Add cinnamon to your morning coffee.

- Stir cocoa powder into Greek yoghurt or kefir.

- Use sumac on salads, eggs, roasted vegetables, hummus or grilled meat.

- Add paprika to tomato-based sauces, soups and stews.

- Cook with turmeric, cumin and coriander more often.

- Be generous with rosemary, oregano, thyme and fresh herbs.

- Scatter fresh parsley over dishes rather than using it as decoration.

- Swap bland meals for colourful, aromatic ones.

Without really trying, you've increased both the flavour and the diversity of compounds reaching your gut.

A polyphenol-rich day doesn't need to look unusual. It could be coffee and yoghurt with berries, cinnamon and cocoa powder for breakfast. A large salad drizzled with extra virgin olive oil and topped with herbs and sumac for lunch. Lentils, beans or colourful vegetables cooked with turmeric, cumin and paprika for dinner. Nothing exotic. Nothing expensive.

Yet across the course of a day you've exposed your body and your microbiome to hundreds of different plant compounds that would never appear on a nutrition label.

## Why "Eat The Rainbow" Is Better Advice Than It Sounds

Health messages like "eat the rainbow" can sound simplistic.

The phrase has been repeated so often that it's easy to dismiss it as another nutritional cliché.

Yet the science behind it is surprisingly compelling.

The deep blue pigments in berries, the reds in cherries, the purples in red cabbage and the vibrant colours of herbs and vegetables often reflect entirely different groups of plant compounds. Different colours frequently mean different polyphenols, and different polyphenols interact with different microbial species. Viewed through that lens, colour becomes about more than appearance. Colour becomes diversity.

And diversity appears to be one of the defining characteristics of a healthy microbiome.

## The Supplement Trap

Whenever science identifies a potentially beneficial compound, it's usually only a matter of time before somebody extracts it, concentrates it and puts it into a capsule.

Polyphenols are no exception. The challenge is that nature rarely packages nutrients in isolation.

A blueberry doesn't arrive with a single active ingredient. It arrives with fibre, polyphenols, water, vitamins, minerals and countless other compounds that interact with one another in ways we're still trying to understand.

Perhaps this helps explain why nutritional science repeatedly arrives at the same conclusion.

Whole dietary patterns tend to outperform isolated nutrients. The further we zoom in, the more complicated nutrition becomes. The further we zoom out, the clearer the message seems to be. Eat more real food.

The more we learn about nutrition, the less it seems to be about finding the perfect nutrient and the more it appears to be about creating the right environment.

An environment rich in diversity.

Diverse plants. Diverse fibres. Diverse polyphenols. Diverse microbes.

That's not a flashy message. It won't sell many supplements.

But it might explain why the foundations of good health still look remarkably similar to what they did before nutrition became obsessed with labels, macros and marketing claims.

Sometimes progress isn't discovering something new.

It's finally understanding why the old wisdom worked in the first place.

# Probiotics

The beneficial bacteria that support our health are known as probiotics. These microscopic allies play a vital role in our well-being by:

- Producing essential vitamins and anti-inflammatory compounds
- Protecting our gut from harmful infections
- Supporting our mood and mental health
- Powering up our immune system—70% of which resides in the gut
Unfortunately, modern lifestyles have disrupted the delicate balance of our gut microbiome. Poor diets, overuse of antibiotics, pesticides, and an obsession with sterility have reduced our probiotic populations and allowed harmful microbes to thrive. This imbalance has been linked to a wide range of health issues—from obesity and inflammation to dementia and mental health disorders.

### Why Probiotics Matter

I’ve touched on probiotics throughout this website and in various blog posts, and I hope by now it’s clear: probiotics are essential for your health.

These beneficial microbes primarily live in our digestive tract, where they feed on components of the food we eat and transform them into powerful compounds our bodies can’t produce on their own—like vitamins and anti-inflammatories.

Some strains are so effective, they act like natural antibiotics, producing substances that kill harmful bacteria. Others strengthen the gut lining or even produce compounds similar to oxytocin, helping us feel calm and relaxed.

We live in a symbiotic relationship with these tiny organisms. Together, we’ve evolved over millennia. The microbiome—the vast community of microbes living inside us—is crucial to nearly every aspect of our health.

### The Modern Threat to Our Microbiome

Today’s world is tough on our microbiome. Major disruptors include:

- Highly processed diets
- Overuse of antibiotics
- Pesticides in our food
- Excessive cleanliness and fear of bacteria
With refrigeration, we’ve lost touch with traditional food preservation methods like fermentation. We throw away anything that shows signs of bacterial or fungal growth, forgetting that many of our favorite foods—yogurt, cheese, wine, beer, even chocolate—are the result of fermentation.

Ironically, in trying to avoid harmful bacteria, we’ve created the perfect environment for them to flourish inside us.

### Rebuilding the Balance

While we can’t undo the past, we can take steps to restore and support our gut health. One of the most powerful ways to do this is by reintroducing cultured and fermented foods into our diets.

By re-learning the art of fermentation, we can begin to repopulate our gut with the probiotics we’ve lost—and reclaim our health in the process.

# Protein

#### Feed Your Muscles, Not The Marketing

For most of human history, nobody knew how many grams of protein they were eating.

There were no protein shakes. No protein puddings. No protein-enriched breakfast cereals.

Yet people still managed to build muscle, raise families, work physically demanding jobs and grow old.

Today, however, protein has become one of the most aggressively marketed nutrients on the planet. Walk through any supermarket and it's difficult to avoid the message. Protein has been added to foods that never needed it, often wrapped in the promise of better health, greater satiety or improved body composition.

The irony is that many of the people buying these products are already consuming adequate protein. Meanwhile, the people who genuinely stand to benefit from higher protein intake, particularly older adults losing muscle mass as they age, are often the least aware of its importance.

Protein matters.

But not always for the reasons social media would have you believe.

## What Is Protein, Actually?

Protein is one of the three major macronutrients, alongside carbohydrate and fat.

Unlike carbohydrates and fats, protein isn't primarily an energy source. Its primary role is structural and functional.

Every cell in your body contains protein. Your muscles, skin, immune system, enzymes, hormones and connective tissues all depend upon it.

At its simplest level, protein is built from smaller molecules called amino acids.

These amino acids are often described as the body's building blocks, and for good reason. Everything from repairing a muscle after exercise to producing neurotransmitters in the brain depends upon them.

But not all amino acids are equal.

## Essential, Non-Essential And Everything In Between

There are twenty amino acids commonly used by the human body.

Eleven can be produced internally.

Nine cannot.

These nine are known as **essential amino acids** because we must obtain them from food.

One amino acid deserves particular attention.

**Leucine.**

Leucine acts almost like a biological switch for muscle protein synthesis. When sufficient leucine is present, it helps signal the body to repair and build muscle tissue. This is one reason why not all protein sources have the same effect on muscle growth and maintenance.

This is also why protein quality matters.

## Not All Protein Is Created Equal

The conversation around protein often focuses on quantity.

How many grams?

How many shakes?

How many scoops?

Yet quality may be just as important.

Animal proteins such as eggs, dairy, fish and meat generally contain all essential amino acids in proportions that efficiently support muscle protein synthesis. Plant proteins can absolutely contribute to optimal health, but individual sources may contain lower amounts of specific amino acids, requiring greater variety to achieve the same amino acid profile.

This doesn't mean meat is mandatory.

It simply means diversity matters.

Much like the fibre and polyphenol story, the answer is rarely found in a single food.

## The Protein Question Most People Are Asking Wrong

When people ask how much protein they need, they're usually expecting a single number.

The reality is more nuanced.

The official Recommended Dietary Allowance is approximately 0.8g per kilogram of body weight. However, this amount was designed primarily to prevent deficiency, not necessarily to optimise muscle mass, physical performance or healthy ageing.

This distinction is important.

There's a substantial difference between the amount of protein required to avoid deficiency and the amount that may support long-term health and resilience.

Researchers and practitioners such as Andy Galpin, Andrew Huberman and Stacy Sims frequently discuss intakes closer to 1.6-2.2g/kg/day, particularly for active individuals looking to support muscle retention, recovery and performance.

That doesn't mean everybody needs bodybuilder levels of protein.

It does mean that the minimum requirement and the optimal requirement may not be the same thing.

## The Real Concern Isn't Protein Deficiency

When most people think about protein, they think about building muscle.

The more important conversation may be keeping it.

From around our fourth decade of life, we gradually begin losing skeletal muscle mass. This process accelerates with age and can eventually lead to sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle mass, strength and physical function.

Sarcopenia isn't just about looking less muscular.

## Why Protein Becomes More Important As We Age

One fascinating concept that keeps appearing in the research is something known as **anabolic resistance**.

As we get older, our muscles become less responsive to both protein intake and resistance exercise. In practical terms, older adults often require a greater stimulus to achieve the same muscle-building response as younger adults.

This is one reason Stacy Sims places so much emphasis on protein intake during peri-menopause and post-menopause. As oestrogen declines, maintaining muscle becomes more difficult, making adequate protein and resistance training increasingly important.

A theme you'll hear repeated across the work of Sims, Galpin and Huberman is remarkably consistent:

**Build muscle while you can. Preserve it for as long as possible.**

# Resistant Starch

#### Why a Cold Potato Isn't the Same as a Hot Potato

Most people think of starch as a straightforward source of carbohydrate. But starch is surprisingly dynamic. How it's prepared can dramatically alter the way your body digests it.

Starch is made up of two glucose polymers: amylose and amylopectin. When foods such as potatoes, rice, oats and pasta are cooked in water, the starch granules absorb water, swell and lose their tightly packed crystalline structure. This process, known as gelatinisation, makes starch far more accessible to digestive enzymes.

As the food cools, some of these starch molecules begin to realign. The relatively straight amylose chains form tightly packed crystalline structures held together by hydrogen bonds. This process is called retrogradation. Once these crystals form, they become much more difficult for digestive enzymes to break apart. The result is resistant starch type 3 (RS3), a form of starch that largely escapes digestion in the small intestine.

In simple terms:

#### Cook → starch becomes highly digestible

#### Cool → starch recrystallises

#### Recrystallised starch → resistant starch

This explains why cooled potatoes, rice and pasta often produce a lower blood glucose response than their freshly cooked equivalents.

## The Five Types of Resistant Starch

Resistant starch isn't a single substance. It's a group of starches that resist digestion for different reasons.

### RS1: Physically Protected Starch

RS1 is starch that digestive enzymes simply cannot easily access because it is trapped within the physical structure of the food.

Examples include:

- Intact whole grains
- Beans and lentils
- Seeds
- Coarsely milled cereals
Grinding, blending and extensive processing can reduce RS1 because the starch becomes more accessible.

### RS2: Native Resistant Starch

RS2 occurs naturally when starch granules have a tightly packed crystalline structure that human digestive enzymes struggle to break down.

Examples include:

- Green bananas
- Raw potatoes
- Some legumes
- High-amylose maize
As bananas ripen, much of this resistant starch is converted into sugar. This is why a green banana behaves very differently metabolically from a ripe one.

### RS3: Retrograded Resistant Starch

This is the form most people encounter when discussing resistant starch.

When foods such as potatoes, rice or pasta are cooked, the starch becomes digestible. As the food cools, some of the starch recrystallises into a new structure that resists digestion. This process is known as retrogradation.

Examples include:

- Cooled potatoes
- Potato salad
- Cooled rice
- Sushi rice
- Cooled pasta
- Overnight oats
This is arguably the easiest form of resistant starch to increase in an everyday diet.

### RS4: Chemically Modified Resistant Starch

RS4 is created through industrial food processing. Manufacturers chemically modify starch molecules to make them less digestible.

These starches are commonly used in:

- Processed foods
- Fibre-enriched products
- Some protein bars
- Low-carbohydrate manufactured foods
Although technically a resistant starch, it differs substantially from the naturally occurring forms found in whole foods.

### RS5: Amylose-Lipid Complexes

RS5 forms when amylose binds to fats and creates a structure that digestive enzymes struggle to access.

This can occur when starches are cooked in the presence of fats such as:

- Butter
- Olive oil
- Coconut oil
This is a relatively newer classification but is becoming an area of increasing research interest because it appears to combine some of the benefits of resistant starch formation with everyday cooking practices.

## Which Types Should We Be Eating?

From a health perspective, the most practical sources are generally:

- RS1 from legumes and minimally processed whole grains
- RS2 from foods such as green bananas
- RS3 from cooled potatoes, rice and pasta
These forms occur naturally and have been consumed throughout human history. They also appear to be readily fermented by gut microbes, producing beneficial compounds such as butyrate.

While resistant starch is often discussed as a single nutrient, the reality is that a green banana, a lentil and a cold potato all contain resistant starch for completely different structural reasons. Understanding those differences helps explain why food preparation can be just as important as food choice itself.

### Which Foods Form The Most?

Foods rich in amylose generally produce more resistant starch after cooling because amylose retrogrades more readily than amylopectin.

Good examples include:

- Potatoes
- Rice, particularly higher-amylose varieties such as basmati
- Pasta
- Beans and lentils
- Oats
Interestingly, repeated heating and cooling cycles may increase resistant starch formation even further, as each cycle provides another opportunity for starch molecules to reorganise into stable crystalline structures.

### What Happens When You Reheat It?

Contrary to popular belief, resistant starch isn't completely destroyed by reheating.

Once RS3 has formed, a significant proportion remains intact during gentle reheating. This means reheated leftover potatoes, rice or pasta typically still contain more resistant starch than when they were first cooked and eaten hot.

### So Would Eating A Raw Potato Have The Same Effect?

From a resistant starch perspective, surprisingly, yes.

Raw potatoes contain large amounts of RS2 resistant starch. However, this doesn't make eating raw potatoes a good strategy.

Raw potatoes are:

- Difficult to digest
- Less palatable
- More likely to cause gastrointestinal discomfort
- Potentially contain higher levels of naturally occurring compounds such as glycoalkaloids
Cooking destroys most RS2 but greatly improves digestibility and nutrient availability. Cooling subsequently creates RS3. For most people, cooled cooked potatoes are the more practical option.

Interestingly, green bananas are one of the richest natural sources of resistant starch available. As they ripen, much of that resistant starch is converted into sugar.

### What About Uncooked Pasta?

Not really.

Uncooked pasta is certainly harder to digest because the starch granules remain largely ungelatinised, but it doesn't provide the same benefits as retrograded resistant starch formed after cooking and cooling.

The starch is physically inaccessible rather than intentionally transformed into a beneficial fermentable substrate. Eating dry pasta is also unlikely to be particularly enjoyable and may cause digestive discomfort.

### Why Does Any Of This Matter?

Resistant starch behaves much more like a fermentable fibre than a conventional carbohydrate.

Instead of being digested and absorbed as glucose in the small intestine, it reaches the colon where it is fermented by gut microbes. This process produces short-chain fatty acids, particularly butyrate, which serves as a primary fuel source for colon cells and is associated with improved gut and metabolic health.

Perhaps the most fascinating takeaway is that a potato doesn't need to become a different food to behave differently in the body. Simply allowing it to cool changes the physical arrangement of the starch molecules, altering how your digestive system and gut microbiome interact with it.

# Sleep Strategies

## Optimizing Sleep

#### The Power of Sunlight, Herbs, and Smart Lifestyle Choices

In today’s fast-paced world, quality sleep is more essential than ever for maintaining overall health and well-being. Modern lifestyles often disrupt natural sleep patterns, but adopting simple, science-backed habits can help restore balance. By harnessing morning and evening sun exposure, using sleep-promoting herbs and nutrients, and making mindful lifestyle adjustments, you can create a routine that supports deep, restorative sleep.

#### Morning Sun Exposure: Resetting Your Circadian Rhythm

Morning sunlight is one of the most effective tools for synchronizing your body’s internal clock. Stepping outside shortly after waking exposes your eyes and skin to low-angle sunlight, which plays a crucial role in regulating the circadian rhythm. This early light suppresses melatonin—the hormone responsible for sleep—helping you feel alert during the day. Additionally, it interacts with the adenosine system, which builds up over the day and signals sleepiness around 16 hours later. By setting this physiological timer early in the morning, your body is naturally primed for high-quality sleep later in the evening.

#### The Benefits of Sunset Exposure

Just as morning sunlight kickstarts your daily rhythm, viewing low-angle sunlight at sunset helps reinforce this cycle. The shifting wavelengths of light signal to your brain that the day is winding down, supporting the gradual onset of melatonin production. Spending time outside during sunset also fosters relaxation and mental clarity, promoting a smoother transition into nighttime rest.

#### Limiting Evening Light Exposure

While morning and sunset light are beneficial, bright artificial lighting in the evening can disrupt the circadian rhythm. Blue light from screens, LED bulbs, and even overhead lighting suppresses melatonin production, delaying sleep onset and reducing sleep quality. Dimming lights and minimizing screen time in the hour before bed allows melatonin to rise naturally, helping you fall asleep more easily and experience deeper sleep cycles.

#### Herbal Remedies: Nature’s Sleep Aids

Herbal solutions have been used for centuries to enhance relaxation and sleep.

• **Chamomile**:Rich in apigenin, a flavonoid that binds to brain receptors promoting calmness and sleep readiness. Drinking chamomile tea before bed can help ease anxiety and improve sleep quality.

• **Valerian Root**: Known for its sedative effects, valerian relaxes the nervous system and reduces the time needed to fall asleep.

• **Passionflower**: Supports GABA activity in the brain, helping quiet mental chatter and encourage a restful state.

#### The Role of Magnesium and L-Theanine

Both magnesium and L-theanine contribute to a relaxed nervous system, making them excellent tools for optimizing sleep.

• **Magnesium:** This essential mineral regulates neurotransmitters involved in relaxation and sleep. It plays a role in calming muscle tension, reducing restlessness, and supporting deep, uninterrupted sleep. A deficiency in magnesium can lead to increased stress and trouble sleeping.

• **L-Theanine**: Found naturally in tea leaves, L-theanine promotes relaxation without causing drowsiness. It increases alpha brain waves, fostering a state of calm while modulating neurotransmitter levels like GABA, serotonin, and dopamine. This makes it a valuable addition to any evening routine.

#### The Importance of Routine and Avoiding Sleep Disruptors

Consistency in your sleep schedule helps regulate your circadian rhythm and improve overall sleep quality. Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day strengthens the body’s internal clock, allowing for more predictable and restorative sleep cycles.

Additionally, limiting **caffeine** and **alcohol** intake before bedtime plays a vital role in sleep health. Caffeine blocks adenosine, the sleep-promoting neurotransmitter, keeping the brain stimulated when it should be winding down. Alcohol, though often mistaken as a sedative, disrupts deep sleep stages and impairs overall rest. Ideally, caffeine should be avoided in the late afternoon, and alcohol consumption should be minimized a few hours before bed to ensure uninterrupted, high-quality sleep.

#### Bringing It All Together: A Science-Backed Sleep Strategy

By incorporating these practices into your daily routine, you can significantly improve sleep quality:

• Morning sunlight exposure kickstarts your circadian rhythm and regulates melatonin production.

• Sunset exposure reinforces your sleep-wake cycle and aids relaxation.

• Dimming evening light supports natural melatonin release.

• Herbal remedies like chamomile help ease stress and enhance sleep.

• Magnesium and L-theanine promote physical and mental relaxation.

• A consistent bedtime routine strengthens your body’s natural rhythms.

• Avoiding caffeine and alcohol near bedtime prevents sleep disturbances.

By aligning your daily habits with these natural principles, you can create a sustainable and effective strategy for better sleep. Whether you’re seeking deeper rest, improved mood, or better cognitive function, these adjustments can help you thrive in a fast-paced world while ensuring you wake up refreshed and ready to take on the day.

# Milk Kefir

**Kefir is arguably the most powerful probiotic food you can make at home, and one of the simplest.**

Milk kefir is a traditional fermented food made by adding kefir grains, a living community of beneficial bacteria and yeasts, to milk. Over 24 hours, these microorganisms consume much of the milk's lactose and transform it into a tangy, yoghurt-like drink packed with probiotics, organic acids, bioactive peptides and other health-promoting compounds.

Unlike most commercial yoghurts, homemade kefir contains an incredibly diverse microbial community. Depending on the grains and fermentation conditions, homemade kefir can contain around **30 to 60+ different strains of beneficial bacteria and yeasts**, compared with the handful of strains typically found in many shop-bought products. More diversity means a broader range of microbial metabolites and a potentially greater impact on the gut microbiome.

Regular consumption of kefir has been associated with improved gut microbial diversity, digestive health, immune function and metabolic health. The fermentation process also breaks down much of the lactose naturally present in milk, making kefir easier to tolerate for many people who struggle with regular dairy.

Making kefir couldn't be easier. Simply add kefir grains to milk and leave it at room temperature for around 24 hours. During this time, the microbes multiply and convert the lactose into a wide range of beneficial compounds. The finished kefir thickens into a creamy, slightly tangy drink rich in probiotics and nutrients. The grains are then strained out and added to fresh milk to start the process again.

One glass of kefir provides a wealth of nutrients, including calcium, phosphorus, vitamin B12, riboflavin (B2), magnesium and other bioactive compounds. Beyond its nutritional profile, kefir has also demonstrated antimicrobial properties and may help support a healthier balance of microorganisms within the gut.

Perhaps most importantly, kefir is more than just a source of probiotics. It is a living fermented food that introduces beneficial microbes and their fermentation products into the diet, helping to restore some of the microbial exposures that have become increasingly scarce in our modern, ultra-sanitised food environment.

# Water Kefir

Water Kefir is my newest fermentation venture. I love kombucha, but I wanted an alternative to drink in the evening that did not contain caffeine.

Water Kefir can contain around 26 probiotic strains (or more depending on your grains) that can enhance your gut health and overall wellbeing. What’s not to love.

Water kefir is my newest fermentation venture. I love kombucha, but I wanted an alternative that I could enjoy in the evening without the caffeine. What I discovered was a wonderfully refreshing, lightly sparkling drink that is incredibly easy to make and packed with beneficial microbes.

Made using water kefir grains, a living culture of bacteria and yeasts, water kefir ferments a simple mixture of water and sugar into a subtly sweet, gently tangy drink rich in probiotics and fermentation-derived compounds. For those following a vegan, dairy-free or plant-based lifestyle, it offers many of the microbiome-supporting benefits of milk kefir without the dairy.

What makes water kefir particularly interesting is that it delivers more than just live microorganisms. During fermentation, the bacteria and yeasts consume much of the sugar and produce a wide range of organic acids, enzymes and other bioactive compounds. These compounds, often referred to as postbiotics, are increasingly recognised for their role in supporting gut health and helping to create a more favourable environment for a diverse and resilient microbiome.

A healthy gut microbiome influences far more than digestion alone. Research continues to highlight links between microbial diversity and immune function, metabolic health and overall wellbeing. While no single food is a magic bullet, regularly including fermented foods such as water kefir can be a simple way to increase your exposure to beneficial microorganisms and the compounds they produce.

Compared with kombucha and milk kefir, water kefir has a much lighter and more delicate flavour. It is less acidic than kombucha and less creamy than milk kefir, making it an excellent introduction to home fermentation. The finished drink can be enjoyed as it is, or bottled with fruit, herbs or spices for a second fermentation that creates even more flavour and natural fizz. Some of my favourite additions are ginger, lemon, berries, citrus fruits and fresh herbs.

Water kefir grains are added to sugar water and left to ferment for 24-72 hours. The grains are then strained off and reused for the next batch, while the fermented liquid is ready to enjoy or flavour further.

Refreshing, inexpensive, endlessly customisable and packed with microbial goodness, water kefir is one of the easiest ways to bring more fermented foods into your diet and support your microbiome one glass at a time.

# Probiotic Yogurts

#### Taking Fermented Foods to the Next Level

Traditional yogurt is one of the world's oldest fermented foods, transforming milk into a nutrient-rich food that's often easier to digest than milk itself. During fermentation, beneficial bacteria consume lactose, produce lactic acid and create compounds that contribute to yogurt's flavour, texture and health benefits. Alongside providing protein, calcium and other nutrients, yogurt can be a valuable source of live microbes that help support a healthy gut microbiome.

Making your own yogurt is surprisingly simple. All you need is milk, a starter culture and gentle warmth. By holding milk at around 40-45°C for several hours, the bacteria multiply and ferment the milk into thick, tangy yogurt. Homemade yogurt is often fresher, less processed and more cost-effective than many shop-bought varieties, while allowing you to control the ingredients and avoid added sugars.

Inspired by Dr. William Davis's _Super Gut_ and the work of Cultured Food Life, these probiotic "yogurts" take the concept a step further. Rather than relying solely on traditional yogurt cultures, they use specific probiotic strains such as **L. reuteri**, **L. casei** and **L. gasseri**, fermented for up to 36 hours to achieve exceptionally high concentrations of beneficial bacteria.

Each strain has attracted interest for different reasons. **L. reuteri** has been studied for its potential role in supporting gut health, immune function and even the gut-brain axis. **L. casei** is one of the most researched probiotic species and is often associated with digestive and immune health. **L. gasseri** has gained attention for its possible effects on metabolic health, appetite regulation and body composition. While research is ongoing and no single strain is a magic bullet, it's fascinating to see how different microbes can influence health in different ways.

What makes these yogurts particularly interesting is that you're not only consuming live microbes, but also the beneficial compounds they produce during fermentation. Alongside a fibre-rich, microbiome-friendly diet, they've become a regular part of my wellness routine.

The key is sourcing the correct probiotic strain and using a yogurt maker capable of maintaining a precise temperature for extended periods. Once you've made a successful batch, a small amount can be used to culture future batches, making this a surprisingly affordable way to enjoy probiotic-rich fermented foods at home.

For the creamiest results, I use organic full-fat UHT milk, organic double cream and potato starch as a fermentation substrate. The result is a thick, tangy, delicious fermented food that delivers far more than taste alone. For anyone interested in supporting their microbiome, it's a fascinating experiment and a natural progression from making traditional homemade yogurt.

Kombucha starts with sweetened tea and a SCOBY (Symbiotic Culture of Bacteria and Yeast). Over the next 7-14 days, the microorganisms ferment the tea, transforming it into a lightly sparkling, sweet-and-sour drink rich in organic acids, polyphenols and other beneficial compounds.

During fermentation, the microbes produce a wide range of postbiotics: beneficial compounds created by the fermentation process itself. These include organic acids, polyphenol metabolites and other bioactive compounds that can help support gut health, immune function and the integrity of the gut lining.

Increasingly, researchers believe that many of the benefits of fermented foods come not only from the live organisms themselves, but also from these fermentation-derived compounds.

Tea is naturally rich in polyphenols, and fermentation further modifies these compounds, creating new metabolites that may enhance their biological activity. This is one reason kombucha is often recognised for its antioxidant potential and its role in supporting metabolic and digestive health.

Lavender

## Steps for continuous brewing

#### NOURISH THE STARTER

Once you have your starter SCOBY (and/or pellicle) you want to get it super strong before diving into a full brew.

DO NOT MISS THIS STEP!!!

The most common reason for contamination is the kombucha not being strong and acidic enough to ward off any yeast or contaminants from the air/surfaces.

#### WHAT YOU'LL NEED

- A brewing vessel - to start, you'll only need a 1L jar, but make sure you have around a 5L vessel with a tap.
This tap will need to be food grade stainless steel. If you vessel does not come with it, they are not terribly expensive on [Amazon](https://amzn.eu/d/cAxMyTI).

- Starter SCOBY culture
- Organic cane sugar
- Good quality plain tea - bags or loose tea - normal black or green tea, no herbal, rooibos, flavourings, decaf etc. This may affect the fermentation.
- A large glass jug
- Wooden spoon
- A clean muslin cloth or breathable covering for the top of the vessel & elastic band
- ~1L Clip top or Screw top glass bottle/s for the finished product <3

#### GETTING YOUR STARTER READY ~12 DAYS

Let's assume you have received about 200ml of starter and a small pellicle. This can also apply for the store bought kombucha.

#### Stage 1

1. Make sure you sterilise everything. Ideally a hot dishwasher with just the kombucha utensils and small 1l vessel.
2. Mix around 2 teabags in around 300ml hot water in the glass jug
3. Add ~50g sugar and dissolve
4. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags
5. Add ~300ml of cool water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
6. Add the SCOBY to your small vessel and cover with your cloth.
7. Leave this for about 3-4 days.
#### Stage 2

Basically repeating it all

1. Make sure you sterilise everything. This time, the large vessel you will be using for your continuous brew.
2. Mix around 2 teabags in around 300ml hot water in the glass jug
3. Add ~50g sugar and dissolve
4. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags
5. Add ~300ml of cool water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
6. Add the previous brewing Kombucha including the scoby to your large vessel
7. Add the new sugar tea
8. Cover with your cloth.
9. Leave this for about 3-4 days. You should get a super thin, white-ish pellicle forming on the top of this brew now.
Check for no weird white or green circles on the top!

#### Stage 3

Just adding more sweet tea.

1. Mix around 2 teabags in around 300ml hot water in the glass jug
2. Add ~50g sugar and dissolve
3. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags
4. Add ~300ml of cool water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
5. Add to the continuous brewing Kombucha
6. Leave for another 3-4 days. You should get a thicker, pellicle forming on the top of this brew now.
Now you have a lovely strong starter!

You should have about 2L of Kombucha now.

Depending on the size of your vessel, this will determine how much you add.

#### LETS MAKE KOMBUCHA

For every 1L I usually add around 90g of Cane Sugar and 4Tea Bags

So if you want to get your brew to 4 liters, let's do the following

1. Use your existing ~2L of Kombucha as your super strong starter. Keep it in the brewing vessel.
2. Mix around 8 teabags in around 500ml hot water in the glass jug
3. Add ~180g sugar and dissolve
4. Let the tea brew for a few minutes and remove the tea bags
5. Add ~500ml of cool water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
6. Add to the continuous brewing Kombucha
7. Add another 1l of water (ideally filtered water, but I do use tap water)
8. Leave for around 7-10 days.
9. You should get a lovely thick, pellicle forming on the top of this brew now.
10. After around 7 days, draw some fluid off into a glass to taste. It should be slightly sweet but tangy.

#### FIRST OR SECOND BREW

1. Once the kombucha is to your taste, you can draw off the 1L into your bottle and drink away.
2. If you wanted to flavour it, add the flavour to the 1l close top bottle, then draw off the 1L of Kombucha into the bottle.
3. Leave this for a few days to ferment further, then put in the fridge to enjoy!
4. I wouldn't recommend more than 2L at a time
5. After you have drawn off the fluid, replace the fluid you have drawn off, with the methods above.
6. For every 1L I usually add around 90g of Cane Sugar and 4Tea Bags
7. Make sure the water you add has been cooled down with cold water.
If your Kombucha gets very acidic, draw off most of it, leaving around 10%, then fill that back up - its probably just too strong/active.

#### FLAVOURS

This is all personal preference. I usually make a batch and freeze them into ice-cubes to add to my brews.

If you want your second ferment to be fizzy, make sure you add something sweet - either fruits or a spoon of sugar.

My favorites are:

- Lime juice and mint leaves. Blended with a little bit of sugar.
- Blended strawberries and lime
- Real ginger, blended with some lemon and a little bit of sugar.

# The Art of Doing Nothing

#### Take back your attention…

We are living in a world of constant stimulation. Your phone is always there, notifications, emails, WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, news, podcasts, music and television. There is always something to look at, something to listen to, something new to consume and something demanding our attention.

How often do you find yourself reaching for your phone without even really knowing why? Waiting in a queue, waiting for the kettle to boil, sitting on the toilet, having your morning coffee or simply having a few minutes to yourself. It has become almost automatic to fill those little gaps in our day with something.

But perhaps those gaps are actually quite important.

Our attention is valuable, and there are huge companies whose business models depend on keeping us looking at a screen for as long as possible. The longer we stay scrolling, watching and interacting, the more opportunities there are to show us advertising, recommend content and learn about our behaviour.

This doesn't necessarily require some sinister conspiracy. It is simply how many of these businesses make money. Our attention has value, and these companies have become extremely good at capturing it.

## Why we can't sit still

Think about how social media works. There is rarely a natural end point. You don't reach the bottom of Instagram or TikTok and get told that you have finished. There is always another video, another post, another headline, another notification and something else being recommended specifically for you.

You are constantly being offered something new.

And this is where I think we need to start thinking about dopamine.

## You don't need a dopamine detox

But let's get one thing straight. You don't need a “dopamine detox”. Dopamine isn't a toxin that you use up by looking at Instagram and then replenish by sitting in a dark room for 24 hours. It is a normal and important neurotransmitter involved in motivation, learning, movement, reward and many other functions.

The more interesting question is what we are repeatedly training our brains to do.

Our brains are remarkably adaptable and change in response to what we repeatedly practise. This is part of what we mean when we talk about neuroplasticity. If you spend your day constantly switching between different things, your brain becomes very practised at switching. A notification arrives, you look at a message, have a quick look at social media, return to work, see another headline, watch a short video and then try to return to what you were doing.

We are constantly interrupting ourselves, and then we wonder why sitting down and concentrating on one thing for an extended period can sometimes feel difficult.

What we practise matters.

If we repeatedly give our brains tiny bursts of novelty and stimulation, ordinary life can start to feel comparatively slow. A ten-minute wait can feel unbearable, a walk without a podcast can feel boring, eating dinner without watching something can feel strangely quiet and sitting with our own thoughts can become uncomfortable.

So we reach for the phone again, and the cycle continues.

## Being comfortable doing nothing

I think this is one of the things we have lost: being comfortable doing nothing.

I'm not talking about another productivity technique or telling you that you need to meditate for an hour every day. I mean simply sitting outside with a cup of tea, going for a walk without your headphones, driving without a podcast, standing in a queue without getting your phone out or looking out of a window and letting your mind wander.

Sometimes our best thoughts come when we aren't actively trying to think.

When we give ourselves some space, our minds can wander, make connections, process things and reflect on what is happening in our lives. We don't necessarily need to fill every quiet moment with information or entertainment. In fact, perhaps we need more of those quiet moments.

So try reducing the stimulation.

You don't have to throw your phone away. Technology is incredibly useful and, like most people, I use it every day. The point isn't to become anti-technology. The point is to make sure that **you are using your technology rather than your technology using you.**

## Start small

Start small.

Turn off unnecessary notifications. Your phone doesn't need to tell you every time someone posts something or that something new has happened somewhere in the world. Move social media apps away from your home screen so that accessing them requires a little more intention. If there is an app you don't actually need, consider deleting it.

Try having your morning coffee without your phone. Don't automatically reach for it when you wake up. Get outside instead. Give yourself some time before you allow the outside world to start demanding your attention.

Go for a walk without a podcast or music and just walk. See what you notice. Listen to what is happening around you. Allow your mind to wander.

Try eating a meal without watching television or scrolling. Have a conversation without putting your phone on the table. Create a period in the evening where your phone is simply somewhere else.

## Learn to be bored again

And perhaps most importantly, learn to tolerate being bored again.

Try sitting for ten minutes without doing anything. No phone, no television, no music and no podcast. Just sit and see what happens.

You might be surprised how quickly your brain starts asking for stimulation. That is okay. You don't have to respond to it. Let the thought come and go. See where your mind wanders.

Over time, you may find that you become more comfortable with periods of quiet and that you don't automatically need to fill every spare moment with something.

This isn't about becoming perfectly productive or removing all entertainment from your life. It is about creating some balance and remembering that your attention is yours.

## Your attention is yours

Your attention is one of the most valuable things you have. Where your attention goes, your time follows, and where your time goes, much of your life follows.

There will always be another notification, another video, another headline, another argument and another thing that apparently needs your immediate attention.

It doesn't.

You are allowed to be unavailable. You are allowed to be bored. You are allowed to sit with your thoughts. You are allowed to do nothing.

You don't need to detox from dopamine. Perhaps you simply need to give your brain a little less to process and a little more time to think.

# Flow Rope

#### Enhancing Coordination, Rhythm, and Meditative Movement

Flow rope training brings fluid, dynamic motion into my exercise routine. By incorporating rotational movements, I improve joint mobility, hand-eye coordination, and cardiovascular endurance. The rhythmic nature of flow rope exercises also fosters a meditative state, helping me tap into mindfulness while moving.

By moving the flow rope in an upward motion in the figure of 8, it encourages a more relaxed state. It takes a while to neuroadapt to the motion but once you do, its such an easy practice. It’s a beautiful way of untangling and decompressing your nervous system and embracing a more fluid motion and relaxed core.
